## 1. What successful replication does not settle

> "Without further understanding and analysis, even successful replication tells us little either for or against simple generalization or to support for the conclusion that the next will work in the same way." [@deaton2016rct, p. 27]

**Read that against intuition.** A result found once, then again, then again, feels progressively safer to carry elsewhere. The claim is that without an account of *why* it holds, the accumulation says little about whether it will hold in a new place.

## 2. What failed replication does not settle either

Immediately following, on the same page:

> "Nor do failures of replication make the original result useless." [@deaton2016rct, p. 27]

And the constructive form, overleaf:

> "We often learn much from coming to understand why replication failed and can use that knowledge, in looking for how the factors that caused the original result might operate differently in different settings." [@deaton2016rct, p. 28]

**A failed replication is a finding about the difference between two settings**, and the difference is usually more informative than either result.

This is the same disposition Chapter 5 took toward a failed check and Chapter 7 toward a verdict of *not identified*: the negative result is information about where to look, not the end of the exercise.

## 3. The illustration

The paper gives one, attributed:

> "Bertrand Russell's chicken (Russell (1912)) provides an excellent example of the limitations to straightforward extrapolation from repeated successful replication. The bird infers, on repeated evidence, that when the farmer comes in the morning, he feeds her. The inference serves her well until Christmas morning, when he wrings her neck and serves her for dinner." [@deaton2016rct, p. 28]

And the diagnosis, which is what makes it usable rather than merely amusing:

> "Though this chicken did not base her inference on an RCT, had we constructed one for her, we would have obtained the same result that she did. Her problem was not her methodology, but rather that she did not understand the social and economic structure that gave rise to the causal relations that she observed." [@deaton2016rct, p. 28]

**"Her problem was not her methodology."** That sentence is the chapter's, and it should be quoted.

Every quality control this book has taught would have passed. The record is long, the observations are accurate, the pattern is real, the replication is perfect, and there is no analytic defect anywhere. What is missing is knowledge of the structure that produced the pattern — which is not in the record and could not be got from more of it.

### Attribution discipline

**Russell (1912) was not obtained.** The illustration is used **as reported at** `deaton2016rct` p. 28, and this book makes no claim about Russell's text, his purpose in using the example, or its context. The same handling given to Brier (1950) in Chapter 6 and Matrixx in Chapter 8.

## 4. And the general claim

> "So, establishing causality does nothing in and of itself to guarantee generalizability." [@deaton2016rct, p. 28]

The paper goes further, naming what a merely local causal finding amounts to:

> effects "that may have only local applicability, what might be labeled 'anecdotal causality'" [@deaton2016rct, p. 28]

**This is a hard sentence for a book that has just spent Chapter 7 on identification.** A reader who worked hard to establish that an effect is identified may reasonably feel that they have arrived somewhere, and this says that arriving there does not, by itself, get them to their own setting.

The chapter should carry it plainly rather than softening it. Chapter 7's work is not wasted — an unidentified effect cannot be transported either — but identification is a prerequisite for transport rather than a substitute for it.

**Attribution note:** "anecdotal causality" is the authors' phrase and the surrounding sentence makes clear it is a critique aimed at practice in economics. It should be quoted as theirs, not adopted as the book's own coinage.

## 5. The source's status, restated

`../../sources/deaton2016rct.md` records that this is NBER Working Paper 22595, whose cover states that NBER working papers have not been peer-reviewed, and that the published *Social Science & Medicine* version could not be obtained.

**That status governs pp. 26–29 exactly as it governed pp. 7–11.** The manuscript describes the source as a working paper, attributes its strongest claims to its authors, and does not present them as consensus.

The claims used in this cluster are, on their face, less contested than the paper's headline position about randomization. But the book does not get to decide which parts of an unrefereed source are safe, so the same attribution applies throughout.
