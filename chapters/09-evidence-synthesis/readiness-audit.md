## 1. Readiness verdict

**Drafting-ready after adjudication**, and unusually well supplied because two of its three principal sources are already in the bibliography with reading left deliberately undone.

Three observations.

**The em dash in the central question is the chapter.** *What do many imperfect sources jointly support — here?* The first clause is synthesis. The word after the dash is transport. A chapter that treated them as two topics would miss that they are one question asked about one decision, and that the second half is what makes the first half hard.

**Two sources come due rather than needing to be found.**

`../08-estimation-uncertainty/research-plan.md` and Chapter 7's before it recorded that `deaton2016rct` was read only to printed p. 11 of 70, and that "the paper's second part — which concerns how to use trial results and belongs to Chapter 9's territory — is not characterised anywhere in this book." **Section 2 of that paper begins on printed p. 26 and is Chapter 9's material almost line for line.**

`../../sources/meng2018paradox.md` records insight (IV) from the abstract verbatim — that when combining sources, "those relatively tiny but higher quality ones should be given far more weights than suggested by their sizes" — and Chapter 4 recorded it without using it, because Chapter 4 was about one dataset.

**The core competence again names eight things**, as Chapter 8's did, but it also says **"at an appropriate conceptual level"**, which Chapter 8's did not. That phrase is governed text and it is a licence: this chapter is 28 pages because it is not supposed to teach synthesis machinery.

## 2. Unique-job hypothesis

> Teach readers that combining evidence is not averaging it, that transporting a result is not assuming it holds, and that both failures are decided by things no amount of the evidence can show.

The reader who finishes Chapter 9 should be able to take five reports bearing on one decision, say whether they are even about the same quantity, refuse a weighting rule that hands the answer to the largest and worst source, and say what would have to be true of the target setting for any of them to apply there.

## 4. Neighbouring-chapter boundaries

### Chapter 7 — identification, and a striking coincidence

Chapter 7 found the pump question **not identified**, and its sharpest failure was **positivity**: no zone with a feeder main older than 40 years had ever been upgraded, so the record contained no comparable case.

**Chapter 9's transport failure is the same fact seen from the other side.** Transport asks whether a result established elsewhere applies here; positivity asks whether the record contains cases like here. When the answer to the second is no, the answer to the first is that nothing in the record speaks to your case.

The manuscript should make this explicit **once**, because it is the strongest available demonstration that the book's parts are one architecture rather than a sequence.

### Chapter 8 — estimation

Chapter 8 estimated one quantity from one body of evidence and closed by naming both of this chapter's problems. Chapter 9 must not re-teach intervals or thresholds; it uses them.

### Chapter 10 — values

Chapter 9 closes Part II. Chapter 10 opens Part III and asks what the decision-maker wants. The line is that Chapter 9 delivers what the evidence jointly supports, and stops before what to do about it.

### Chapter 12 — robustness

Choosing an action that survives disagreement among sources is a decision problem. Chapter 9 characterises the disagreement; Chapter 12 acts under it.

## 6. High-risk conceptual collapses to prevent

1. **Combining means averaging.** The anchor shows four defensible rules giving −0.70 to −3.43.
2. **Weight by sample size.** The demonstration hands 96% of the weight to the worst source.
3. **More sources are better.** They are, and only after the question of whether they are about the same quantity is settled.
4. **Sources agreeing means they are right.** Dependence — shared data, shared assumptions, shared authors — makes agreement cheap.
5. **Replication settles transport.** `deaton2016rct` p. 27: "even successful replication tells us little either for or against simple generalization".
6. **Failure to replicate means the original was wrong.** Same page: "Nor do failures of replication make the original result useless."
7. **External validity is a property a study has.** It is a relation between a study and a target, and the binary framing is the source's own objection.
8. **Establishing causality settles generalizability.** `deaton2016rct` p. 28 says it does not.
9. **A bigger dataset deserves more weight.** `meng2018paradox` insight (IV) says the reverse can hold, and gives a 5,750-to-1 instance.
10. **Expert judgment is not evidence.** It is, and Chapter 6 already taught how to score it.
11. **Transport is a statistical adjustment.** It requires knowledge of how the settings differ, which is not in either dataset.
