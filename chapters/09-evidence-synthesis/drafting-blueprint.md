# Chapter 9 Drafting Blueprint

Status: drafting control. Governs how `chapter.md` is written. Scope, terminology, and sources are governed by `spec.md` and are not restated here.

Provisional in the same sense as `spec.md`: built on proposed `../../decisions/0016-chapter9-synthesis-terminology-and-boundary.md`.

## 1. Drafting objective

28 pages / 5 learning hours that leave the reader able to take several sources bearing on one decision, refuse to average them, and say what would have to be true of their own setting for any of them to apply.

The chapter must **defeat** twelve named collapses (`spec.md`, "Failure modes").

## 3. Voice and exposition rules

- One sentence per line in manuscript prose.
- Second person for reader tasks; third person for the case.
- **No new notation.**
- Every combined number carries the rule that produced it.
- Russell, Mackie, Cartwright and Hardie cited **as reported at** `deaton2016rct`.

### Register discipline

Three failure modes specific to this chapter.

**Sounding like a counsel of despair.** Five sources and no answer could read as *nothing can be known*. §5 must end with what would settle it, and §3 must say plainly that combining is genuinely more powerful than any single source [@bareinboim2016fusion, p. 7345].

**Sounding like a meta-analysis chapter.** No method is taught. If a passage reaches for one, it belongs to the depth curriculum.

**Over-claiming on dependence.** §3's dependence subsection has no source. It must say so in the manuscript, not only in the dossier.

## 4. Reader-facing sequence

Per `../../decisions/0008`. No new case; the anchor is the utility's five reports.

Self-explanation pauses: exactly three — §3 (which rule would you have used), §4 (what a sixth confirming source adds), §5 (which fact would settle it).

## 5. Section 1 — Five Reports and One Decision

**Beats.**

1. The situation: Chapter 7 said *not identified*, Chapter 8 estimated something else, and next summer is coming.
2. The five reports, named with sizes and estimates. No defects yet.
3. Note that they range from **−0.6 to −4.8** — a factor of eight.
4. **Opening task, about six minutes.** Which would you trust, and how would you combine them? Preserve unscored.
5. Close on the central question's em dash: the word *here* is the hard part, and §5 is about it.

**Do not** reveal the defects, the weighting results, or the terrain fact here.

## 6. Section 2 — Are They About the Same Thing?

**Beats.**

1. The prior question. Before weighting, ask what each source is a number about.
2. **Chapter 7's five attributes, applied to each source.** Table.
3. Result: **D measures a different quantity** (a rig has no feeder main, so Mechanism B cannot occur), and **B measures the same quantity in a setting where one mechanism is inoperative**.
4. The general framing, quoted [@bareinboim2016fusion, p. 7345]: data fusion, and its three kinds of heterogeneity.
5. **"Not all data are created equal"** [@bareinboim2016fusion, p. 7345], with design treated as part of what a dataset is — Chapter 4's question asked of five sources at once.
6. The three biases separated [@bareinboim2016fusion, p. 7345], and the observation that **only transportability bias is new to this book**.
7. Say explicitly that the attribute test is **the book's own extension** of Chapter 7.
8. **Reader task.** Which sources are about the utility's quantity?

## 7. Section 3 — Combining Is Not Averaging

**Beats.**

1. Grant the upside first, quoted [@bareinboim2016fusion, p. 7345]: combined knowledge "would not be possible from any individual source alone". And the second sentence, which is the chapter.
2. **The four rules, computed.** −2.48, −2.40, −0.70, −3.43.
3. **Self-explanation pause 1.** Which would you have used, and what does it assume?
4. The weight table: **C carries 95.8%**.
5. **Size is not worth** [@meng2018paradox, p. 685, insight IV], quoted.
6. The documented instance: 2,300,000 records with the mean squared error of about 400, with **all four cautions from the source note carried**.
7. The book's own summary: sample size measures how much of a source you have, not how much it is worth.
8. **Dependence**, one page. B is inside C. Two panel members wrote C's definition. D's protocol used C's definition. **Say plainly that no source was found for this passage.**
9. What to do instead: compute several rules, report the spread, treat the spread as the finding — Chapter 8's discipline in a new setting.
10. **Reader task.** Four rules, and which of the five are not independent.

## 8. Section 4 — Replication

**Beats.**

1. Frame: the utility could commission a sixth study. What would it buy?
2. **Successful replication settles little** [@deaton2016rct, p. 27], quoted.
3. **Failed replication does not condemn** [@deaton2016rct, pp. 27–28], quoted, with the constructive form.
4. **Russell's chicken**, quoted as reported, with the diagnosis sentence — "Her problem was not her methodology".
5. Draw it out: every quality control this book teaches would have passed.
6. **Self-explanation pause 2.** What would a sixth confirming study add?
7. The answer: nothing about Hillcrest, unless it contains a hilltop zone with an old main.
8. **Causality does not guarantee generalizability** [@deaton2016rct, p. 28], and the "anecdotal causality" phrase attributed.
9. Say plainly that this is uncomfortable after Chapter 7, and why it does not make Chapter 7 wasted.
10. State the source's status: unrefereed working paper; strongest claims attributed.

## 9. Section 5 — Will It Work Here?

**Beats.**

1. `external validity` introduced, then criticised [@deaton2016rct, p. 27], both quotations.
2. `transportability` as the preferred question form [@bareinboim2016fusion, p. 7350], quoted.
3. **Support factors** [@deaton2016rct, p. 28], with the television example.
4. The condition for equal average effects [@deaton2016rct, p. 29], and why those factors differ.
5. **The anchor's support factor: terrain.** Hillcrest lifts; B's forty zones do not. The mechanism is absent from every zone in B.
6. **Self-explanation pause 3.** Which single fact about the five sources settles whether any applies at Hillcrest?
7. The answer: **no source contains a zone with a main older than 40 years.**
8. **The identity, once**: this is Chapter 7's positivity failure, seen from the other side. Label as the book's own.
9. The second tradition [@bareinboim2016fusion, p. 7350], on study populations and populations of interest.
10. **Chapter 3's contextual specificity extended**, one paragraph, per `../../decisions/0010` clause 2.4.
11. The constructive close: what would settle it — a hilltop zone with an old main, anywhere; or an argument from hydraulics; or one deliberately arranged case. Chapter 7's §7 list, reused.
12. **Reader task.** What would have to be true for source B to apply?

## 10. Section 6 — Expert Judgment Is a Source

**Beats.**

1. The panel is a source. Not excluded for being judgment; not privileged for being expert.
2. **Chapter 6, reused not re-taught.** An unscored panel is Chapter 6's unscored forecaster.
3. The remedy: a record — what was said, when, conditional on what, what happened.
4. The heuristics are useful [@tversky1974judgment, p. 1124], so do not present judgment as defective.
5. Two panel members wrote C's definition — **experts are a dependence problem too.**
6. **Planted-defect diagnosis task.** Five defects per `spec.md`. Feedback linked only after production.

## 12. What the draft may not do

- Teach any synthesis, pooling, or heterogeneity method.
- Teach selection diagrams, transport formulas, or elicitation protocols.
- Introduce any notation.
- Claim any of the four rules is correct.
- Present source C or D as worthless.
- Cite Russell, Mackie, or Cartwright and Hardie directly.
- Present `deaton2016rct` as refereed, or its strongest claims as consensus.
- Assert the dependence material without saying it is unsourced.
- Characterise the replication-crisis literature.
- Overturn Chapter 7's not-identified verdict by adding sources.
- Restate Chapter 5's or Chapter 7's tables.
- Recommend an action for the utility.
- Present synthetic case values as typical, standard, or recommended.
