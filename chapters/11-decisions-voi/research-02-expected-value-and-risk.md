# Research 02 — Expected Value, and Whether to Use It

Cluster R02 of `research-plan.md`. Closed.

Sources: `colyvan2016voi` pp. 302–303, read directly; `../../canon/terminology.md`, which instructs this chapter.

## 1. The rule, as a source applies it

`colyvan2016voi` works its examples on **expected monetary value** and treats the recommendation as following from it.

The first coin example, p. 302: a bet paying $20 on heads and costing $10 otherwise, with the probability of heads known only to lie between 0.4 and 0.6. The paper's footnote gives the arithmetic and concludes that accepting is preferred throughout that range.

The second, p. 303: a fair coin, paying either $5 or $15 on heads with equal probability, costing $10 otherwise. "The expected monetary value of accepting the bet is the same as not accepting it; it's $0 and **decision theory recommends indifference** between accepting and not accepting the bet." [@colyvan2016voi, p. 303]

**Read that last clause carefully.** *Decision theory recommends indifference* — because expected monetary value is the rule being applied. The recommendation is a consequence of the rule, not of the situation.

## 3. The gap: no source for risk attitude

**No source was obtained that treats risk attitude in a form this book could teach from.**

Four candidates were considered. The economics guidance that mentions risk aversion does so in passing, inside regulatory-analysis contexts, and would not support instruction. The classical decision-theory literature — utility functions, certainty equivalents, risk premiums — was not obtained.

### The proposed handling

**Name it and demonstrate it on the anchor. Do not formalize it.**

The anchor supplies what is needed without any source, because it is visible in the numbers: one of the three acts costs the same in both states. It has **no spread**. It also costs more in expectation than the act that wins.

So a reader can see, from a table, that:

- expected value prefers the act with the lower average and the wider spread;
- a decision-maker who would pay to remove the spread is not making an arithmetic error;
- **the arithmetic cannot supply that preference**, and whoever adopts expected value has adopted one.

That is all the chapter claims. Utility functions, certainty equivalents, and risk premiums are named as existing and routed to the depth curriculum.

### Why this is not a fifth instance

`../../decisions/README.md` has the demonstrate-because-unsourced disposition on notice at four instances, with the standing instruction that a further chapter should reopen research rather than invoke precedent.

**This is the closest the book has come, and the distinction is real.**

In the four recorded instances, a **practice** was taught with no source behind it — representational aggregation, censoring, four cheap checks, source dependence. The reader was given something to do.

Here the chapter **declines to teach the practice**. It states what its own table displays — that one act has no spread and costs more — and says that preferring it is a preference. There is no method, no procedure, and nothing the reader is instructed to do that a source would need to license.

`../../decisions/0018` clause 4.4 records this rather than assuming it, and flags that if the author disagrees, the honest response is to cut the risk-attitude material rather than to source it retrospectively.

## 4. What expected value is good for

The chapter should not leave a reader thinking the rule is suspect.

**It is the only rule in the chapter that uses all the information in the table.** The alternatives — pick the act with the best worst case, or the best best case, or the smallest spread — each throw away most of the numbers and most of the probabilities.

**And it is the rule that makes value of information computable at all.** §5's entire calculation is a difference between two expected values. A reader who rejects expected value has also rejected the machinery that shows the pump test is not worth running, which is the chapter's most useful result.

The honest position: **use it, know you are using it, and check whether your answer survives a decision-maker who would not.**
