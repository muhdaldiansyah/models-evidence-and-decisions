# Chapter 11: Decisions Under Uncertainty and Value of Information

## 1. Seven Alternatives and No Way to Choose

Chapter 10 left the utility better off and no closer to a decision.

It has three fundamental objectives that can be tested rather than agreed to. It has seven alternatives, none of which required new data. It has four conflicts between the objectives, stated in directions somebody could argue about.

**And it has no way to choose among any of it.**

That was deliberate. Weighing objectives against each other is a different activity from writing them down, and doing it early hides the fact that a choice occurred.

This chapter does the weighing.

### Before reading further

Take about **six minutes**.

> **Of Chapter 10's seven alternatives, which would you recommend — and how did you decide?**
>
> The second half is the part to write down.

Keep what you write. You will come back to it.

---

### Two questions, and the second is the one

The chapter's governed question has two halves.

> **Which act is defensible, and would more evidence change it?**

Most of what follows serves the first half: laying a decision out, computing, checking where the answer turns.

**The second half closes the longest thread in this book.**

Chapter 2 named the pump test — run the duty pump at elevated output through a hot afternoon and record pressure at the top of the zone. Chapter 5 confirmed it was obtainable and had never been done. Chapter 6 computed what it was worth in evidential terms: belief moves from roughly two-to-one to either ten-to-one or one-to-three, decisive in both directions.

Nine chapters have pointed at that test.

**This chapter computes what it is worth to the decision, and the answer is £2,300 against a cost of £8,000.**

Do not run it.

That is not a trick and Chapter 6 was not wrong. The test is exactly as informative as Chapter 6 said. What this chapter adds is that **informative and valuable are different things**, and that the arithmetic separating them takes an afternoon and should have been done before anybody costed a study.

### What this chapter costs you

Chapters 6 to 9 each took something away — a probability without conditioning, a causal claim without assumptions, an interval that meant more than it did, five sources that could be averaged.

**This one takes away the study.**

By the end you will find it harder to propose research, harder to accept "we need more data" as a conclusion, and much harder to sign off a monitoring programme without asking which decision it feeds. Several things that currently look like diligence will stop being available.

**What replaces them is cheaper and better.** Two lines of arithmetic that say whether the answer could turn on what you are proposing to find out — and which, in this case, would have saved a year.

### Where this sits

Chapter 10 worked from a six-step process: decision context, objectives, alternatives, prospects, trade-offs, recommendations [@bradley2016structured, p. 8].

**It took the first three. This chapter is the last three.**

### What changes about the work

Parts I and II asked whether the evidence was any good. Chapter 10 asked what the utility wanted. This chapter puts the two together, and the joining is where most of the difficulty lives.

**Evidence arrives as beliefs about states. Values arrive as consequences of acts. A decision needs both in the same object**, and building that object forces choices that neither activity makes on its own — which acts to consider, which states matter, and what everything is worth in a single currency.

Those choices are made in §2, before any arithmetic, and they carry more of the answer than the arithmetic does. The chapter is arranged so you can see them being made.

### And a warning about what the machinery hides

Everything after §2 looks objective. There are numbers, they combine by rules, and the rules are not in dispute.

**The judgments are all upstream.** By the time a committee sees £173,600 against £216,000, the acts have been chosen, the states fixed, three objectives collapsed into pounds, and a probability adopted from a register of eleven cases. None of that is visible in the two figures.

That is not an argument against computing. It is the reason §6 exists.

## 2. Laying the Decision Out

A decision under uncertainty has three components, and every treatment of the subject uses them under one name or another.

**The acts** available to you.
**The states** the world might be in, which you do not control.
**The consequences** that follow from each act in each state.

That is it. Add probabilities over the states and you can compute; leave them out and you can still see the shape.

**A note on where this comes from.** No source read for this book teaches the layout. It is standard material in a decision-analysis literature this project did not obtain, and the presentation here is the book's own of something that is nobody's invention. Said plainly rather than dressed up as a finding.

### Why a table rather than a tree

Two presentations are conventional and they carry the same information.

A **tree** branches from a choice through what you do not control to outcomes. It earns its keep when the decision is sequential — decide, observe, decide again — and §5 needs it once for exactly that.

A **table** with acts as rows and states as columns is the same thing for a one-shot decision, and it is far easier to read without diagram conventions.

**This chapter uses a table everywhere except §5.** The book has no figure conventions and does not need any; a table and one text tree carry everything.

### The states are not new

The utility's uncertainty is the one it has had since Chapter 2.

**Mechanism A** — the duty pump's capacity limits refill, so the hilltop tank falls.
**Mechanism B** — friction loss along the old feeder main drops pressure at the top of the zone.

One of those is the case. Chapter 6 put the odds at **7 : 4** for Mechanism A, from a register of eleven investigations in pumped zones, so about **0.636**.

**Nothing new is invented here.** The states and the probabilities are Chapter 6's, unchanged, and this chapter does not get to improve them.

### Three acts, from seven

Seven alternatives will not fit a table anyone can read. Three will.

**The narrowing is a choice, and here is the criterion used**: one act for each mechanism, plus one act that works under either.

| | Act | Cost | Why it is here |
|---|---|---:|---|
| **A** | Fit a variable-speed drive to the existing duty pump | **£40,000** | acts on Mechanism A |
| **B** | Reline the feeder main | **£260,000** | acts on Mechanism B |
| **C** | Targeted contingency for the forty households, three years | **£36,000** | acts on neither, and works under both |

The four dropped: the pressure-managed sub-zone and local storage are variants of C at higher cost; the deliberate arranged upgrade and the instrument-and-defer option are information-gathering acts, which §5 handles separately; and like-for-like replacement was dominated by A once Chapter 10 dissolved the housing constraint.

**Chapter 10 spent five pages establishing that a small option set is a claim about what matters.** This one is no exception. A different criterion gives a different table, and the criterion is on the page so it can be argued with.

Notice what the criterion did, though, because it is doing real work.

**One act per mechanism** guarantees the table can distinguish the states. If both remaining acts helped equally under both mechanisms, the whole question of which mechanism operates would drop out of the decision — and §5's answer would be zero rather than small.

**One act that works under either** is the one most option sets omit, because it does not address the cause. It is Chapter 10's alternative 6, and it is on the table here precisely because it is the act a capital paper cannot express.

A narrowing chosen for other reasons — the three the engineers costed, say, or the three that fit the envelope — would produce a table that looks identical and answers a different question.

### The table

Total cost over three years, in **£ thousands**. Lower is better.

| Act | If Mechanism A operates | If Mechanism B operates |
|---|---:|---:|
| **A** — variable-speed drive | **130** | **250** |
| **B** — reline the main | **330** | **300** |
| **C** — targeted contingency | **216** | **216** |

Read the rows before reading on.

**Act A** is cheap and works if the pump is the constraint. If it is not, more flow through a sixty-eight-year-old main achieves little and the household-events continue.

**Act B** is expensive and works if the main is the constraint. It costs a great deal in either state because the excavation happens either way.

**Act C** costs the same whatever the world turns out to be. It does not fix anything; it gets water to forty households on the days they would otherwise be without.

### The number in each cell is a value judgment

Each cell is capital plus a monetisation of the household-events that remain.

**That monetisation is not a measurement.**

Chapter 10 produced three fundamental objectives — households without adequate pressure, whole-life cost, and disruption during works. A table has one number per cell, so all three have been collapsed into pounds by somebody who decided what a household-event is worth.

This is not an artefact of the anchor. It is a general property of the machinery, and the source that supplies this chapter's value-of-information material names it as a limitation:

> "value of information studies typically do require a single currency for the relevant values, be it monetary value, a biodiversity measure, or whatever." [@colyvan2016voi, p. 305]

**So the table is not the decision made objective.** It is the decision with a value judgment compressed into each cell, and a different judgment gives a different table. §6 returns to this.

### Where the probabilities came from, and why that matters here

The table needs one more thing before it computes: how likely each state is.

**Chapter 6 supplied it and this chapter does not get to improve it.** Seven pump-limited investigations against four main-related, in pumped zones, from a register covering eighteen years and three network configurations. `p(Mechanism A) = 7/11 ≈ 0.636`.

Chapter 6 also said, at length, what is wrong with that number: eleven cases is not many, the restriction to pumped zones is a judgment, and the network that produced the early entries is not the network Hillcrest sits in now.

**All of that is inherited.** A decision built on a probability inherits everything the probability's provenance carries, and §4 is where the chapter asks whether it matters.

This is worth pausing on because it is the join between the book's two halves. Ten chapters interrogated evidence. The product of all that interrogation, for this decision, is one number — and the honest thing to do with it is not to polish it further but to find out whether the answer depends on it.

### And the table can send you backwards

One warning before the arithmetic.

If the table produces an answer nobody can live with, the response is not to accept it. It is to go back to Chapter 10 and ask whether the alternatives were the right ones — which is what that chapter's source says: "If alternatives are not achieving the objectives, it could imply that they should be reformulated" [@bradley2016structured, p. 54].

The six steps are not a pipeline. They are a loop, and the loop runs backwards more often than forwards.

### What the table leaves out

Two things are missing from every decision table, and knowing what they are is part of using one.

**Everyone else's response.** The utility's forty households, its regulator, its contractors, and the three zones losing their renewal money all have interests in what it decides, and several of them will act on the decision once it is made. A contingency scheme that becomes known may change who complains. A deferred upgrade may change what the regulator asks next year.

**None of that is in the table**, and putting it there requires treating other parties as choosers rather than as states of the world. That is Chapter 15's subject.

**And the passage of time.** The table treats this as one choice made once. It is not: the utility will decide again next year, with more information, and an act that preserves the ability to decide again is worth something the table cannot show.

Chapter 10's alternative 5 — upgrade one comparable zone deliberately and watch — is exactly such an act, and it does not appear in §2's three because a one-shot table cannot express it. **Chapters 12 and 14 can.**

### Task: build one

Take a decision currently live in your own organisation.

1. Write the **acts** — at least three, including one that does not fix the problem but limits its consequences.
2. Write the **states** — at least two things you do not control that would change which act is best.
3. Fill the **consequences**, in one currency.
4. Then write one sentence saying what the currency conversion assumed, and who decided it.

Question 4 is the one that will be missing from every version of this table you have ever been shown.

## 3. Expected Value, and Whether to Use It

The table has six numbers and a probability. Now the arithmetic.

### The three expected costs

Weight each row by the probability of each state and add.

**Act A:** `0.636 × 130 + 0.364 × 250 = 82.7 + 90.9 = ` **173.6**

**Act B:** `0.636 × 330 + 0.364 × 300 = 210.0 + 109.1 = ` **319.1**

**Act C:** `216.0` in both states, so **216.0**

| Act | Expected cost |
|---|---:|
| **A** — variable-speed drive | **173.6** |
| B — reline the main | 319.1 |
| C — targeted contingency | 216.0 |

**Act A is best, at £173,600.**

Before going on, notice two things about that table.

**Act B is dominated and can be dropped.** It costs more than Act C in both states — 330 against 216, and 300 against 216. There is no belief about the world, and no attitude to risk, under which relining the main is preferable to the contingency. It does not need weighing; it needs deleting.

**That is worth doing first, always.** Scanning for dominated acts costs seconds, needs no probabilities, and it removed a £260,000 option from the argument before anyone weighted anything. A committee that spends an hour debating Act B is debating something the table already settled.

**And Act A wins by a lot.** £173,600 against £216,000 is a margin of £42,400 — about a quarter. That is not a close call, and §4 asks whether it survives the uncertainty in the probability.

### And now the move Chapter 6 refused to make

Chapter 6 computed an expected end-of-week storage and then stopped, deliberately. It said:

> *The expected end-of-week storage is 3.8 ML.*
> *Therefore we should plan as though storage will be 3.8 ML.*
>
> The first sentence is arithmetic. The second is a **decision rule**.

And it named what the rule assumes, then handed the move here: "that choice gets made deliberately" in Chapter 11.

**So make it. Choosing the act with the lowest expected cost is a rule, and using it is a choice.**

The rule treats a certainty of £173,600 as equivalent to a gamble between £130,000 and £250,000 with those probabilities. That equivalence is not a fact about arithmetic. It is a statement about how much you care about spread, and the name for caring about it not at all is **risk neutrality**.

The source that supplies this chapter's material shows the same thing from the other side. Working a bet whose expected monetary value is zero either way, it says: "it's $0 and **decision theory recommends indifference** between accepting and not accepting the bet" [@colyvan2016voi, p. 303].

**Decision theory recommends indifference because expected monetary value is the rule being applied.** The recommendation follows from the rule, not from the situation.

### Pause: which act would a cautious committee choose?

Before reading on, write two or three sentences.

> Look again at the table. **Act C costs £216,000 whatever happens.**
>
> A committee that would rather know its cost than gamble on it — which act do they choose, and what does the choice cost them?

---

**They choose C, and it costs £42,400.**

`216.0 − 173.6 = 42.4`

That is what they pay, in expectation, to remove the spread entirely. Under Act A they face a real chance of £250,000; under C they face £216,000 with certainty.

**Neither choice is an error.**

A regulated utility that has just had two bad summers, whose board has been in front of a select committee, may reasonably decide that a known cost is worth £42,400. A larger organisation making fifty such decisions a year, where the highs and lows average out, may reasonably decide it is not.

**The arithmetic cannot supply that preference and neither can any amount of evidence.** It belongs to the decision-maker, and Chapter 10 established that values are theirs to supply.

### What this book does and does not teach about risk

**No source was obtained for a formal treatment of risk attitude.**

Four candidates were tried. The economics guidance that mentions risk aversion does so in passing, inside regulatory contexts, and would not support teaching from.

So this chapter does what its own table can display and stops there. **It names the phenomenon, shows that one act has no spread and costs more, and says that preferring it is a preference.**

There is machinery for this — utility functions, certainty equivalents, risk premiums — and it lets you express how much spread is worth to you and then compute with it. **This book does not teach it, and says so rather than gesturing.** It is depth curriculum.

What survives without any of it: **the question**. Does this decision have a spread? Which act removes it? What does removing it cost? Three lines of arithmetic on a table you already have, and they are the part most committees actually argue about.

### Which act does a bad summer punish?

There is a way of reading the table that committees find more natural than expectations, and it is worth having because it makes the same point without arithmetic.

**Ask what happens to each act if the world goes the wrong way.**

Act A's bad case is £250,000 — the drive is fitted, the main was the problem, and the household-events continue at a cost the utility has just spent capital failing to prevent.

Act C has no bad case. £216,000 either way.

Act B's bad case is £330,000, and its good case is £300,000, which is why §3 dropped it.

So the real question in the room is: **is avoiding a £250,000 outcome worth £42,400 in expectation?**

That is a question about the utility's circumstances rather than about pumps. A board that has been in front of a select committee twice may answer yes. A board with headroom may answer no. **Neither is answerable from the table**, and both are legitimate.

**And this is why the table is worth building even for a committee that will not compute.** It converts *should we spend £310,000 on a pump* into *is removing a £250,000 downside worth £42,400*, which is a question the people in the room are qualified to answer and the analysts are not.

### The case for expected value anyway

Having said the rule is a choice, the chapter should say why it is a good one.

**It is the only rule here that uses all the information.** Choose the best worst case and you have used two of the six numbers and none of the probabilities. Choose the smallest spread and you have used the differences and ignored the levels. Expected value uses every cell and both probabilities.

**And it is what makes §5 possible.** The entire value-of-information calculation is a difference between two expected values. A reader who rejects the rule has also rejected the machinery that shows the pump test is not worth running — which is the most useful result in the chapter.

The honest position: **use it, know you are using it, and check whether the answer survives a decision-maker who would not.**

### Where the rule quietly decided something

One consequence of adopting expected value deserves stating, because it is invisible in the result.

**The rule treats the forty households as interchangeable with money at the rate the table's monetisation set.**

Under Act A in the bad state, the utility spends £40,000 and forty households continue to lose pressure on hot days, at a monetised cost of £210,000. Under Act C, the utility spends £36,000 and the households get water, at a monetised cost of £180,000.

Those numbers are commensurable only because somebody made them so. **Expected value then averages across states as though a pound of avoided household-event in the world where Mechanism B operates were the same thing as a pound in the world where it does not** — which is fine, and is an assumption.

**The rule did not make that assumption. §2 did**, when it filled the cells. The rule simply carried it forward, and by the time a committee sees £173,600 the judgment is four steps upstream and invisible.

That is the general hazard with any decision rule: **it is faithful to the table, and the table is where the judgments went.**

### A good decision is not a good outcome

One more thing belongs here, briefly, because it is where organisations lose the thread.

Suppose the utility fits the variable-speed drive, and Mechanism B turns out to be the case, and next summer is worse than the last.

**Was the decision wrong?**

No. It was made against the best available belief, with the alternatives written down, the states stated, and the rule named. The world went the other way, as it does about a third of the time on these numbers.

`nasem2026decisionmaking` supports separating decision process from outcome, and the practical form is simple: **a decision is defensible when its acts, states, consequences, probabilities, and the rule used to choose are all written down and arguable.** That is the same discipline Chapters 6 to 10 applied to probabilities, causal claims, estimates, sources, and objectives, arriving one last time.

Grading a decision by its outcome is the error the idea exists to prevent, and it is what an organisation does by default after a bad summer.

### Two rules that are not this one

Expected value is not the only rule available, and naming the alternatives makes clearer what it is doing.

**Choose the best worst case.** Look at each act's worst column and take the act whose worst is least bad. Act A's worst is 250, Act B's is 330, Act C's is 216 — so this rule chooses **C**.

**Choose the smallest spread.** Act A spans 120, Act B spans 30, Act C spans 0 — so this rule chooses **C** as well, and would choose **B** over **A**, which is plainly wrong given that B costs more in both states.

Both rules are used in practice, usually without being named. The first is what a committee is doing when it asks what happens if this goes badly. The second is what an organisation is doing when it prefers predictable spending.

**And both throw information away.** The first ignores the probabilities entirely — it would choose the same act if Mechanism A were certain. The second ignores the levels, which is how it recommended a dominated act.

**Expected value is the only rule here that uses every cell and both probabilities.** That is the case for it, and it is a real one.

1. Compute the three expected costs yourself and check them against the figures above.
2. Write one sentence stating what choosing on that basis assumes.
3. Suppose the committee will not accept a chance of £250,000 under any circumstances. Which act do they take, and what have they paid?

## 4. Does the Answer Turn on Anything?

Act A wins by a wide margin — £173,600 against £216,000. Before going further, it is worth asking whether that margin is real.

### The probability is not a precise number

Chapter 6 was careful about where 7 : 4 came from. Eleven investigations, in pumped zones, from a register covering eighteen years and three different network configurations.

**Eleven.** One reclassified case moves it. And the network that produced the early entries is not the network Hillcrest sits in now.

So `p(Mechanism A) = 0.636` is a stated starting point with known weaknesses. It might reasonably be 0.5. It might reasonably be 0.75.

**That is `ambiguity`**, and it is different from the uncertainty already in the table. The table handles not knowing which state obtains. This is not knowing the probability of the states.

### Two intervals, one lesson

The source works the distinction on a bet, and it is worth having in full because the anchor is the same shape.

You are offered $20 if a coin lands heads, and you pay $10 otherwise. All you know is that the probability of heads lies somewhere between 0.4 and 0.6.

> "a simple sensitivity analysis of the decision model here shows that the expected utility of accepting the bet is greater than rejecting it. **No further information is required in deciding whether to accept this bet or not.**" [@colyvan2016voi, p. 302]

Now change one thing. Suppose the probability lies between 0.2 and 0.4 instead.

> "Because the interval [0.2, 0.4] straddles the critical value of 1/3, all we can say is that if the probability of heads is between 1/3 and 0.4, we should accept the bet but not otherwise." [@colyvan2016voi, p. 302]

And in that case, the paper notes, "sensitivity analysis does not help in deciding whether to accept the bet" [@colyvan2016voi, pp. 302–303].

**Same bet. Same ignorance about the probability. Opposite conclusions about whether the ignorance matters.**

What separates them is whether the range **straddles the point at which the best act changes**.

### Pause: where does the utility's answer turn?

Before reading on, work it.

> Act A costs 130 if Mechanism A operates and 250 if it does not. Act C costs 216 either way.
>
> **At what probability of Mechanism A do they cost the same?**

---

Set them equal.

`130p + 250(1 − p) = 216`

`250 − 120p = 216`

`120p = 34`

`p = ` **0.283**

**Above 0.283, Act A is better. Below it, Act C is.**

| | p(Mechanism A) | Against 0.283 |
|---|---:|---|
| The prior | **0.636** | comfortably above |
| A pessimistic reading | 0.50 | still above |
| A very pessimistic reading | 0.35 | still above |

**The prior would have to be less than half what the register says before the answer changed.**

So the decision is insensitive to the probability across any range a reasonable person would defend, which is the coin's first interval exactly. The ambiguity is real and it does not matter here.

**That is a finding, and it took two lines of arithmetic.**

### The other things the answer might turn on

The probability is not the only number that could be wrong, and the same test applies to each.

**The cost of Act A.** At £40,000 capital it is cheap. Capital adds equally to both of its cells, so it adds directly to its expected cost — which means **Act A stops winning at £82,400 of capital**, an overrun of £42,400.

That figure should look familiar: it is exactly the margin from §3. The two are the same number because capital shifts the row without tilting it, and noticing that saves recomputing anything.

So the answer survives a doubling of the drive's cost and fails at slightly more than that. Whether a £40,000 estimate is good to within £42,000 is a question for whoever produced it, and nobody has asked.

**The monetisation.** If a household-event is worth half what the table assumes, every cell's event component halves, the acts compress toward their capital costs, and Act A's margin widens. If it is worth double, the compression runs the other way.

**The horizon.** Three years was chosen. Over ten, the capital acts amortise and the contingency accumulates — Act C is £12,000 a year forever, and Act A is £40,000 once.

**Two of those three are more fragile than the probability**, which is the number everybody was arguing about.

That is the general finding and it is worth stating plainly: **the input everyone disputes is frequently not the one the answer turns on.** The probability of Mechanism A has occupied five chapters of this book, and the decision would survive being badly wrong about it. The cost of a variable-speed drive has occupied nobody, and a fifty-thousand-pound error in it flips the recommendation.

**Run the check before the argument**, not after.

### Sensitivity analysis, for the third time

This book has now used those words three times, for three jobs.

**Chapter 5** named it and refused it — varying inputs inside a formulation cannot see the formulation, so offering it as the criticism section is a way of skipping criticism.

**Chapter 8** used it as a model check — which assumptions does the answer move on, and therefore where should checking effort go.

**Chapter 11** asks the decision question — **at what point does the best act change?**

One technique, three jobs, and the third is the one that makes §5 tractable.

**And what it is not**, in any of the three: varying every input by ±20% and reporting that the answer held. That produces a number nobody asked for. The useful output is not a range of answers — it is **a critical value**, and the question is whether your uncertainty reaches it.

### What to do when the answer does turn

The anchor was fortunate. Suppose it had not been — suppose the prior sat at 0.30, just above the critical value of 0.283, and a defensible range ran from 0.20 to 0.45.

Then the range straddles, and the source's second coin applies: sensitivity analysis "does not help in deciding" [@colyvan2016voi, p. 303].

**Three responses are available and they are the rest of Part III.**

**Get information** — if a study could move belief across the critical value and is cheap enough. That is §5, and it is the only one of the three this chapter treats.

**Hedge** — choose an act that is acceptable across the whole range rather than best at any point in it. Act C is exactly such an act here: it costs 216 whatever the world does, so a decision-maker who cannot defend a probability at all can still take it. **That is Chapter 12's subject**, and it is a genuinely different way of using the same table.

**Stage the decision** — do something small and reversible now, learn from it, and decide the rest later. Chapter 10's alternative 5 was of this kind, and sequential decisions of that shape are Chapters 12 and 14.

**Notice that all three become available only once you know where the critical value is.** Two lines of arithmetic in §4 determine which of the three you are in, and organisations routinely reach for the first without checking whether they need any.

### One number is doing all the work

Step back and notice how thin the basis for §3's recommendation actually is.

**£173,600 against £216,000 rests on:** six cells whose values embed an unstated exchange rate between money, households, and disruption; one probability from eleven investigations across three network configurations; a three-year horizon nobody defended; and three acts chosen by a criterion this chapter wrote down and could have written differently.

**And it is still the best available basis**, which is the uncomfortable part.

The alternative is not a firmer number. It is a recommendation reached the way the committee paper in Chapter 10 reached one — by somebody's judgment, unstated, with none of the four items above visible to anyone who might disagree.

**The value of the machinery is not that it is reliable. It is that its unreliability is located.** Every one of the four items above can be named, argued with, and varied, and §4 is how you find out which of them matters. A recommendation with no table has the same weaknesses and no way to find them.

### Task: find the critical value

For the table you built in §2:

1. Take the two acts with the lowest expected costs and find the probability at which they are equal.
2. Say how far that value is from your stated probability.
3. Then answer: **would you have to be badly wrong, or slightly wrong, for the answer to change?**

If the answer is *slightly*, §5 is about what to do. If it is *badly*, §5 will tell you to stop.

## 5. What Would It Be Worth to Know?

Now the second half of the chapter's question.

### The assumption everybody holds

The source opens on it:

> "It is natural to think that more information will lead to better decision making and thus better outcomes." [@colyvan2016voi, p. 302]

And notes how deeply it runs:

> "It is a common assumption that more and better quality data will lead to better conservation management decisions. Indeed, this assumption **lies behind, and motivates, a great deal of current work** in conservation biology. Of course, more data can lead to better decisions in some cases but decision-theoretic models of the value of information show that this need not always be the case: sometimes the cost of data collection is too high." [@colyvan2016voi, p. 302]

**Not a mistake somebody makes. The premise of a research programme.**

And of ten chapters of this book. Chapters 3, 4, 6, 7, and 8 each found a term that more data does not touch — but the whole enterprise of interrogating evidence assumes evidence is worth interrogating.

### A test that works perfectly and is worth nothing

> "some medical diagnostic tests have this character. Radiographing a suspected broken toe costs time and money (not to mention the radiation exposure to the patient), and yet the treatment for a bruised toe or a broken toe is the same: strap it and avoid any activities that hurt the toe. **The value of information delivered by the radiographic examination in this decision about treatment is zero.**" [@colyvan2016voi, p. 303]

**Zero, and nothing is wrong with the radiograph.**

It is accurate. It answers its question correctly. It resolves a real uncertainty. And the treatment is the same either way, so knowing changes nothing you would do.

Then the qualification that makes it usable:

> "Of course the same information might be valuable for other purposes. For instance, typically a broken toe will take longer to mend than a bruised toe, so the radiographic examination will have some value in determining, for example, when the patient can expect to be able to kick a football again." [@colyvan2016voi, p. 303]

**Value of information is relative to a decision.**

You have met that shape now in nine chapters. Adequacy for a use. Validity for an interpretation. Trustworthiness for a quantity. Criticism for stakes. Probability for information. Identification for assumptions. Transport for a setting. Objectives for a decision. **And now the worth of evidence, for a decision.**

The same information can be worth nothing for one question and a great deal for another, and the number is not a property of the information.

### Pause: what do you think the test is worth?

Before any arithmetic, commit to a figure.

> The pump test moves belief about Mechanism A from **64%** to either **91%** or **24%**. Chapter 6 called it decisive in both directions.
>
> **What is it worth to this decision?** Write a number in pounds.

---

### Test first, or act now?

The choice has a sequence, so it needs a shape the table cannot show.

```
                     ┌── recovery (0.596) ── choose best act ──▶ ?
   ┌── run the test ─┤
   │                 └── no recovery (0.404) ── choose best act ──▶ ?
───┤
   │
   └── act now ──────────────────────────── choose best act ──▶ 173.6
```

That is the only tree in this book. It is here because *decide, observe, decide again* cannot be laid out as a table, and it is drawn in text because the book has no figure conventions and does not need any.

### Working the branches

Chapter 6's likelihoods, unchanged: a recovery is expected with probability **0.85** under Mechanism A and **0.15** under Mechanism B.

**How often does the test come back positive?**

`(7/11)(0.85) + (4/11)(0.15) = 0.541 + 0.055 = ` **0.596**

So no recovery **0.404** of the time.

**And Chapter 6 already computed the beliefs afterwards**: 0.908 for Mechanism A after a recovery, 0.236 after none.

Now recompute the table under each.

**If the test shows recovery** — `p(A) = 0.908`:

| Act | Expected cost |
|---|---:|
| **A** | **141.0** |
| B | 327.3 |
| C | 216.0 |

Best: **A**, at 141.0. *Which is what you would have done anyway.*

**If it shows no recovery** — `p(A) = 0.236`:

| Act | Expected cost |
|---|---:|
| A | 221.7 |
| B | 307.1 |
| **C** | **216.0** |

Best: **C**, at 216.0. *The act changes — and it wins by £5,700.*

### What the test is worth

Expected cost **with** the test:

`0.596 × 141.0 + 0.404 × 216.0 = 84.0 + 87.3 = ` **171.3**

Expected cost **without** it: **173.6**

**The difference is £2,300.**

The test costs **£8,000** — a day of elevated pumping, a crew, instrumentation, and a planned service risk on a network that has had two bad summers.

**Do not run it.**

### Reading the two branches

Before drawing the conclusion, it is worth seeing exactly where the £2,300 comes from, because the shape is the lesson.

**On the recovery branch — which happens 60% of the time — the act does not change.** The utility fits the variable-speed drive, which is what it would have done without testing. The test cost £8,000 and bought a firmer belief about something the utility was not going to act on differently.

That branch contributes **nothing**. Not a little; nothing. The improvement from 173.6 to 141.0 on that branch is not a gain from the test — it is the same act looking cheaper because belief moved in its favour. **The utility does not get to bank that.** It was always going to fit the drive; learning that this was a better idea than it thought does not save any money.

**On the no-recovery branch — 40% of the time — the act does change**, from A to C, and C is better by £5,700.

`0.404 × 5.7 = ` **£2,300.**

**That is the entire value of the test**: the chance that it changes your mind, multiplied by how much better off you are when it does.

**Which is the definition, arrived at from the numbers rather than asserted.** And it makes the two ways a test can be worthless obvious: it is worth nothing if it never changes the act, and worth nothing if the act it changes to is barely better.

### Sit with that

The pump test was named in Chapter 2 as the observation that would settle which mechanism operates. Chapter 5 confirmed it was obtainable, had not been done, and that this was the single most conspicuous gap in the utility's reasoning. Chapter 6 spent six pages computing that one hot afternoon moves belief from roughly two-to-one to either ten-to-one or one-to-three.

**Every one of those chapters was right.**

The test is exactly as informative as Chapter 6 said. It is decisive about the mechanism. It would settle a question that has been open for nine chapters.

**And it is worth £2,300 to the decision the utility actually faces**, because on the branch where it comes back positive the utility does what it was going to do anyway, and on the branch where it does not, the act changes to one that is barely better.

This is the broken toe. Informative, accurate, and irrelevant to the treatment.

### Why, in one line

§4 already showed it, from the other direction.

**The critical value is 0.283 and belief sits at 0.636.** For the test to change the act it has to drag belief below 0.283, and it only manages that on one of its two branches, where it lands at 0.236 — just below, so the act changes but the improvement is small.

**Sensitivity analysis and value of information are the same finding twice.** If the answer does not turn on the uncertainty, resolving the uncertainty is not worth much. §4 costs two lines; §5 costs ten.

### What would have made it worth running

It is worth asking what would have to be different, because the answer sharpens the idea.

**A bigger gap between the acts on the branch where the act changes.** The test flips A to C on the no-recovery branch, and C beats A there by £5,700. If it beat it by £60,000, the test would be worth having — the information would be steering you away from a real mistake rather than a marginal one.

**A prior closer to the critical value.** If belief sat at 0.35 rather than 0.636, both branches would matter and the test would be resolving a genuine doubt rather than confirming a settled one.

**A cheaper test.** £2,300 of value would justify a £1,500 test. The pump test costs £8,000 because it needs a crew, a hot afternoon, and a deliberate service risk.

**Notice what is not on that list.** A *more accurate* test does not help much, and §5's closing task asks you to see why: the ceiling is £12,400 whatever the test's accuracy, and even a perfect test only earns its keep against that.

**The lesson generalises.** Information is worth having when the decision is close, the stakes of getting it wrong are large, and the information is cheap. Two of those three are properties of the decision, not of the information — which is why *how good is this study* is the wrong first question.

### And notice what Chapter 6 could not have told you

Chapter 6 computed a likelihood ratio of about **5.7** for this test — the recovery is nearly six times more expected under Mechanism A than under Mechanism B — and correctly called that a strong observation.

**That number is about the evidence. It contains nothing about the acts, the costs, or the currency.**

Two decisions could face the identical test, with the identical ratio and the identical prior, and it could be worth £2,300 to one and £200,000 to the other. What separates them is entirely on the consequence side: how much better the alternative act is, in the cases where the test flips it.

**So Chapter 6's arithmetic and this chapter's are answering different questions**, and the second cannot be inferred from the first. A ratio near 1 does tell you the test is worthless — Chapter 6 said so — but a large ratio tells you nothing about worth at all.

### A test that changes nothing is not a bad test

One clarification, because the conclusion invites the wrong reflex.

**The pump test is a good test.** It is well specified, it discriminates sharply between the two mechanisms, it can be done in an afternoon, and Chapter 5 was right to identify it as the conspicuous gap in the utility's reasoning.

**Its low value is a property of the decision, not of the test.** Belief already sits far from the point at which the act changes, and the act it would change to is barely better. Move either of those and the same test becomes worth having.

**Which is why the finding cannot be generalised into a rule about tests.** *Diagnostic tests are usually not worth running* is not what this section establishes and would be a bad thing to carry away. What it establishes is that the question has an answer, the answer is computable in an afternoon, and it is frequently no.

### The ceiling, which costs one line

There is a shortcut that should be run before any of this.

> "the value of imperfect information is always less than that of perfect information." [@colyvan2016voi, p. 303]

So compute what it would be worth to know the answer **with certainty**, and you have an upper bound on every study anyone could ever propose.

With perfect knowledge: if Mechanism A, choose Act A at 130. If Mechanism B, choose Act C at 216.

`0.636 × 130 + 0.364 × 216 = 82.7 + 78.5 = ` **161.3**

Against **173.6** without.

**The value of perfect information is £12,400.**

**No study of this question, however good, can be worth more than £12,400.** Not a better-instrumented pump test. Not a three-month monitoring programme. Not a consultant's hydraulic model. The ceiling does not care what the study is, because it already assumes the study works perfectly.

**That is one line of arithmetic on a table you already have**, and it disposes of every proposal before any of them is costed. It is the cheapest thing in this chapter and almost nobody runs it.

### One more thing the ceiling tells you

The ceiling is £12,400. The proposed test costs £8,000 and is worth £2,300.

**But suppose the utility had been offered a perfect test for £5,000.**

It would be worth running — £12,400 of value for £5,000 — and it would still leave the decision in the same place two-thirds of the time, because on the recovery branch the utility does what it was going to do anyway.

**So a study can be worth running and still change nothing most of the time.** That is not a contradiction: the value is in the cases where it changes something, weighted by how often those arise and how much they are worth.

Which means the useful question about any proposed study is not *what will it tell us* but **what would we do differently, in which cases, and how often do those arise?** Three questions, all answerable from a table, and none of them about the study's methodology.

### Task: compute both

Using the anchor's figures:

1. Compute the value of the test yourself, and check it.
2. Compute the value of perfect information, and check it.
3. Then answer the question that matters: **what is the most the utility should pay for any study of this question, and what does that rule out?**
4. Finally: **would a better test help?** Suppose one existed with likelihoods of 0.99 and 0.01. Would it be worth £12,400?

Question 4 has an answer you can reason to without computing it, and it is the point of the ceiling.

## 6. When the Analysis Is Not Worth It

The chapter's brief, in the book's governed architecture, ends with **recognition of when further analysis itself is not worthwhile**.

So the chapter turns on itself.

### The analysis costs something too

> "In some cases we also need to factor in the cost of the value of information study itself. Sometimes these studies require considerable resources (additional scenario modelling and the like) and this cost should not be ignored." [@colyvan2016voi, p. 308, n. 16]

**A value-of-information study is a study.**

Everything in §5 — the branch recomputation, the probabilities of each result, the second table — took real work. On a decision worth £173,600 it was worth doing. On a decision worth £8,000 it would not be.

**Which is why the ceiling is the version to run first.** It is one multiplication and one subtraction on a table you already built. If it comes back below the cheapest study anyone has proposed, you are finished, and you have spent ten minutes.

### Why nobody runs the cheap version

If the ceiling is one line of arithmetic and it disposes of study proposals, the honest question is why it is not standard.

**It requires a decision to exist.** You cannot compute what information is worth without acts, states, consequences, and a currency — and in most organisations, by the time somebody proposes a study, none of those has been written down. The study is proposed *instead of* the decision analysis, not after it.

**It produces an unwelcome number.** A ceiling of £12,400 is a finding that the year's proposed research programme cannot pay for itself, delivered to the people who proposed it.

**And nobody owns it.** The analyst owns the study. The committee owns the decision. The question of whether the study is worth the money to the decision sits between them, which in most organisations means it sits nowhere.

**The structural fix is the same shape as Chapter 8's**: give the paper a line. *What is the most this question could be worth, and what does the study cost?* Two figures, and their absence becomes visible.

### Four limitations, worked on the anchor

The source is not selling this machinery. It devotes a section to what goes wrong.

**Framing.** "Finding the appropriate formulation of a value of information decision can be difficult and requires careful reflection on what the aim of the exercise is and to what purposes we might put the data in question." [@colyvan2016voi, p. 305]

On the anchor: the £2,300 is the value of the test **for choosing among these three acts, under this monetisation, over three years**. Change the acts and it changes. Ask instead what the test is worth for the utility's asset-planning model over twenty years and you have a different question with a different answer.

**A single currency is required.** "value of information studies typically do require a single currency for the relevant values" [@colyvan2016voi, p. 305].

**This is the sharpest one for this book.** Chapter 10 spent thirty pages establishing that the utility has three fundamental objectives that conflict. The table in §2 collapsed them into pounds, and the collapse embeds an exchange rate between households-without-water, money, and disruption that nobody has stated.

Every figure in §§3 to 5 inherits it. The £2,300 is as solid as that exchange rate and no more.

**Budgets are not fungible.** "funds not spent on data collection are not always available (in their entirety) for conservation efforts. For example, resources cannot always be (costlessly) reallocated. Sometimes funds are tied to particular data collection projects and cannot be reallocated to conservation interventions at all." [@colyvan2016voi, p. 305]

On the anchor: *do not spend £8,000 on the test, spend it on the contingency instead* assumes the £8,000 can move. Chapter 10 found that the utility's capital envelope is an annual convention with a precedent for exceptions — but an operational testing budget may not move into capital at all.

**Value can arrive later.** The source's example, reporting Møller and Fiedler (2010): amateur bird records collected purely as a hobby "are now valuable components of long-term data sets, painting a clear picture of changes in bird numbers and species over long time periods". And the warning: "It is all too easy to overlook such value in a narrowly-construed value of information study conducted at the time of data collection." [@colyvan2016voi, p. 305]

On the anchor: a pump test performed this summer is one row in a register that, in twenty years, might be the thing that identifies what actually drives pressure failures across the network. **The £2,300 does not include that**, and cannot.

### The limitation the anchor makes vivid

Of the four, the single currency is the one worth dwelling on, because this book has spent a chapter establishing why it hurts.

Chapter 10 found three fundamental objectives, and found them by asking *why is this important* until the answer stopped being another objective:

> Minimize households without adequate pressure during heat events.
> Minimize whole-life cost across the network.
> Minimize disruption to residents during works.

**Every number in §§3 to 5 required those three to become one.**

Somebody decided what a household-event is worth in pounds, and what a week of excavation outside somebody's door is worth in pounds, and those two judgments are now inside every figure in this chapter — inside the £42,400, inside the critical value of 0.283, inside the £2,300, and inside the £12,400 ceiling.

**A different exchange rate gives a different answer to all four.**

That is not a reason to abandon the machinery. It is a reason to report the exchange rate alongside the result, and to check whether the recommendation survives a defensible alternative to it. **Which is §4's sensitivity question, applied to the value judgment rather than to the probability** — and it is the version almost nobody runs, because the probability looks uncertain and the exchange rate looks like a fact.

### Three kinds of information gathering

The source draws a distinction the chapter needs:

> "(1) information gathered for pure science, for intellectual curiosity and with no intended benefits for practical decisions (2) information gathered for the purposes of improving a specific well-defined decision and (3) information gathered because it might be useful for some ill-defined or unknown decision down the track." [@colyvan2016voi, p. 306]

**Value of information applies to the second, and only to the second.**

The source defends the first: "Surely there should be a place for (1), pure scientific research, and it might be argued that such research is not an appropriate target for value of information studies" [@colyvan2016voi, p. 306].

And it names the problem:

> "The question is how to guard against cases of (3) which might slip though under the guise of (1) or gesturing towards (2)". [@colyvan2016voi, p. 306]

**Most organisational data collection is category three.** The dashboard nobody uses. The monitoring programme that has run for a decade. The annual survey. All of it gathered because it might be useful, none of it attached to a decision, and all of it defended by gesturing at a decision that has not been specified.

**The machinery cannot price category three**, because there is no decision to compute against. What it can do is ask the question that exposes it: *which decision would this change, and how?* — and note that an answer beginning "it would help us understand" is category three wearing category two's clothes.

### And not every decision needs any of this

Chapter 10 carried this and it applies again:

> "In many cases, single issue, well-defined decisions do not need a formal methodology for successful outcomes." [@bradley2016structured, p. 7]

A reader who builds a decision table before choosing a supplier for stationery has learned ceremony.

**The test is the same as the ceiling's**: what would getting this wrong cost, and is that more than the analysis? On a £310,000 capital decision affecting a thousand households, an afternoon is obviously worth it. On most things, it is obviously not.

### What the book's evidence arc actually established

Ten chapters were about evidence. This one has just shown that the most conspicuous piece of evidence in the whole case is not worth buying.

**The conclusion is not that evidence does not matter.**

It is the one Chapter 10 reached without arithmetic and this chapter reaches with it: **what matters is prior to which evidence is worth having.**

Chapter 10 quoted it as an advantage of thinking about values first — that they "help prioritize the spending of limited resources on gathering information relevant to what is important" [@bradley2016structured, p. 6]. §5 is what that looks like when you compute it.

The utility spent a year assembling five sources, and Chapter 9 found none of them transported. It never ran the pump test, and this chapter finds it would not have been worth running. **Both of those were determinable in advance**, by an afternoon's work of the kind Chapters 10 and 11 describe, and neither was done because nobody thought the value of evidence was a thing you could compute.

## 7. Cold-Start Practice and Retrieval

### Return to your six-minute answer

Find what you wrote at the start of §1.

Read it against what you can now produce. Do not score it.

- Did you name a **rule**, or only an act?
- Did you say what the choice would depend on?
- Did you propose **finding out more** before deciding?

Three patterns are common.

Most readers name an act and give reasons, without noticing that the reasons constitute a rule. That is not carelessness; it is how recommendations are made everywhere.

Many propose running the pump test first. It is the most natural response in the book — nine chapters have pointed at it — and it is the one §5 disposes of.

A few say the choice depends on how much the utility minds a bad outcome. That is the strongest opening answer and it is §3's whole subject.

### Retrieve the procedure from memory

Before looking back, write down how you would handle a decision under uncertainty with a study on offer.

Aim for the sequence, not the wording.

1. What are the **acts**? Include one that limits consequences without fixing the cause.
2. What are the **states**? What do you not control that changes which act is best?
3. What are the **consequences**, in one currency — and **what did the conversion assume**?
4. What are the **probabilities**, and where did they come from?
5. Compute the **expected value of each act**.
6. Say what using that rule assumes, and whether any act has **no spread**.
7. Find the **critical value**: where does the best act change?
8. Is your uncertainty anywhere near it?
9. Compute the **ceiling** — what would perfect knowledge be worth?
10. **Is the cheapest available study cheaper than the ceiling?** If not, stop.

Step 3's second half is the one everybody skips, and it carries more of the answer than any other number.

Step 9 takes one line and should come before any study is costed.

Step 10 is the question that ends most analyses, and it is almost never asked.

### The three sentences worth memorising

Most of this chapter reduces to three claims.

> **Using expected value is a choice, and the choice is called risk neutrality.**

> **The useful output of a sensitivity analysis is a critical value, not a range of answers.**

> **Information is worth what it changes about what you do — which is frequently nothing, however informative it is.**

Each has an operational form, which is what you do on a Tuesday.

For the first: **when you report an expected value, name the rule** — and if any act has no spread, report what removing the spread would cost.

For the second: **find the point at which the recommendation flips**, and say how far your belief is from it. Two lines.

For the third: **before costing any study, compute the ceiling.** If the most that perfect knowledge could be worth is less than the cheapest study, you are finished.

### If the transfer went badly

- **You chose an act without naming a rule.** Reread §3. The rule is the thing somebody can disagree with.
- **You treated expected value as the answer.** It is a rule whose use is a choice, and §3 says what the choice is called.
- **You said the study was worth running because it was informative.** Reread §5. The broken toe is accurate and worth nothing.
- **You computed the study's value without computing the ceiling.** The ceiling is cheaper and often ends the exercise.
- **You treated the currency conversion as neutral.** It is where the value judgment went, and §6 names it as this machinery's sharpest limitation.

### What Chapter 12 asks next

You can now lay a decision out, choose defensibly, find where the answer turns, and refuse a study.

All of it rested on one number: `p(Mechanism A) = 0.636`, from eleven investigations over eighteen years and three network configurations.

§4 showed the answer survives any reasonable range around it — which was lucky. **Sometimes it does not**, and then you face a decision whose best act depends on a probability you cannot defend.

There are two responses. Insist on a number anyway, and accept that the recommendation inherits its weakness. Or **stop optimising, and look instead for an act that performs acceptably across the whole range** — one that is not best under any particular belief and is not badly wrong under any of them.

That second response has a name and a literature, and it is a different way of thinking about the same table.

**Chapter 11 optimises. Chapter 12 hedges** — and it also asks what to do when the decision is not one choice but a sequence of them, with the chance to change your mind as things unfold.
