## Central question

Which act is defensible, and would more evidence change it?

## Core competence

Use decision trees, expected utility, risk attitudes, sensitivity analysis, value of information, decision-quality reasoning, ambiguity awareness, and recognition of when further analysis itself is not worthwhile.

## Role in the book

Chapter 11 is the chapter the book has been walking toward, and it closes the evidence thread on a negative result.

**Fourteen located promises** from Chapters 1, 5, 6, 7, and 10 come due here, and the canon entry on `expectation` does not merely defer — it instructs this chapter to make a move deliberately that Chapter 6 refused to make silently.

The last item in the governed core competence is the chapter's brief rather than an afterthought: **recognition of when further analysis itself is not worthwhile.**

And the anchor's arithmetic delivers it. The pump test — named in Chapter 2, confirmed obtainable in Chapter 5, computed in Chapter 6 as decisive in both directions — is worth **£2,300** against a cost of **£8,000**. The value of *perfect* information about which mechanism operates is **£12,400**, so no study of the question can pay for itself.

## Exercise architecture

1. **Opening attempt (§1).** Which of Chapter 10's seven would you choose, and how? Six minutes, unscored.
2. **Build the table (§2).** Acts, states, consequences.
3. **Expected values (§3).** All three; then say what using them assumes.
4. **Risk (§3).** Which act would a risk-averse committee prefer, and what does it cost?
5. **The critical value (§4).** Where does the best act change?
6. **The test (§5).** Compute its value; compute the ceiling.
7. **Planted-defect diagnosis (§6).** Five defects.
8. **Cold transfer (§7).** One assigned parallel form.
9. **Retrieval and delayed retest (§7).**

### Planted defects

| Planted defect | Collapse targeted |
|---|---|
| "The test moves belief from 64% to 91%, so we should run it" | informative means valuable |
| "Expected value says Act A, so Act A it is" | the rule is the decision |
| "We varied every input by 20% and the answer held" | sensitivity is percentage-wiggling |
| "We can't put a number on it, so we can't analyse it" | ambiguity blocks analysis |
| "The study costs £40,000 and could tell us a lot" | a study is worth what it reveals |

### Rubric dimensions

1. A decision laid out as acts, states, consequences.
2. Expected values computed, and the rule named as a choice.
3. Risk attitude identified as a preference, not an error.
4. A critical value found.
5. The value of a proposed study computed or bounded.
6. A ceiling used to dispose of a study before costing it.
7. A recommendation that names what it is conditional on.

## Transfer target

> Given a decision with two or three acts, two states, a payoff table, a prior, and a proposed study with stated reliability, compute the expected values, find where the best act changes, compute or bound the study's value, and say whether to commission it.

### Parallel forms

- **Form A — a rail operator deciding on a rail-grinding programme** (engineering).
- **Form B — a retailer deciding on a returns-fraud screening rule** (commercial).

Both supply: three acts including one with no spread; two states; a prior; a study with stated reliability whose value is small; and a ceiling that disposes of a larger proposed study.

Every prior transfer and contrast domain is excluded. Neither domain is sensitive.

Chapter 11 must not claim durable far transfer.
