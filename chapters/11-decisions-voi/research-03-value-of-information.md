# Research 03 — Value of Information

Cluster R03 of `research-plan.md`. Closed.

Source read directly: `colyvan2016voi` printed pp. 302–305.

## 1. The assumption the paper opposes

> "It is a common assumption that more and better quality data will lead to better conservation management decisions. Indeed, this assumption lies behind, and motivates, a great deal of current work in conservation biology. Of course, more data can lead to better decisions in some cases but decision-theoretic models of the value of information show that this need not always be the case: sometimes the cost of data collection is too high." [@colyvan2016voi, p. 302]

And the paper's opening line, which is the belief in its plainest form:

> "It is natural to think that more information will lead to better decision making and thus better outcomes." [@colyvan2016voi, p. 302]

**"Lies behind, and motivates, a great deal of current work."** Not a mistake somebody makes — the premise of a research programme.

**This book has spent ten chapters on that premise.** Not endorsing it — Chapters 3, 4, 6, 7, and 8 each established a term that more data does not touch — but the whole enterprise of interrogating evidence assumes evidence is worth interrogating. This chapter asks whether it was worth having.

## 2. When no further information is needed

The paper's first worked example, p. 302: a bet paying $20 on heads and costing $10 otherwise, with the probability of heads known only to lie between 0.4 and 0.6.

> "a simple sensitivity analysis of the decision model here shows that the expected utility of accepting the bet is greater than rejecting it. **No further information is required in deciding whether to accept this bet or not.**" [@colyvan2016voi, p. 302]

Then the contrast, on the same page: if the interval were 0.2 to 0.4 instead, it "straddles the critical value of 1/3", so the decision does depend on where in the interval the truth lies, and "sensitivity analysis does not help in deciding whether to accept the bet" [@colyvan2016voi, pp. 302–303].

**Two intervals, one decision, and what separates them is whether the interval crosses the point at which the best act changes.**

That is the whole idea, and it is arithmetic. It also connects directly to Chapter 8's finding that whether a wider spread raises or lowers a threshold probability depends on which side of the threshold the centre sits. **Same structure: it is the position of the uncertainty relative to a critical value that decides everything**, and in both chapters nobody checks.

## 3. The example the chapter should carry

> "some medical diagnostic tests have this character. Radiographing a suspected broken toe costs time and money (not to mention the radiation exposure to the patient), and yet the treatment for a bruised toe or a broken toe is the same: strap it and avoid any activities that hurt the toe. **The value of information delivered by the radiographic examination in this decision about treatment is zero.**" [@colyvan2016voi, p. 303]

**Zero, and the test works perfectly.** The radiograph is not inaccurate, not underpowered, not confounded. It answers its question correctly and the answer changes nothing you would do.

And immediately, the qualification that makes it usable rather than glib:

> "Of course the same information might be valuable for other purposes. For instance, typically a broken toe will take longer to mend than a bruised toe, so the radiographic examination will have some value in determining, for example, when the patient can expect to be able to kick a football again." [@colyvan2016voi, p. 303]

**Value of information is relative to a decision.** The ninth instance of the book's recurring relativity shape, and the first in Part III.

## 4. The ceiling, and the bottom line

The paper records, without developing it here, that "we can calculate the value of imperfect information and **the value of imperfect information is always less than that of perfect information**" [@colyvan2016voi, p. 303].

**That is a screening rule and it is the cheapest thing in the chapter.**

Compute what it would be worth to know the answer with certainty. No study can be worth more. If that ceiling is below the price of the cheapest study you could commission, you are finished, and you have spent an afternoon rather than a year.

The paper's own summary:

> "The bottom line is that it does not always pay to get the best information available—sometimes the price is too high." [@colyvan2016voi, p. 303]

The paper also notes that the same machinery applies "to the utility side of decision making" — where what is uncertain is the value of an outcome rather than its probability — and works an example at p. 303. **Chapter 10's objectives are exactly that kind of uncertainty**, and the chapter should note the connection in a sentence without developing it.

## 5. Four limitations, all documented

The paper is not a sales pitch and the chapter must carry its cautions.

**Framing.** "Finding the appropriate formulation of a value of information decision can be difficult and requires careful reflection on what the aim of the exercise is and to what purposes we might put the data in question." [@colyvan2016voi, p. 305]

**A single currency is required.** "value of information studies generally need to trade in a common currency—typically monetary value—but, as we are well aware, we need to deal with other kinds of value" and "value of information studies typically do require a single currency for the relevant values" [@colyvan2016voi, p. 305].

**This is the sharpest limitation for this book**, because Chapter 10 spent thirty pages establishing that decisions have several objectives that conflict. Collapsing them into one number to compute VOI undoes that, and the chapter must say so rather than let the arithmetic quietly settle a value question.

**Budgets are not fungible.** "funds not spent on data collection are not always available (in their entirety) for conservation efforts. For example, resources cannot always be (costlessly) reallocated. Sometimes funds are tied to particular data collection projects and cannot be reallocated to conservation interventions at all." [@colyvan2016voi, p. 305]

So "the money would be better spent on action" can be true and unavailable.

**Value can arrive later.** The paper's bird-watching example, reporting Møller and Fiedler (2010): amateur records collected for their own sake "are now valuable components of long-term data sets, painting a clear picture of changes in bird numbers and species over long time periods". And the warning: "It is all too easy to overlook such value in a narrowly-construed value of information study conducted at the time of data collection." [@colyvan2016voi, p. 305]

**Møller and Fiedler (2010) was not obtained**; the example is used as reported.

## 6. The worked application, as reported

> "Rhodes et al. (2011) considered the cost of monitoring studies aimed at resolving the uncertainty about birth and death rates of Koalas … It was shown that there is little value in additional monitoring; the money spent on monitoring would be better spent directly on conservation efforts: '[I]f resolving uncertainty costs more than 1.7 % of the koala management budget, it would be more cost-effective to allocate that money to direct management action now.'" [@colyvan2016voi, p. 304]

**Rhodes et al. (2011) was not obtained**; used as reported.

And the summary line the chapter should quote for its own sake:

> "Reducing ignorance is not the name of the game, it's improving management decisions." [@colyvan2016voi, p. 304]

With the operative qualifier, on the same page: "As always in value of information studies, **it depends on what you're going to do with the information**."
