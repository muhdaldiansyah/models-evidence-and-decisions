## 2. The structure

A decision under uncertainty has three components, and every treatment of the subject uses them under one name or another:

- the **acts** available;
- the **states** the world might be in, which you do not control;
- the **consequences** that follow from each act in each state.

Add probabilities over the states and you can compute; leave them out and you can still see the shape.

`bradley2016structured` p. 8's "Prospects" step — "The potential outcomes from the decisions and their uncertainties" — is this, compressed into a phrase.

**No source read for this book teaches the decision tree as a device.** It is standard material in every decision-analysis text and none of those texts was obtained. `../../decisions/0018` clause 2.2 therefore records the layout as **the book's own presentation of standard material** rather than attributing it, which is the honest position: the structure is not the book's invention and nor is it any read source's.

## 3. Why a table rather than a tree

The chapter needs to show acts against states, and there are two conventional presentations.

A **tree** branches from a decision node through chance nodes to outcomes, and it earns its keep when the decision is sequential — decide, observe, decide again. The anchor's value-of-information question *is* sequential, so the tree form is needed once, in §5, to show test-then-act against act-now.

A **table** with acts as rows and states as columns is the same information for a one-shot decision, and it is far easier to read on a page without diagram conventions.

**Recommendation for `../../decisions/0018`:** the chapter uses a table for the one-shot decision, and one inline text tree in §5 where sequence matters. Nothing else.

## 4. What the narrowing costs

Seven alternatives will not fit a table at this depth. Three will.

**The narrowing must be shown as a choice**, because it is one, and because Chapter 10 spent five pages establishing that a small option set is a claim about what matters.

The three carried forward should be chosen to make different things visible rather than to be the best three, and the manuscript should say which criterion was used. Recorded in `research-04` §2.

## 5. Where the objectives went

Chapter 10 produced three fundamental objectives. A decision table has one number per cell.

**Collapsing three objectives into one currency is a real cost and a real assumption**, and `colyvan2016voi` p. 305 states it plainly as a general limitation of this machinery — see `research-03` §5. The chapter must not present the collapse as neutral.

The honest presentation: the cell values are a **stated monetisation** of three objectives, the monetisation is a value judgment, and a different one produces a different table. Which returns the reader to Chapter 10 rather than escaping it.

## 6. What the other source supports

`../../sources/nasem2026decisionmaking.md` records that its source separates "assessing predicted consequences and uncertainty" from "deciding" as distinct steps, and treats decision framing as iterative rather than a one-directional pipeline.

**Both matter here.** The first is the acts-states-consequences structure under another name. The second warns against presenting §§2–5 as a pipeline: a table that produces an uncomfortable answer is a reason to revisit the alternatives, which is Chapter 10's step and `bradley2016structured` p. 54's "If alternatives are not achieving the objectives, it could imply that they should be reformulated."
