## Form A — rail delay attribution, and station signage

### 1. The decisions

**Problem 1.** Whether the infrastructure owner should keep accepting the automated system's first attributions. **It is the owner's decision**, about a system the owner procured and maintains, against a drift that is costing the owner money.

**Problem 2.** Whether to repaint 40 stations' signage this year at £186,000 or next year at £198,000.

**Do not credit** "whether the system is accurate" for Problem 1. Accuracy against what is the question §4 of the chapter was about, and it is not the decision.

### 2. The routing records

**Problem 1 should run to roughly ten or eleven material rows.** Chapters 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 7, 8, 13, 14, and 15 all have work to do. Chapters 6, 9, and 12 are the clearest negatives, and 10 and 11 are arguable either way.

**Problem 2 should run to about five.** Chapters 1, 4, 8, and 11 certainly; Chapter 7 arguably — see below.

**Every "not material" row needs a reason.** Count yours. **The rows you marked "not material" with no reason are indistinguishable, to any reader, from rows you forgot.**

**Two negatives worth checking in Problem 1.**

**Chapter 6.** The system outputs an attribution, not a probability. Nothing is being forecast or scored. **The temptation is to treat 88% as a probability of correctness; it is a rate of agreement with a clerk.**

**Chapter 12.** No envelope, no portfolio, no indivisible schemes. The compensation figure is large but it is a consequence, not a budget being allocated.

### 3. What the two counts mean

**Roughly ten against roughly five.**

**It does not mean Problem 1 was analysed more thoroughly.** It means Problem 1 is a system with a training set, an incentive structure, a queue, and a party that responds; Problem 2 is a spending decision with six years of data.

**A strong answer says that the second analysis is complete.** Nothing was skipped; ten rows were ruled out with reasons.

### 4. Where to start, and why

**Not Chapter 1.**

The natural start is **Chapter 4**: 2020's 19,800 incidents against 32,400 the year before. **A 39% drop, because the trains stopped running.** A model trained on this data has learned what a pandemic timetable looks like.

**Or Chapter 3**, on what an attribution is — but Chapter 4 gets you to the label faster.

**A reader who started at Chapter 1 because Chapter 1 is first has not read §4 of the chapter.** Starting there is not wrong; being unable to say why is.

### 5. The fact that changes the routing

**"The label the system was trained to predict is the attribution a clerk recorded."**

**One clause, stated once**, in the paragraph about the training data.

**What it changes.** Chapter 3's row — what an attribution score stands for — and Chapter 1's — what question is being answered. The system does not attribute delay. It predicts what a clerk would have written.

**And it changes Chapter 8's row**, because it explains why 88% and 64% are not two attempts at the same measurement.

**Also creditable:** the last line of Problem 1 — that the system was procured and is maintained by the infrastructure owner — which changes Chapter 15's row and is discussed below.

### 6. The backward revision

**The expected one:** Chapters 1 and 3 revised from Chapter 7, when the label's identity surfaces.

**Five parts, and the fifth is the one being assessed.**

*Stage revised.* Chapters 1 and 3.
*Triggered by.* The label clause, found while asking what the target quantity is.
*Previous answer.* The system attributes responsibility for delay.
*Revised answer.* The system predicts a clerk's attribution decision.
***Was the previous answer reasonable?* Yes.** It is what the system is called, what the regime calls it, and what the supplier's documentation says.

**Answers that describe the revision as a correction, an oversight, or a mistake have missed the chapter.** Nothing was done badly. The question turned out to be a different question.

### 7. Problem 2's load-bearing row

**Chapter 4.**

The two partial-repaint years happened **because the signage contractor had spare capacity at year end**. That is not random allocation; it is allocation by contractor scheduling, which may well correlate with how busy the network was, which may well correlate with complaint volume.

**Chapter 8's row matters too** — four no-repaint years with a standard deviation of 16.1 against a difference of 78 — and the difference is large relative to that spread, which is worth saying.

**But Chapter 4 is the row that decides whether the 78 means anything at all.**

### 8. Problem 2's recommendation

Either answer is defensible if its conditions are stated.

**The difference is large relative to the year-to-year spread**, unlike the chapter's charity example. **And it is confounded**, unlike a randomised test.

**£12,000 is the cost of waiting**, which is small against £186,000 and is the number the decision actually turns on.

### 9. Chapter 7 in Problem 2, which is the item most likely to be got wrong by pattern-matching

**The chapter's §6 said Chapter 7 was not material because a randomised test was available.**

**No test is available here.** The repaint is the intervention. There is no cheap way to randomise 40 stations, and the two confounded years are all the evidence there will ever be before the money is spent.

**So Chapter 7 is closer to material in this problem than in the chapter's example**, and a reader who marked it "not material" in both without noticing the difference has generalised from one case.

**This is the sharpest thing either form tests**, and getting it wrong is not a serious failure — noticing it is a strong answer.

### 10. Chapter 15, which runs backwards

**The obvious reading is wrong here and the form is built to catch it.**

The share attributed to the infrastructure owner is rising. The owner pays £46 a minute. **The owner also procured and maintains the system.**

**So the party that benefits from the drift is the train operators**, who did not build it. **The party losing money is the one that did.**

**What follows.** Chapter 15 is material, and its content is not "the system's owner gamed it". The available accounts are that the clerks whose decisions formed the label had been under pressure from operators; that operators escalate more readily than the owner does, so the panel-reviewed subset is unrepresentative; or that the drift is real and the network genuinely deteriorated — against which stands the track-fault figure, up 1.1%.

**A reader who wrote "the owner gamed the metric" has not read who pays.** Credit any answer that identifies the direction correctly, whatever mechanism it then proposes.

### 11. Unresolved, in two kinds

**Open because nobody has done the work:** what a full panel re-review would show; whether the drift is concentrated in particular incident types; what the clerks' decisions looked like before 2018.

**Open because it is somebody's decision:** whether to keep the system; what an attribution regime is for; what the owner is willing to spend to find out.

**Confusing the two is how an analysis ends up waiting for evidence that would not settle anything.**

## Form B — helpline call triage, and the newsletter

### 1. The decisions

**Problem 1.** Whether the charity should keep the model's recommendation as the escalation default. **The charity's decision.**

**Problem 2.** Whether to move the newsletter from Tuesday to Thursday.

### 2. The routing records

**Problem 1: roughly ten or eleven material rows. Problem 2: four.**

**Problem 2's four are Chapters 1, 4, 8, and 11** — and Chapter 11's is nearly trivial, because **the split test is free**. That makes Chapter 7 a clean negative for the reason the chapter's §6 gives: the assumptions it exists to interrogate are satisfied by randomisation.

### 3. What the two counts mean

As Form A. **The second analysis is complete at four rows.**

### 4. Where to start

**Chapter 4**, and the 2020 figure: **58,300 calls against 44,100 — up 32%.**

**A reader expecting a dip has pattern-matched.** Rail incidents fell when the trains stopped; helpline calls rose when people were confined at home. **The competence is asking why a year is unusual, not remembering which way 2020 went.**

**"The label the model was trained to predict is whether a shift supervisor escalated the call."**

The model predicts a supervisor's decision, not risk.

**Also creditable:** the funder's 2023 "high-risk calls handled" measure, which changes Chapter 15's row.

### 6. The backward revision

As Form A: Chapters 1 and 3 revised from Chapter 7. **Was the previous answer reasonable? Yes.**

### 7. Problem 2's load-bearing row

**Chapter 4.** The two Thursday years happened **because public holidays moved the production schedule** — not by design.

**Chapter 8 is close behind:** five Tuesdays with a standard deviation of 1.35 against a difference of 1.46. **The difference is about one standard deviation, from two observations.**

### 8. Problem 2's recommendation

**Run the split test.** It costs nothing but a fortnight, and the evidence otherwise available cannot distinguish a real effect from ordinary year-to-year variation.

**An answer that recommends moving to Thursday on the strength of the two years has not looked at the spread.**

### 9. Chapter 13, which is the row most likely to be missed

**Senior counsellors are a fixed establishment of nine.**

**The share routed to them has nearly doubled — 8.4% to 15.6% — and the establishment has not changed.**

**That is a stock with a rising inflow and a fixed servicing rate**, which is Chapter 13, and its consequence is that calls at the back of the senior queue wait longer than they would have in the volunteer queue.

**£40,660 is the visible cost and the queue is the real one.** An answer that reports only the money has costed the wrong thing.

### 10. Chapter 15, in its ordinary shape

**The funder introduced a reported "high-risk calls handled" measure in 2023. The share routed to senior counsellors has risen ever since. Safeguarding referrals, recorded by a different team under a separate procedure, are up 2.5%.**

**That is the shape Chapter 15 taught**, and unlike Form A it runs in the obvious direction.

### 11. Unresolved, in two kinds

## The trap in both forms

**Both forms reward the reader who writes less.**

Thirty routing rows across two problems is a lot of writing, and the temptation is to make both look substantial. **The second problem in each form is complete at four or five rows**, and a reader who padded it has produced a document that looks thorough and demonstrates the opposite of what the chapter is about.

## Four answers that look right and are not

**"The model is 86% accurate, which is good enough to keep using."**
86% against what. The second figure is 59% and it is against a different thing.

**"The share of escalations rose, so the model is over-triaging."**
Possibly. It could also be that the model is right and the supervisors were under-escalating. **The independent indicator is what distinguishes them**, and it did not move.

**"There is no randomised comparison, so nothing can be concluded."**
Chapter 7 taught what to do when randomisation is unavailable, and it is not "give up". It is to state what would have to be true.

**"The infrastructure owner built the system and the attributions moved in its favour."**
They moved **against** it. Read who pays.

## A note on tone

**Neither form describes anybody doing anything wrong**, and both are set in organisations doing sensible things.

The rail regime automated a slow manual process. The helpline gave volunteers a second opinion in the first ninety seconds of a difficult call. **Both are reasonable, both were procured in good faith, and both have produced a drift that nobody planned and nobody has yet explained.**

**Form B in particular concerns a service supporting people in distress**, and the form describes no call, no caller, and no clinical judgment. Every escalation in it routes a call to a **more** senior person. Nothing here is a claim about how such services should be run.
