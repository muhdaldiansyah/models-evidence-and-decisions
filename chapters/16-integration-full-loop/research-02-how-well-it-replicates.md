# Research 02: How Much of the Categorization Result Survives

## 1. The source, and its pagination

`masonsingh2016categorization` — Andrew Mason and Chandralekha Singh, "Categorization of Mechanics Problems by Students in Large Introductory Physics Courses: A Comparison with the Chi, Feltovich, and Glaser Study", arXiv:1602.08775.

**It carries no printed page numbers.** It is cited by named section — Abstract, Introduction, Conclusions.

**This is the second application of the bounded pagination exception proposed at `../../decisions/0022` clause 8**, and `../../decisions/0023` clause 6 records the dependency: **if the author declines that clause, both applications fall**, and this chapter reverts to paraphrase without locators.

## 2. What the classic study actually consisted of

`masonsingh2016categorization`, Introduction:

> "In the classic study conducted by Chi, Feltovich and Glaser [1] (here called the Chi study), eight introductory physics students in calculus-based [courses]"

**Eight.**

The most-cited paper in its journal, on which a large literature about expertise rests, compared **eight introductory students** with a comparable number of experts.

The replicating authors draw the obvious consequence, same section:

> "Due to the small sample size in the Chi study, we refrain from performing statistical analysis of the Chi data"

**Chapter 8 gave the reader everything needed to react to this properly**, and the chapter should let them: a finding from eight subjects is not thereby wrong, and it is not a basis for confident quantitative claims about anybody.

## 3. What the replication found

`masonsingh2016categorization`, Abstract:

> "Our findings, which contrast from those of Chi et al., suggest that there is a much wider distribution of expertise among introductory students."

The specifics, from the Conclusions:

> "the percentage of introductory physics students who chose 'surface-feature' categories such as 'ramp' and 'pulley' was significantly lower than the percentages reported in the Chi study"

And the sharper finding:

> "What is more surprising however is that none of the 8 introductory students in the Chi study... chose Chi's expert categories, e.g., Newton's second law, energy principles, circular motion or linear kinematics as categories at all."

In the replication, substantial minorities of introductory students chose exactly those categories — and the authors call the contrast "hard to reconcile even considering the small number of student volunteers in the Chi study."

Their conclusion:

> "Our finding suggests that since expertise plays a role in categorization, it is not appropriate to call all introductory students novices as in the Chi study."

## 4. What this does to the chapter

**It does not overturn the distinction.** Surface and structure remain two different bases for categorising, and the replicating authors use the distinction throughout — their own measure is which kind of category students chose.

**It does overturn the population claim.** "Novices sort by surface features" is not a fact about beginners. It is a description of one basis for sorting, which some beginners use and others do not, in proportions that vary by group.

**And that is better for this book than the tidy version would have been.**

The chapter is not telling readers they are novices who sort by pulleys. It is giving them a second basis for sorting and fifteen categories to sort into. **Whether they already sort that way is exactly the thing Chapter 1's preserved baseline lets each reader check for themselves**, which is a stronger arrangement than an assertion about readers in general.

## 5. Cautions

- **Physics education research**, and the book applies its distinction to a different kind of problem entirely. That widening is the book's own.
- **A conference-proceedings paper**, short, with no printed pagination.
- **The comparison is imperfect by the authors' own statement**: "full comparison is impossible without access to all of the original problems in the Chi study."
- **Do not use this source to claim the Chi finding is wrong.** It reports a different distribution in a different population, which the authors attribute partly to how the two samples were recruited.
