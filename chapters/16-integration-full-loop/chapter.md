# Chapter 16: Integration: The Full Loop on Unfamiliar Problems

## 1. Fifteen Chapters and a Problem Nobody Has Framed

Chapter 15 ended by naming the question this chapter exists for.

Fifteen chapters have supplied machinery.
Representation and mechanisms.
Measurement.
Observation processes.
Model criticism.
Probability.
Identification.
Estimation.
Synthesis.
Values.
Decisions under uncertainty.
Optimisation.
Dynamics.
Sequential control.
Strategic response.

**And at no point has anybody had to choose.**

Every chapter arrived with its subject announced, its case prepared, and its relevance guaranteed by the fact that it was the chapter you were reading.
The book chose for you, fifteen times.

### What this chapter adds

**Nothing.**

There is no new machinery in this chapter, no new technique, and no new notation.
Everything it does, it does with what you already have.

**That makes it the hardest chapter in the book to write and possibly the most useful to read**, because the competence it is about is the one nobody teaches: deciding which of the available tools a problem needs, and being able to say why the others are not needed.

### The water case leaves

Fourteen chapters have worked one municipal water utility through a heatwave, a pump, a residual, a capital programme, and a regulator.

**It does not appear here as the case.**

The reason is structural rather than sentimental.
This chapter is about triage of an **unfamiliar** problem, and you cannot demonstrate triage on a system you have spent fifteen chapters inside.
You already know what it needs, and you know because you were told, one chapter at a time.

**The water case will be referred to throughout and reworked nowhere.**

### Why an integration chapter is hard to write honestly

There is a shape that final chapters usually take, and this one is going to avoid it.

**The usual shape is the summary.** Fifteen sections, one per chapter, each recalling what was established.
It is easy to write, comfortable to read, and it teaches nothing, because a reader who has finished fifteen chapters does not need them recalled.

**The second usual shape is the congratulation.** *You can now do the following twelve things.* This book is not going to say that, and §8 explains why in more detail than you may want.

**What is left is harder and thinner.** Two problems, worked.
One of them touches most of the book and one of them touches four chapters, and the interesting work in both is deciding which.

### Before reading further

Here is a problem nobody has framed for you.

**A local authority manages 14,000 homes.** Tenants report repairs by phone and web form; a contractor attends.

**Since January 2023 an automated tool has scored each incoming report from 0 to 100 for urgency.** Reports scoring above **70** are routed as emergencies.

**The tool was trained on 96,400 repair jobs from 2018 to 2022.** The label it learned to predict is the priority code a human scheduler assigned to each job.

| Year | Jobs in the training set |
|---|---:|
| 2018 | 18,900 |
| 2019 | 19,400 |
| 2020 | **15,600** |
| 2021 | 20,800 |
| 2022 | 21,700 |

**The vendor reported two accuracy figures.** Agreement with held-out scheduler codes: **84%**.
Agreement with an independent re-inspection of 300 jobs: **61%**.

**Since deployment**, the share of reports scoring above 70 has gone **11.2%, 12.4%, 14.9%, 17.1%, 19.8%, 22.3%** across six quarters.

**Jobs completed as emergencies** were **4,180** in 2022 and **5,100** in 2024.

**Statutory hazard referrals** — generated by a separate inspection regime with no connection to the tool — were **612** in 2022 and **631** in 2024.

**The contractor is paid £148 for an emergency job and £62 for a routine one.**

**Allow about fifteen minutes and write two lists.**

**Which parts of this book does this problem need?**

**Which parts does it not need — and why not?**

The second list is the one this chapter is about.
Give a reason for every entry on it.

Do this before reading on.
Sections 3 and 4 contain the answer, and skipping forward costs you the comparison.

### What you will be able to do

Take a problem nobody has framed and produce a **routing record** — which stages are material, which are not, and a reason on every row.

Defend a negative. *Not material here* is a finding, and it is the finding this book's own architecture warns is most often skipped.

Work the material stages **in the order the problem dictates** rather than the order the book taught them.

Go back.
Twice in this chapter, something found at a late stage will invalidate something settled at an early one, and both times the earlier work was reasonable.

And say what remains unresolved — because the chapter will not resolve its own case, and that is what the end of a real analysis looks like.

## 2. Triage

The activity has a name in this book and it is **triage**: deciding, with reasons recorded, which of the available machinery a problem needs.

**It is not doing a bit of everything**, and the difference is the whole chapter.

### What is known about how people do this

There is a well-known finding here and this book could not obtain the paper.

In 1981 Chi, Feltovich and Glaser gave physics problems to experts and to students and asked them to sort the problems into groups. **That paper could not be found in any form this book could read** — three routes, all refused or empty.

What was obtained is **the first author's own one-page retrospective**, published twelve years later, which reproduces the paper's abstract:

> "The mental representation of physics problems was investigated in experts and novices. Results from sorting tasks and protocols revealed that experts initiated problem solving by abstracting physics principles, suggesting that their problem schema were organized around the principles, whereas novices approached problem solving on the basis of the problem's literal surface features." [@chi1993classic]

And her account of how it emerged:

> "It was clear that novices sorted together problems that 'looked' alike, but experts did not." [@chi1993classic]

> "This suggested that novices sorted according to the concrete objects mentioned in the problems (e.g., pulleys); but it was not clear how experts were sorting." [@chi1993classic]

### Two words

A **surface feature** is what a problem is *about*.
Its objects, its setting, its vocabulary.
Pulleys.
Housing repairs.
Water.

A **deep structure** is what a problem *needs*.
The principle or the machinery that applies to it.

**Both are categorisations.** The students in that study were not failing to sort; they were sorting on a different basis.
That distinction matters for how this chapter reads: nobody here is being told they have been doing it wrong.

### The author's own hedge

The same page contains a sentence the chapter would be wrong to leave out.
Writing twelve years after the paper, about her own central term:

> "Although this interpretation must be correct at some level, we continue to elaborate on the characterization of the experts' deep level of representation of physics concepts." [@chi1993classic]

And when she generalises the result, the parenthesis is hers:

> "The basic expert-novice result, that experts' knowledge is represented at a 'deep' level (however one characterizes 'deep'), while novices' knowledge is represented at a more concrete level, has been replicated in many domains, ranging from knowledge possessed by scientists to taxi drivers." [@chi1993classic]

**"However one characterizes 'deep'."** The author is offering a generalisation with its central term marked as unsettled, and a chapter that presented deep structure as a solved concept would be overreading its own source.

### And the study was eight students

A 2016 paper set out to repeat the sorting task with introductory physics classes, and its first useful contribution is a fact about the original.

> "In the classic study conducted by Chi, Feltovich and Glaser [1] (here called the Chi study), eight introductory physics students in calculus-based [courses]" [@masonsingh2016categorization, Introduction]

**Eight.**

The replicating authors draw the consequence themselves:

> "Due to the small sample size in the Chi study, we refrain from performing statistical analysis of the Chi data" [@masonsingh2016categorization, Introduction]

**Chapter 8 gave you everything you need to react to this properly.** A finding from eight subjects is not thereby wrong.
It is not a basis for confident quantitative claims about anybody, and the people repeating the study say so.

### What the repetition found

> "Our findings, which contrast from those of Chi et al., suggest that there is a much wider distribution of expertise among introductory students." [@masonsingh2016categorization, Abstract]

> "the percentage of introductory physics students who chose 'surface-feature' categories such as 'ramp' and 'pulley' was significantly lower than the percentages reported in the Chi study" [@masonsingh2016categorization, Conclusions]

And the conclusion:

> "Our finding suggests that since expertise plays a role in categorization, it is not appropriate to call all introductory students novices as in the Chi study." [@masonsingh2016categorization, Conclusions]

### What survives, and why the surviving version is better

**The distinction survives.** Surface and structure are two different bases for sorting a problem, and the repeating authors use the distinction throughout — their own measurement is *which kind of category students chose*.

**The population claim does not.** "Novices sort by surface features" is not a fact about beginners.

**And this is better for you than the tidy version would have been.**

The tidy version says: you are a novice, you sort by pulleys, here is how experts do it.
That version is not supported, and it would be an odd thing to say to someone who has read fifteen chapters.

**What is supported is narrower and more useful.** There is more than one basis on which to sort an unfamiliar problem.
This book has given you fifteen categories that sort by structure. **Whether you already sort that way is something you can check** — Chapter 1 preserved your first unaided attempt for exactly this purpose, and §8 is where you look at it.

### One number the chapter will not use

The retrospective also records that the 1981 paper "has been cited in more than 375 publications, making it the most-cited paper published in this journal" [@chi1993classic].

**That is a fact about influence, and this chapter does not use it as evidence.**

Chapter 9 established that agreement among dependent sources is cheap.
Three hundred and seventy-five citations of one paper are not three hundred and seventy-five findings; they are one finding, cited.

### The deep structures in this book are its chapters

Here is the application, and it is the book's own rather than the source's.

**When you meet an unfamiliar problem, the categories available to you are the fifteen chapters you have read.**

*This is a problem about what a number stands for* is Chapter 3. *This is a problem about why these records exist* is Chapter 4. *This is a problem about whether the available evidence could establish the claim* is Chapter 7. *This is a problem about people responding to a measure* is Chapter 15.

**The surface features are the housing, the water, the reserve procurement, the gritting.** They are what the problem is about, and they are usually what you are told first.

### The negative finding

Now the part that this book's own architecture singles out.

Chapter 1's specification lists the major category errors the book exists to prevent, and one of them is:

> Mechanically applying every stage to every problem

with the remedy assigned here: *require relevance justification and allow explicit "not material here" findings*.

**`materiality` is the term.** A stage is material to a problem if working it would change what you conclude or do.

**And a "not material here" finding is a finding.** It has to carry its reason, because a negative without a reason is indistinguishable from an omission — and the two are very different things to hand somebody.

### The routing record

The written output of triage is a **routing record**: one row per stage, a judgment, and a reason.

**The reasons on the negative rows are the interesting part.** Anybody can write down the chapters they used.
What an omission cannot produce is a line saying *Chapter 12 is not material here because there is no binding envelope and nothing indivisible to choose between* — and that line is what tells a reader, or a successor, that the question was asked.

**A routing record is not a plan, an order, or a commitment.** It is revisable, and §5 revises this chapter's twice.

### Pause: mark your own

Go back to what you wrote in §1.

**Mark every entry on your "not needed" list that has a reason attached.**

Count them.
Two minutes.

## 3. A Tool That Scores Repairs

Here is the problem again, with everything the authority knows.

### The situation

**14,000 homes.** Tenants report repairs; a contractor attends.
Since January 2023 an automated tool scores each report **0 to 100**, and **above 70 is routed as an emergency**.

**Trained on 96,400 jobs from 2018 to 2022**, on a label that is **the priority code a human scheduler assigned**.

**2020's 15,600 jobs is 20% below 2019's 19,400.** Fewer repairs were reported and attended during the pandemic.
The number of homes needing repair did not fall by a fifth.

**Two accuracy figures. 84% against held-out scheduler codes. 61% against an independent re-inspection of 300 jobs.**

**The board minute records the first and not the second.** Hold that; §4 returns to it.

**Share of reports scored above 70, by quarter since deployment: 11.2, 12.4, 14.9, 17.1, 19.8, 22.3.** Roughly doubled in six quarters.

**Emergency jobs: 4,180 in 2022, 5,100 in 2024 — up 22%.**

**Statutory hazard referrals: 612 in 2022, 631 in 2024 — up 3.1%.**

**The contractor is paid £148 for an emergency job and £62 for a routine one.**

### One thing to notice about the framing

The problem above was stated in about three hundred words, and the statement contains a choice.

**It says the tool scores reports for urgency.** That is what the authority calls it, what the field is named, and what the vendor's documentation says.

**Nothing in the statement says what the tool was trained to predict**, except one clause that is easy to read past: the label is the priority code a human scheduler assigned.

**That clause is in the problem statement because it is a fact the authority has**, and it is buried because that is where such facts live. **Section 4 is largely about what happens when somebody stops to read it.**

### What is odd about this, before any machinery

Three things are strange here and none of them requires a chapter to notice.

**The count that is supposed to measure emergencies has nearly doubled, and the count generated by a different regime has not.**

**Two accuracy figures were reported and they are 23 points apart.**

**And nothing about the housing changed.** The tool was installed; the homes were the same homes.

**Noticing those three takes no training**, and this chapter is not going to pretend otherwise.
What takes training is knowing which questions each one opens, and that is what the routing record is for.

### Eleven of fourteen, and that is unusual

**Say this plainly before going further: most problems do not look like this.**

This case was chosen as the substantial one **because** it touches most of the book.
That makes it a good stress test and a bad model of a typical morning.
Section 6 has a problem that needs four chapters, and four is the commoner shape.

**A reader who finishes this chapter believing that thorough analysis means eleven rows has taken the wrong lesson from the more interesting case.**

### How to read the table

Two things about its form, because the form is the instrument.

**Every row has a judgment and a reason, and the reasons are checkable.** Somebody can disagree with any line and say why — *Chapter 12 is material, because the authority's repairs budget is fixed and the emergency rate consumes it* would be a real objection, and the table is what makes it possible to raise.

**And the reasons are in the problem's words, not the book's.** *No binding envelope, no indivisible schemes* is a Chapter 12 sentence; *the tool sees six fields and a paragraph* is a housing sentence. **A routing record written entirely in the book's vocabulary has not been applied to anything.**

### The three negatives, which took longer than the positives

**Chapter 6 — probability and simulation.
Not material.**

The tool outputs a score, not a probability.
Nobody is forecasting anything, nobody is updating a belief in light of evidence, and no forecast is being scored against outcomes.
Chapter 6's machinery — conditioning, the odds update, calibration, Monte Carlo — has nothing here to attach to.

**This one is worth dwelling on because the temptation is real.** A number between 0 and 100 that sorts reports by urgency *looks* like a probability, and a reader who treats it as one will start asking whether it is calibrated.
It is not a probability and there is nothing it would be calibrated against.

**Chapter 9 — combining and transporting evidence.
Not material.**

There is one body of evidence: the authority's own job records.
Nothing to combine, no weighting rule to choose, no second source to disagree with the first.

**Transport is the closer call**, and it is worth naming why it still fails.
The tool was trained on 2018–2022 and is applied to 2023–2024, which sounds like a transport question.
But Chapter 9's transport is about carrying a result to a *population* it did not come from, and the question here — whether the relationship still holds — belongs to Chapter 15 and Chapter 8, both of which are on the list.

**Chapter 12 — optimisation and robustness.
Not material.**

There is no capital envelope, no set of indivisible schemes, and no portfolio to select.
The authority is deciding whether to keep using a tool, not how to allocate a budget across competing projects.

**Notice how much longer these three took than most of the eleven.** That is normal, and it is why negatives get skipped: they are more work and they produce nothing to show.

### What the table is not

**Not a plan.** Nothing here says what order to work in, and §4 does not use the book's order.

**Not a commitment.** Two of these rows change in §5.

**And not a claim about artificial intelligence.** This book's architecture is explicit that AI is an application and a stress test rather than a foundation, and the table is the demonstration: **every row names an ordinary chapter.** There is no row for the tool being automated.
What is automated here is a scheduling judgment, and every question that raises is a question this book has already asked about judgments made by people.

## 4. Working It: What Turned Out to Matter

The routing record says which stages are material.
It does not say what order to work them in, and the book's order is a teaching order.

**This case starts at Chapter 4.**

### Chapter 4 — why do these records exist?

Two findings, and the second is the one that matters.

**The 2020 volume is an artefact.** 15,600 jobs against 19,400 the year before, because reporting and attendance collapsed during the pandemic — not because 3,800 fewer repairs were needed. **A model trained on this data has learned, in part, what a lockdown looks like.**

**And the label is a decision, not a fact.** The tool was trained to predict the priority code a human scheduler assigned.
Not whether the repair was urgent. **What a scheduler wrote down.**

That second finding is going to cost the analysis two earlier stages, and §5 is where it does.

### Chapter 3 — what does 70 stand for?

Given the label, the honest answer is: **70 is the point above which the model believes a scheduler would have coded this job as high priority.**

That is a coherent quantity.
It is not the quantity the routing rule is named after, and it is not what anybody in the building thinks it is.

### Chapter 8 — two numbers about different things

**84% agreement with held-out scheduler codes.**

**61% agreement with an independent re-inspection.**

**These are not two attempts to measure the same thing.** The first measures how well the model reproduces a human scheduling decision.
The second measures how well it corresponds to a physical assessment of the property.

**The gap between them is the gap between the label and the thing**, and it is 23 points.

**The board minute records the 84%.** Not because anybody hid the other figure — it is in the vendor's report — but because 84% is the number that answers the question the board asked, which was whether the tool works.

### Pause: which number would you check?

**Emergency jobs are up 22%.
Statutory hazard referrals are up 3.1%.**

You have both.
Which do you check next, and what would you be checking it for?

Three minutes, before reading on.

### Why the order started at Chapter 4

It is worth saying why this case does not start where the book does.

**Because the first thing that is odd about it is a number in the training data.** A 20% drop in one year, in a series that is otherwise steady, is the kind of thing Chapter 4 taught you to ask about — and asking about it takes ten minutes and changes what the rest of the analysis is about.

**Starting at Chapter 1 would not have been wrong.** It would have produced a careful statement of the decision — *should we keep using the tool to route repairs* — which is correct, useful, and would have survived until §5 revised it.

**The general point is that the order is a property of the problem.** On Problem B in §6, the work starts at Chapter 4 as well, but for a completely different reason.
On the water case it started at Chapter 1 because Chapter 1 was the chapter.

**There is no rule here and the chapter is not going to invent one.** What there is: start where the problem is odd, and expect to move.

### Chapter 5 — the rival explanation

The authority's own account is that the tool has found emergencies that were previously being missed.

**That is a real possibility and it has a testable consequence**: if genuine emergencies were being missed before, the independent hazard-referral count should have been rising too, because the homes were dangerous whether or not the repair was coded as urgent.

**It rose 3.1%.**

That does not settle it.
It does mean the authority's account requires the extra 920 emergency jobs to be concentrated in homes that were never dangerous enough to attract a statutory referral — which is possible, and is a claim somebody should have to make out loud.

### Chapter 13 — the queue is a stock

Emergency jobs go into a queue with a **fixed servicing capacity**: the contractor has so many crews.

**Raising the share of reports that enter that queue does not raise the capacity.** It lengthens the queue, and the jobs at the back of an emergency queue are being attended later than they would have been in a routine queue that was shorter.

**Chapter 13's lesson applies directly**: a stock with an inflow that has doubled and an unchanged outflow is not a system that has improved.

### Chapter 15 — and now the payment

**£148 for an emergency job. £62 for a routine one.**

**The share of reports scored above 70 has gone from 11.2% to 22.3% in six quarters.**

The tool's scores depend on the text of the report, and the report is taken over the phone by a call handler who works for the contractor.

**Nobody has to have done anything improper for this to matter.** Chapter 15's four mechanisms are all available here, and only one of them requires anybody to decide anything.

**920 extra emergency jobs at £86 of difference is £79,120**, which is the size of the thing that would have to be explained.

### Chapter 7 — what would settle it

The identification question here is narrow and worth stating precisely, because it is where Revision 1 comes from.

**The claim the authority wants to make is: routing by score gets urgent repairs done faster than routing by scheduler did.**

**What would establish it?** A comparison between repairs routed by the tool and repairs routed by a scheduler, on jobs that were comparable in the state of the property — which is exactly the quantity nobody measures.

**And here Chapter 7's positivity question bites.** Since January 2023 every report has gone through the tool.
There is no contemporaneous group routed the other way, so the comparison would have to be against 2022 — a different year, a different contractor establishment, and a different set of homes by two years of ageing.

**The re-inspection is the closest available thing**, and it does not answer this question.
It answers a narrower one: how often the score corresponds to a physical assessment. **That is worth knowing and it is not the claim.**

### Chapters 10 and 11, briskly

**Whose objective?** The tenant wants the repair done.
The authority wants its statutory duties met at a defensible cost.
The contractor is paid per job at two rates. **These are three objectives and the tool serves one of them by construction** — it was trained on scheduler codes, and the scheduler works for the authority.

**Is a larger re-inspection worth commissioning?** The 300-job re-inspection cost something and produced the 61%.
A larger one would narrow that figure. **Chapter 11's ceiling applies**: the decision it informs is whether to continue a routing rule that moves roughly £79,000 a year of contractor payment, so a re-inspection cannot be screened out on cost.
That is not the same as saying it should be commissioned.

### Chapter 2 — what the tool can see

One row of the routing record has not been used yet, and it is the one that explains several of the others.

**The tool sees a free-text report and six structured fields.** Property reference, tenure type, reported category, date, channel, and whether the tenant has reported before.

**It does not see the property.** Not its age, not its condition survey, not whether the same fault has been reported by neighbours, not what the last engineer wrote.

**Chapter 2's question — what is inside the representation, at what grain — has an answer here that nobody wrote down.** The tool's representation of a home is seven things, one of which is a paragraph typed by a call handler during a phone call.

**And that is why the label mattered so much.** A representation this thin cannot distinguish a dangerous fault from an alarming description of a minor one.
What it can do is reproduce the judgment of somebody who was also working from seven things, which is exactly what it was trained to do and exactly what it does at 84%.

### Chapter 14 — and this is a policy

The authority is not making a decision.
It is running a **rule**, thousands of times, and the rule keys on a score.

**And the state the rule needs is not observable from what the authority measures.** Whether a report describes a genuine emergency, and whether a high score reflects that or reflects how the report was written, produce the same record: a number above 70.

**Chapter 14's question — can the instruments determine the state? — has the answer no**, and the re-inspection is exactly the instrument that would change that.

### Where this stops

The case is not resolved and §7 says so.

## 5. Two Times It Sent Us Backwards

Twice in §4 something found late invalidated something settled early.
Both times the earlier work was reasonable. **That is the ordinary condition of doing this, and this book's own architecture warns about pretending otherwise.**

### What it felt like at the time

Neither revision announced itself.

**At the identification stage, the question was ordinary**: what is the target quantity here — what is the thing the tool is trying to get right? That question has a routine answer in most projects, and the routine answer was going to be *urgency*.

**It became a revision because somebody looked at what the training label actually was**, which is a two-minute check that is skipped constantly because it feels like a formality.

**At the strategic stage the same thing happened.** The question was whether anybody responds to the score.
The routine answer is *no, it is an internal routing tool*.
It became a revision when somebody noticed who takes the phone call and who gets paid what.

**Both revisions came from checks that look like box-ticking.** That is not a coincidence, and it is worth carrying: the stages most likely to send you backwards are the ones whose questions have obvious answers.

### The term

**Backward revision** is revising a completed stage because a later stage produced evidence that changes it.

**It is not model criticism.** Chapter 5 examined what four chapters had built, deliberately, as an activity in its own right.
This is different: nobody set out to revisit Chapter 1's question, and the revision happened because the identification work turned up a fact.

**And it is evidence-triggered, not scheduled.** A revision performed because the method says to revise at this point is not a revision; it is a step.

### Revision 1 — the target was not what we said

**Trigger:** the two accuracy figures. 84% against scheduler codes and 61% against re-inspection.

**What it revised:** Chapter 1's question and Chapter 3's answer, both settled early.

Chapter 1's question — what is being asked — had been answered as *how urgent is this repair*.
It is *how would a scheduler have coded this*.

Chapter 3's — what does the number stand for — had been answered as *urgency*.
It stands for *predicted scheduler code*.

**Both answers were reasonable when they were given.** The tool is called an urgency scorer, the field is called urgency, and everybody in the building calls it urgency. **Nothing available at the framing stage would have revealed it.** The label's identity became visible only when somebody asked what the target quantity was, which is a Chapter 7 question.

**This is a revision about what the target was.**

### Revision 2 — the answer had gone stale

**Trigger:** the payment differential and the rising share of high scores.

**What it revised:** the Chapter 8 work.

The uncertainty around the tool's performance was computed on post-deployment data. **But the share of reports scored above 70 nearly doubled during that period.** The data being summarised came from a process that was changing while it was being measured.

**The interval is not wrong.** It is about a period, not about a system, and it will not describe next year.

**This is a revision about an answer having gone stale**, and it is a different kind of thing from the first.
Revision 1 said: you answered a different question from the one you thought.
Revision 2 said: you answered the right question, and the answer has a shelf life nobody wrote on it.

### Why revisions are so rarely recorded

Both of these revisions will be invisible in the finished document unless somebody chooses to write them down, and mostly nobody does.

**The finished analysis reads better without them.** *The tool predicts a scheduling decision rather than a need* is a clean finding. *We initially thought it predicted urgency, and revised that at the identification stage* is the same finding with a confession attached.

**And the confession is not one.** Nothing was done badly.
But it reads as one, and the person writing the document knows it will.

**So the revision gets absorbed.** The final version says what was concluded, and the route disappears — which means the next person to work on this system starts from the conclusion without knowing which parts of it were hard-won and which were assumed.

**There is a second cost, and it is worse.** A reader who cannot see that Chapter 1's answer was revised has no reason to think it might need revising again. **The record of a revision is also a warning about where the soft ground is**, and deleting it removes the map.

**This is the same argument Chapter 4 made about records** and Chapter 5 made about assumptions, arriving now about your own working.
The tidy document is the expensive one.

### What this book's own architecture says about it

Chapter 1's specification lists, among the book's major category errors:

> Treating the teaching order as a real-world waterfall

with the remedy assigned to this chapter and the next: *worked demonstration of two distinct backward revisions*.

**That is what §4 and §5 are.** Not an illustration of the possibility of revision, but two of them, distinct in kind, in a case where each was triggered by something the work turned up.

**The book taught fifteen chapters in an order.
The order was for teaching.** This case was worked starting at Chapter 4, revisited Chapter 1 from Chapter 7, and revisited Chapter 8 from Chapter 15.

### What a recorded revision looks like

The revisions are worth writing down in a particular form, because the point of recording them is to be useful to somebody who was not there.

**Revision 1, as it would appear in the file:**

> *Stage revised:* Chapter 1 (decision and question), Chapter 3 (what the score stands for).
> *Triggered by:* the 84% / 61% pair, found while working the identification question.
> *Previous answer:* the tool scores how urgent a repair is.
> *Revised answer:* the tool predicts the priority code a scheduler would have assigned.
> *Status of the previous answer:* reasonable on the information available at the time; not an error.

**Revision 2:**

> *Stage revised:* Chapter 8 (uncertainty in the tool's performance).
> *Triggered by:* the payment differential and the rising share of high scores, found while working the strategic question.
> *Previous answer:* an interval around post-deployment performance.
> *Revised answer:* that interval describes a period during which the process was changing, and does not describe the system going forward.
> *Status of the previous answer:* correctly computed; wrongly scoped.

**Five lines each, and the fifth line is the one that matters.** Without it, a reader of the finished analysis sees two corrections and concludes that somebody was careless.
With it, they see what this chapter is about.

### And the discipline that keeps it honest

**Backward revision does not mean the earlier work was wrong.**

Chapter 1's answer was a correct answer to the question anybody would have asked.
Chapter 8's interval was correctly computed from the data available.

**What changed was what the question turned out to be, and what the data turned out to be about.** Recording the revision — what was revised, what triggered it, and what the earlier answer was — is what stops a reader of the finished analysis from thinking it went in a straight line.

## 6. A Problem That Needs Four Chapters

Here is a smaller problem.

**A charity mails an annual fundraising appeal.
It is deciding whether to move it from October to November.**

| Year | Month | Names mailed | Response rate |
|---|---|---:|---:|
| 2016 | Oct | 62,400 | 3.4% |
| 2017 | Oct | 64,100 | 3.1% |
| 2018 | Oct | 66,800 | 3.6% |
| 2019 | **Nov** | 71,200 | **3.9%** |
| 2020 | Oct | 69,500 | 4.2% |
| 2021 | Oct | 72,900 | 3.3% |
| 2022 | **Nov** | 78,400 | **4.1%** |
| 2023 | Oct | 81,600 | 3.5% |

**October: six years, mean 3.52%, standard deviation 0.34.**
**November: two years, mean 4.00%.**
**Difference: +0.48 points, or 13.7%.**

Next year's list is **90,000 names** and the mean gift is **£27.40**, so the difference is worth about **£11,800**. **A split test costs £4,800.**

### Pause: how many chapters?

**Write down a number before reading on.** How many of the fifteen does this problem need?

One minute.

### Four

| Chapter | Material? | Because |
|---|---|---|
| 1 Decisions and questions | **yes** | The decision is which month to mail — narrower than how to raise more money |
| 2 Representation | no | No model is being built |
| 3 Measurement | no | Response rate is well defined and directly counted |
| 4 Observation process | **yes** | **Why do the two November years exist?** |
| 5 Assumptions and rival models | no | No model to criticise |
| 6 Probability and simulation | no | Nothing being represented or scored as a probability |
| 7 Targets and identification | no | **A randomised split test is available** |
| 8 Estimation and uncertainty | **yes** | Two observations against a six-year spread of 0.34 |
| 9 Combining and transporting | no | One source |
| 10 Values and alternatives | no | The objective is agreed and uncontested |
| 11 Decisions under uncertainty | **yes** | Is the £4,800 split test worth running? |
| 12 Optimisation and robustness | no | No portfolio, no envelope |
| 13 Dynamics and feedback | no | No stock, no delay that matters at this horizon |
| 14 Sequential decisions and control | no | Annual, but not a policy in Chapter 14's sense |
| 15 Strategic interaction | no | Donors do not know the mailing date is being varied |

### Chapter 4's row is most of the finding

**Why do the two November years exist?**

**Because of production delays.** Not because anybody decided to test November.
The mailing was late twice, for reasons internal to the print process.

**So the two November years are not a random sample of years.** And they are also **the second and fourth largest lists of the eight**, which means whatever else distinguishes them, it is not only the month.

**That is Chapter 4's question — why do these records exist and not others — and on this problem it is most of the analysis.**

### Chapter 8's row

Six October observations with a standard deviation of **0.34**, and a difference of **0.48** based on **two** November observations.

**The difference is about one and a half times the year-to-year spread, from two data points.** Chapter 8 gave you the vocabulary: this is a number with an interval so wide that both "November is better" and "there is no month effect" sit comfortably inside it.

### Chapter 11's row

**£4,800 to resolve a question worth about £11,800** — roughly 40%.

That is a genuine decision, and it is Chapter 11's shape exactly: what would the information be worth, and does it clear the cost.

### Chapter 7's row, which is the interesting negative

**Chapter 7 is not material here, and the reason is not that causation does not matter.**

It is that **a randomised split test is available**.
Half the list in October, half in November, allocated at random.
The assumptions Chapter 7 exists to interrogate — exchangeability, positivity, consistency — are satisfied by construction.

**Chapter 7 was thirty-eight pages about what to do when you cannot randomise.** When you can, its machinery is not needed, and saying so is a Chapter 7 competence rather than a failure to use one.

### Four of fifteen, and the analysis is complete

**Nothing has been skipped.** Eleven rows carry a reason, and the reasons are checkable — somebody can disagree with any of them and say why.

**This is the commoner shape.** Section 3's eleven-row case is the unusual one, and it was chosen because it is unusual.

### Why this problem is the common one

It is worth being explicit about why §6 exists at all, because a chapter could have stopped after §5 with a rich case and a satisfied reader.

**Most problems are Problem B.**

Somebody has a decision, a small amount of data that bears on it, and a question about whether to spend money finding out more. **No model is being built.** No causal claim is being made.
Nothing is accumulating, nobody is responding to a metric, and the objective is not in dispute.

**On a problem like that, the value of having read fifteen chapters is almost entirely negative.** It is the eleven rows you can rule out quickly and defensibly, which leaves you working on the four that matter with the time you did not spend on the rest.

**That is an unglamorous claim and it is the honest one.** The book did not make you able to do eleven new things to this charity's problem.
It made you able to say, in a sentence each, why eleven of them would have been a waste of everybody's afternoon.

### The eleven negatives, in one paragraph each is too much

A note on proportion, because §3 gave three negatives a page and this section has eleven.

**Not every negative deserves the same treatment.** *Chapter 12 is not material, there is no portfolio* is a complete reason and does not need elaborating. *Chapter 7 is not material because a randomised test is available* needed a paragraph, because the reason is interesting and because a reader might otherwise think causation was being ignored.

**The rule is proportionality to how easily somebody could disagree.** A negative that nobody would question gets a clause.
A negative that a competent reader might challenge gets the space to defend itself.

**And the test for which is which is whether you can imagine the objection.** If you cannot, one line.
If you can, answer it.

## 7. What Is Still Unresolved

Problem A is not resolved, and this chapter is not going to resolve it.

**What is open.** Whether the authority should keep routing with the tool.
What a larger re-inspection would show.
Whether the contractor's payment structure should change, and what that would cost.
Whether the tool could be retrained on a better label, and whether such a label exists — somebody would have to define what an urgent repair is, and Chapter 3 established how much work that sentence hides.

**What is settled.** That the tool predicts a scheduling decision rather than a need.
That the 84% and the 61% are about different things.
That the emergency queue has an inflow which has doubled and a capacity which has not.
And that £79,120 a year of contractor payment turns on a number nobody has audited.

**That is what the end of a real analysis looks like.** Four things established, five open, and a recommendation that would have to be argued for rather than computed.
A chapter that tied it off would be teaching you that these problems close.

**And notice what the four settled things have in common.** Not one of them required a technique this book invented.
They required somebody to ask what the label was, what two numbers were about, what happens to a queue whose inflow doubles, and who gets paid what.

**The five open ones are open for two different reasons**, and the distinction is worth carrying out of the chapter.
Two of them are open because nobody has done the work — the larger re-inspection, the contract review. **Three are open because they are somebody's decision and not a finding**: whether to keep the tool, what an urgent repair is, and what the authority is willing to pay to find out.

**Confusing those two kinds of openness is how an analysis ends up waiting for evidence that would not settle anything.**
