# Research 01: How People Categorise Unfamiliar Problems

Cluster 1 of four. Every locator below was taken from reading the document directly.

## 1. What could not be obtained

The finding this chapter's §2 rests on comes from **Chi, Feltovich and Glaser (1981), "Categorization and representation of physics problems by experts and novices", *Cognitive Science* 5: 121–152.**

**That paper could not be obtained.** Three routes were attempted: the publisher's full text (403), a university mirror (403), and a Defense Technical Information Center report version, which returned a PDF containing no extractable text.

**What was obtained is the first author's own retrospective**, published twelve years later: Michelene T. H. Chi, "Experts' vs. Novices' Knowledge", *Current Contents* "This Week's Citation Classic", CC/NUMBER 42, 18 October 1993.

**It is one page.** It reproduces the 1981 abstract verbatim and adds Chi's account of how the finding emerged. **Because it is a single page, no page locator is needed** — the document is the locator — so this is not an application of `../../decisions/0022` clause 8's pagination exception.

**It is still a retrospective commentary rather than the study**, and the manuscript says so where it uses it.

## 2. The finding, in the paper's own abstract

`chi1993classic` reproduces the 1981 abstract:

> "The mental representation of physics problems was investigated in experts and novices. Results from sorting tasks and protocols revealed that experts initiated problem solving by abstracting physics principles, suggesting that their problem schema were organized around the principles, whereas novices approached problem solving on the basis of the problem's literal surface features."

**Two phrases carry the chapter.**

**"Abstracting physics principles."** The expert's first move is to decide what kind of problem this is, and the kind is defined by the principle that applies.

**"The problem's literal surface features."** The novice's first move is also a categorisation. It is not that novices fail to categorise; it is that they categorise on what the problem is *about* rather than on what it *needs*.

## 3. Chi's own account of how it emerged

`chi1993classic`, in the retrospective:

> "It was clear that novices sorted together problems that 'looked' alike, but experts did not."

> "This suggested that novices sorted according to the concrete objects mentioned in the problems (e.g., pulleys); but it was not clear how experts were sorting."

And the conclusion they reached:

> "We concluded that experts mentally represented and directed their problem solving by the 'deep' principles embodied in the problems."

**Note the hedge in the same paragraph**, which the manuscript should carry:

> "Although this interpretation must be correct at some level, we continue to elaborate on the characterization of the experts' deep level of representation of physics concepts."

**The author is saying, twelve years later, that the characterisation is still being worked out.** A chapter presenting deep structure as a settled thing would be overreading its own source.

## 4. The claim to generality, and its limits

`chi1993classic`:

> "The basic expert-novice result, that experts' knowledge is represented at a 'deep' level (however one characterizes 'deep'), while novices' knowledge is represented at a more concrete level, has been replicated in many domains, ranging from knowledge possessed by scientists to taxi drivers."

**"However one characterizes 'deep'" is the author's own parenthesis** and it is doing real work. The generalisation is offered with the central term acknowledged as unsettled.

The same page records that the paper "has been cited in more than 375 publications, making it the most-cited paper published in this journal."

**That citation count is a fact about influence and not about evidence**, and the manuscript should be careful with it — Chapter 9 taught that agreement among dependent sources is cheap, and 375 citations of one paper are not 375 findings.

## 5. What the chapter takes, and how it is framed

The chapter uses this as a **description of what triage is**, not as a claim about the reader.

**The useful form is:** the first thing anybody does with an unfamiliar problem is decide what kind of problem it is, and there is more than one basis on which to do that. Surface — what it is about. Structure — what it needs.

**The chapter is a set of instructions for the second**, and its fifteen chapters are the categories.

## 6. What was not taken

- Anything about physics.
- The protocol-analysis methodology.
- Any claim about how expertise is acquired, which the source does not address.
- The citation count as evidence of anything but influence.
- The Simon and Simon forward/backward-reasoning result, which the retrospective mentions and the book has not read.
