# Research 02 — How a Formulation Fails, and What Would Show It

Status: bounded research dossier. Evidence for author adjudication; **not** an author decision.

Cluster R02 of `research-plan.md` §5. Research conducted 2026-08-18.

Sources: `platt1964strong` (primary, pp. 347–348 read), `levins1966strategy`, `sterman2002models`.

## 1. Q1–Q2 — The method

There is an established method, it is short, and it maps almost exactly onto Chapter 5's governed competence.

`platt1964strong` p. 347:

> "Strong inference consists of applying the following steps to every problem in science, formally and explicitly and regularly: 1) Devising alternative hypotheses; 2) Devising a crucial experiment (or several of them), with alternative possible outcomes, each of which will, as nearly as possible, exclude one or more of the hypotheses; 3) Carrying out the experiment so as to get a clean result; 1′) Recycling the procedure, making subhypotheses or sequential hypotheses to refine the possibilities that remain; and so on."

Three steps and a loop. Note what each does.

**Step 1 is rival models.** Chapter 5's competence names them; Platt makes them the starting move rather than an afterthought.

**Step 2 is the chapter's central question in operational form.** *What would show it?* becomes: what observation has alternative possible outcomes, at least one of which excludes something?

**Step 3 is the part nobody does.**

The paper is explicit that the schema is a tree: "It is like climbing a tree. At the first fork, we choose—or in this case, 'nature' or the experimental outcome chooses—to go to the right branch or the left" (p. 347). It attributes the structure to Bacon, calling it a "conditional inductive tree".

### The line the chapter should quote

`platt1964strong` p. 347:

> "Any conclusion that is not an exclusion is insecure and must be rechecked."

That is Chapter 5's thesis in nine words. A check that could not have come out the other way has established nothing.

And it connects backwards. Chapter 3 taught that an interpretation which could never have been contradicted has not been supported. Chapter 4 taught that the surviving data is exactly the subset the process kept. Platt gives the general form.

## 2. Q3 — The artifact a critic produces

This is where the paper is more useful than its reputation suggests, because it does not stop at exhortation.

`platt1964strong` p. 348 records two concrete practices from working scientists:

- Lederberg is credited with giving "a list of nine propositions 'subject to denial,' discussing which ones would be 'most vulnerable to experimental test'";
- Jacob and Monod are quoted writing: "Our conclusions … might be invalid if … (i) … (ii) … or (iii)…. We shall describe experiments which eliminate these alternatives."

**That second quotation is a template.** It is exactly the artifact Chapter 5 should ask readers to produce:

> Our conclusion might be invalid if (i), (ii), or (iii). Here is what would eliminate each.

The paper also diagnoses why people do not produce it, at p. 348: "How many of us write down our alternatives and crucial experiments every day, focusing on the *exclusion* of a hypothesis? … in between, we do busywork. We become 'method-oriented' rather than 'problem-oriented.'"

That is a better account of why criticism fails in practice than "people are careless". People are busy doing legitimate-looking work that could not have changed their answer.

## 3. Q4 — Adapting the method where no experiment is possible

**This is the chapter's main honesty problem, and the adaptation is the book's own.**

Platt assumes crucial experiments are available. Molecular biology can run one. A water utility cannot rerun a drought, a city cannot un-resurface a road, and a food bank cannot randomise who gets a referral.

Two things survive the adaptation and one does not.

**Survives: devising alternatives.** Nothing about step 1 requires an experiment.

**Survives: naming what would discriminate.** You can always ask what observation would have alternative outcomes, at least one of which excludes something — even when you cannot make that observation.

**Does not survive: getting a clean result.** Often the discriminating observation is unobtainable, and the honest finding is that it is unobtainable.

That last case is not a failure of the method. It is a result, and an important one: *this conclusion rests on something we have not excluded and cannot currently exclude.* A reader who can produce that sentence is doing better than one who produces a confident answer.

Chapter 2's Mechanism A and Mechanism B are the book's own worked instance. Both remain alive after three chapters, and the discriminating observation — running the duty pump at elevated output through a hot afternoon — was named in Chapter 2 and never made.

**The adaptation must be labelled as the book's own** per `canon/pedagogy.md`. Platt does not license it; he does not discuss it.

## 4. Q5 — Boundaries

### Against Chapter 7 — identification

Rival models in Chapter 5 are **instruments of criticism**. The question is whether the analysis survives them, not which is true. Chapter 7 owns what evidence could establish a causal claim.

The tell: Chapter 5 is content to leave two rivals alive and say so. Chapter 7 wants to know which one holds.

### Against Chapter 11 — value of information

Chapter 5 **names** the discriminating observation. Chapter 11 asks whether it is **worth acquiring**.

That line is clean and worth stating to the reader, because "we should find out" and "finding out is worth what it costs" are different claims and readers merge them.

### Against Chapter 12 — robustness

`levins1966strategy` p. 423 supplies the multiple-models version of the same idea: treat one problem with several models with different simplifications, and a result surviving across them is "a robust theorem which is relatively free of the details of the model. Hence our truth is the intersection of independent lies."

Chapter 2 already uses this. Chapter 5 may use it as a criticism technique — build a differently-simplified representation and see whether the conclusion survives — and must leave formal robustness, regret, and adaptive planning to Chapter 12. `robustness` is already registered with a Chapter 12 home.

### Against abdication

`sterman2002models` p. 505 is worth pairing with Platt, because "all models are wrong" is routinely used to end criticism rather than to start it. The article's own move is the opposite: recognising the limits of our mental models is a reason to "expand the boundaries" and "take responsibility", not to stop judging.

## 5. Cautions — claims the manuscript must NOT make

1. Do not present strong inference as uncontested. It is influential and has been debated; that literature was **not read** and must not be characterized.
2. Do not attribute the no-experiment adaptation to `platt1964strong`. It is the book's own.
3. Do not import the paper's disciplinary polemic about fast and slow fields, or the reported dispute among named scientists.
4. Do not cite `platt1964strong` beyond p. 348.
5. Do not present rival models as options to choose between. Leaving both alive is a legitimate outcome.
6. Do not use `levins1966strategy` p. 423 as general epistemology; its own source note limits it to robust theorems across models sharing a common assumption.
7. Do not teach value of information, robustness formalism, or identification.

## 6. Verdict on the stop condition

`research-plan.md` §5 requires the method stated in reader-facing steps, sourced, with the adaptation marked as the book's own.

**Met.** Proposed reader-facing form:

1. Write down the alternatives — what else could be true?
2. For each, name the observation that would exclude it.
3. Make the observation, if you can.
4. If you cannot, say so, and say what your conclusion is resting on.

Steps 1–3 are `platt1964strong` p. 347. **Step 4 is the book's own addition** and must be labelled.

## 7. Unresolved author decisions

1. Are the steps given as a numbered method, risking checklist behaviour?
2. Is the Jacob–Monod sentence used as an explicit template for the reader's artifact?
3. Is "any conclusion that is not an exclusion is insecure" quoted, or paraphrased?
4. How prominently is step 4 marked as the book's own — a footnote, or in the sentence?

Decision 5 is the one worth thinking about. It is honest and it models the behaviour the chapter teaches; it also leaves Part I ending on an unresolved question, which some readers will find unsatisfying and which is arguably the point.
