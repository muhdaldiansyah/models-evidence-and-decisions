## Form A — the grid operator

### 1. The totals

| Rule | Worst margin (MW) | Hours below standard | Unused reserve (MWh) | Extra procured (MWh) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| R0 | 150 | 30 | 0 | 0 |
| R1 | 210 | 11 | 660 | 5,050 |
| **R2** | **560** | **0** | 1,050 | 5,800 |
| **R3** | 470 | 1 | **300** | **4,200** |

Worst margin is a **minimum** across the five winters — the lowest the margin ever got. The other three columns are sums.

**This item takes six minutes and is not the point of the exercise.** If it took you twenty, check what you spent the time on.

### 2. The dominated rule

**R1 is dominated by R3.**

Higher worst margin — 470 against 210.
Fewer hours below the standard — 1 against 11.
Less unused reserve — 300 against 660.
Less extra procured — 4,200 against 5,050.

**Better on all four.** Not a preference, not a trade-off.

**And now the sentence the item actually asks for.**

The operator's failure is **not that it wrote a bad rule**. R1 watches the right quantity, acts in proportion, and has a stand-down. Somebody thought about it.

**The failure is that it never compared R1 with anything.** For eleven years the rule was applied, reviewed, and re-approved without any alternative ever being placed beside it — and a rule that has never been compared is a rule somebody wrote, not a rule anybody chose.

**Do not credit** an answer that concludes the operator was incompetent, or that blames the analyst, or that treats eleven years of use as evidence the rule was good. **Eleven years of use is evidence the rule is survivable**, which is a much weaker claim, and the difference only becomes visible when something is put next to it.

### 3. The winters that teach nothing

**Two of five: mild and long cold.**

On the mild winter all four rules produce identical results. On the long cold winter R2 and R3 are identical — 620 / 0 / 0 / 1,150 in both columns.

**So three winters in five carry information about the choice between R2 and R3, and the operator gets one winter a year.**

A strong answer draws the consequence: settling this by running a rule and watching is a decade-scale project, conducted by an organisation whose staff turn over faster than that — and the informative winters arrive on the weather's schedule, not the operator's.

**Also credit** the observation that the operator cannot run R2 and R3 side by side, because there is one system.

### 4. The undecidable remainder

**R2 and R3 do not dominate each other.**

R2 never breaches the security standard and pays for 1,050 MWh of unused reserve. R3 breaches it for one hour and pays for 300.

**So the question is what one hour below the security standard is worth against 750 MWh of unused reserve**, and the table prices neither.

**That is a question about what the operator values, and it is not an arithmetic question.** An answer that picks one and says why is fine. An answer that picks one and presents it as what the numbers show has missed the item.

### 5. Observability

**No. The four instruments cannot separate the two causes.**

**Metered demand** shows demand, and demand is high in both cases — in the second because the shortfall must be met from somewhere.
**Declared availability** is a submission, not a measurement. A generator that over-declares reports the same figure as one that can deliver.
**System frequency** tightens under either cause.
**Settlement data** arrives six weeks later, which is not a rule input.

**Two different situations, one record.**

**And now what the item asks for — what follows for every rule in the table.**

All four rules fire on a margin or an error signal that is present under both causes. **On the second cause, procuring more reserve buys capacity from the same fleet that has over-declared**, so the response may deliver nothing while being paid for. The rule cannot distinguish the case in which it works from the case in which it does not.

**Do not credit** an answer that identifies the ambiguity and stops. Identifying it is half the item.

**Strong answers add** that this is not a data-quality complaint: no amount of care with declared availability separates the two, because declared availability is not a measurement of output.

### 6. The model

**No. Five more winters would not separate baseline from embedded generation shortfall — and neither would five hundred.**

**And the reason is in the model, not in the data.** The two parameters enter only through their sum. Any increase in one is exactly cancelled by a decrease in the other, and the model's predictions do not move. There is nothing to observe, because the candidate worlds produce identical outputs.

**This was knowable on the day the model was written.** Write down the model, write down what is measured, look for parameters appearing only in combination — four steps, ten minutes, no data required.

**Answers reasoning about sample size, measurement error, or statistical power have missed the item**, and this is the single most useful check in the chapter to have understood. If you argued that more winters would narrow the estimate, reread the three splits: they do not fit approximately equally well, they fit **exactly** equally well.

### 7. Which is fixable

**Item 5's problem is fixable, by a different instrument.** Nothing about the situation is inherently unobservable; the operator simply has the wrong sensors. Measure output directly and the two causes separate.

**Item 6's problem is not fixable by more of the same data.** It is structural. It yields only to a measurement of a different kind — one that observes embedded generation on its own — or to changing the model.

**And the alternative to collecting more data is to model less**: drop the distinction, report a single combined figure, and **write down that you have done so**.

**Credit an answer that notes the second option is almost never proposed.** It produces a shorter model and an admission rather than a project, and organisations reach for the instrument nearly every time.

**Do not credit** an answer that treats both problems as the same kind, in either direction.

### 8. The instrument

**It resolves both.** Per-unit output telemetry replaces declared availability with measurement, separating item 5's two causes; and it meters embedded generation directly, splitting item 6's 31,400.

**£240,000 against a £5,100,000 decision is 4.7%.**

**What follows:** the instrument **cannot be screened out on cost**. Almost any chance of the information changing a five-million-pound decision clears a quarter-million-pound bar.

**What does not follow:** that it is worth buying. A ceiling argument rules things out; it does not rule them in.

**And the reason you cannot compute what it is worth is that nobody will supply a probability over the three splits.** Inventing one converts a stated ignorance into a number that will be quoted without its provenance.

**A strong answer says all three of those things.** An answer that assigns probabilities to the splits and computes an expected value has done arithmetic the form does not support — and the arithmetic is not wrong, the inputs are absent.

**Also credit** an answer that adds the running cost: £240,000 is the installed price, and somebody has to read the feed, maintain it, and be authorised to act on it. An instrument nobody watches provides no information at all.

## Form B — the highways authority

### 1. The totals

| Rule | Worst ice-hours | Nights above threshold | Wasted runs | Salt (t) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| G0 | 480 | 30 | 0 | 0 |
| G1 | 290 | 11 | 33 | 3,920 |
| **G2** | **110** | **0** | 53 | 4,500 |
| **G3** | 130 | 1 | **16** | **3,250** |

**Worst ice-hours is a maximum**, because for this measure lower is better. If you took a minimum you have reported the best winter as though it were the worst, and the ranking inverts.

### 2. The dominated rule

**G1 is dominated by G3.**

Fewer worst-case ice-hours — 130 against 290.
Fewer nights above the threshold — 1 against 11.
Fewer wasted runs — 16 against 33.
Less salt — 3,250 against 3,920.

**The failure is not writing G1. It is never comparing it.** Eleven years of use is evidence the rule is survivable, not that it is good.

### 3. The winters that teach nothing

**Two of five: mild and long frost.** On the mild winter all four rules agree; on the long frost winter G2 and G3 are identical at 110 / 0 / 0 / 890.

**Three winters in five discriminate, and the authority gets one winter a year.**

### 4. The undecidable remainder

**One night above the service threshold, against 37 wasted runs and 1,250 tonnes of salt.**

The table prices neither, and it is not an arithmetic question.

**Credit an answer that notices the environmental dimension** — 1,250 tonnes of salt entering watercourses is a cost the table records in tonnes and does not price at all.

### 5. Observability

**No. The records cannot separate "no ice" from "ice nobody drove on."**

The authority's outcome measure is **incident reports**, and an icy road with no traffic produces none. Surface temperature at four sites does not cover the network. Runs and salt record what the authority did, not what happened.

**What follows for every rule:** all four are being evaluated against a measure that is **partly a measure of traffic**. A rule that leaves quiet rural roads untreated will look better than it is, and a rule that treats them will look worse.

**Strong answers go further and notice that this corrupts the table itself.** Three of the four columns are recorded quantities; "ice-affected road-hours" is a modelled estimate, and item 6 shows the model's parameters are not separately determinable. **The table is less solid than its tidiness suggests**, and saying so is the best available answer to this form.

### 6. The model

**No, and the reason is the model.**

Base exposure and residual salt protection enter only through their **difference**. Any increase in one is exactly cancelled by an increase in the other. The three splits fit **exactly** equally well, not approximately.

**If you were looking for two things added together and did not spot a difference**, that is worth noting: the pattern is *parameters appearing only in combination*, and a sum is only the commonest case.

### 7. Which is fixable

**Item 5's is fixable with different instruments** — measure surface state rather than infer from incidents.

**Item 6's is not fixable by more winters.** It yields to a measurement of residual salt directly, or to dropping the distinction.

**The alternative is to model less**: report a single combined term and write down that you have.

### 8. The instrument

**£96,000 against a £2,100,000 decision is 4.6%. It cannot be screened out on cost, and that is not the same as being worth buying.**

**No expected value can be computed**, because nobody will supply a probability over the splits.

**The instrument resolves both:** surface state separates ice-that-nobody-drove-on from no-ice, and residual salt concentration splits the 46.

## The trap in both forms

**The arithmetic is six minutes and the reasoning is forty.**

If you spent the session computing, the forms did to you what the table does to the organisations in them: it looks like the analysis, and it is the part that was already done.

**Every question that mattered was about what the numbers could not settle** — which comparisons the histories do not discriminate, which states the instruments do not separate, which parameters no data will pull apart, and what an instrument is worth when there is no probability to weigh it with.

## Four answers that look right and are not

**"The rule has been in force for eleven years and performed acceptably, so it is a reasonable rule."**
Eleven years of use is evidence of survivability. The alternative that beats it on every measure was never run, so the record contains nothing about it.

**"We should collect more data to resolve the parameter question."**
Not this one. The parameters enter only in combination; more of the same data produces the same exact tie.

**"We should run the alternative rule next winter and see."**
You may, and two winters in five will tell you nothing, and you get one winter a year.

**"The instrument costs 4.7% of the decision, so it is clearly worth buying."**
A ceiling says it cannot be dismissed on price. Turning that into a recommendation is the one inference a ceiling does not license.

## A note on tone

Both forms describe organisations that have been running a rule for eleven years without ever comparing it with an alternative.

**That is extremely common and it is not stupidity.** Nobody is assigned to ask *compared to what?* about a procedure that is working. The rule appears in the plan, gets reviewed against itself, and is re-approved. The comparison in item 1 takes six minutes and nobody's job description contains it.

**An answer that locates the failure in the people has explained the wrong thing.**
