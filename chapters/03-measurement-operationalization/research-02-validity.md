# Research 02 — Validity: What It Is a Property Of

Status: bounded research dossier. Evidence for author adjudication; **not** an author decision.

Cluster R02 of `research-plan.md` §5. Research conducted 2026-08-18.

Sources inspected: `adcock2001validity` (pp. 529–532), `jcgm2012vim`.

## 1. Q1 — What validity is predicated of

### The definition

`adcock2001validity` p. 530: "Valid measurement is achieved when scores (including the results of qualitative classification) meaningfully capture the ideas contained in the corresponding concept."

Sharpened at p. 531: "measurement is valid when the scores (level 4 …), derived from a given indicator (level 3), can meaningfully be interpreted in terms of the systematized concept (level 2) that the indicator seeks to operationalize."

And decisively, in the same passage: "the appropriate focus of measurement validation is on the **conjunction of these components**."

### What this rules out

Validity is **not** a property of the instrument.

An instrument on its own has no validity, because validity is a relation among three things — an indicator, the scores it produces, and the systematized concept those scores are being interpreted in terms of. Remove the concept and the question of validity does not arise.

p. 531 states the same point from the other side: "Scores are never examined in isolation; rather, they are interpreted and given meaning in relation to the systematized concept."

### The structural echo

This is the third time the book meets the same shape, and each time it is established rather than invented.

| Chapter | The thing that is not self-standing | What it is relative to |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | adequacy of an answer | stated intended use |
| 2 | content of a representation; a mechanism | purpose; a specified phenomenon |
| 3 | validity of a measurement | the interpretation being placed on the scores |

Chapter 3 should make this continuity visible, because a reader who has seen it three times has learned the book's central habit rather than three unrelated facts.

## 2. Q2 — What follows for the reader

Three consequences, all supportable.

**A device cannot be "validated" once and for all.** What gets supported by evidence is an interpretation of its scores for a purpose.

**The question "is this measure valid?" is malformed.** The answerable question is "are these scores interpretable as this concept, for this use?"

**Validity assessment is empirical work, not certification.** p. 532: interpretations of scores are "falsifiable claims" that "require supporting evidence. Validity assessment is the search for this evidence."

That last sentence gives Chapter 3 the same epistemic move Chapter 2 made about mechanisms — a drawing is a hypothesis; an interpretation of a score is a hypothesis — and gives the reader something to do rather than a caution to remember.

## 3. Q3 — Contextual specificity

`adcock2001validity` p. 530 introduces it as "an issue that arises when a measure that is valid in one context is invalid in another", and describes the article's response as seeking a middle ground between "a universalizing tendency, which is inattentive to contextual differences, and a particularizing approach, which is skeptical about the feasibility of constructing measures that transcend specific contexts."

### How far it extends — and the boundary risk

This is genuinely Chapter 3 material and it is adjacent to Chapter 9's external validity. The proposed split:

- **Chapter 3** — this indicator, these scores, this concept, in the setting where they are being used. A pressure sensor calibrated for one zone may not support the same interpretation in another.
- **Chapter 9** — moving *evidence and findings* between populations and settings.

The line is that Chapter 3 asks whether the number means what you think **here**; Chapter 9 asks whether what you learned **here** applies **there**.

## 4. Q4 — The proliferation of validity adjectives

`adcock2001validity` p. 530 reports finding **37 different adjectives** attached to "validity" in discussions of conceptualization and measurement, and notes that the same procedure is treated as evidence for different named validities by different authors.

Their resolution is the useful part, and it is the one Chapter 3 should adopt: the procedures supply "different *types of evidence for validity*", not "multiple independent *types of validity*". They also separate **validity** (the overall idea) from **validation** (the procedures for assessing it).

**Implication.** Chapter 3 should teach *one* validity and several kinds of evidence for it. Teaching a taxonomy of validities at core depth would install exactly the confusion the source documents.

### Caution

Do not extend "37 adjectives" into a claim that the terminology is meaningless or that the distinctions are worthless. The article's complaint is about proliferation and inconsistent usage, not about the underlying ideas.

## 5. Q5 — Boundaries

### Against Chapter 5 — model validation

**A genuine terminology hazard.** `asme2025credibility` and `fda2023credibility` are already in the bibliography and use `validation` for assessing whether a computational model is adequate for a context of use. `adcock2001validity` uses `validation` for assessing evidence that scores support an interpretation.

These are different practices that share a word. Chapter 3 must say so explicitly at first use, or readers who go on to Chapter 5 will merge them.

### Against Chapter 7 — causal inference

`adcock2001validity` p. 529 does the boundary work itself: measurement validity is distinct from the validity of causal inference, and from Cook and Campbell's internal and external validity — "Although measurement validity is interconnected with causal inference, it stands as an important methodological topic in its own right."

That sentence is quotable and settles the boundary from within the source.

### Against Chapter 10 — metrics

`metric` is already registered in canon with a Chapter 10 home. A metric can be examined as a measurement in Chapter 3; whether it is the right thing to optimize is Chapter 10.

### Against Chapter 15 — gaming

Chapter 3 may observe that people can act on a measure. Goodhart-type failure is Chapter 15 and must not be taught here.

## 6. Cautions — claims the manuscript must NOT make

1. Do not say an instrument "is valid". Say what the scores support, and for what.
2. Do not present validity as certification, or as something a device carries.
3. Do not teach a taxonomy of validities.
4. Do not merge measurement validation with model validation. Name the collision.
5. Do not attribute the falsifiable-claims formulation to Adcock and Collier alone; it is credited to Messick (1989), unread.
6. Do not extend contextual specificity into transportability. Chapter 9 owns that.
7. Do not claim this account is the consensus of all measurement traditions. It is a well-argued proposal from the social-science methodology literature, consistent with the psychometric reconceptualization it cites.

## 7. Verdict on the stop condition

`research-plan.md` §5 requires a reader-facing sentence stating what validity is predicated of, defensible from a verified locator.

**Met.** Proposed sentence, traceable to p. 531:

> A measurement is valid when the scores it produces can meaningfully be interpreted as the concept you set out to measure — so validity belongs to the interpretation, not to the instrument.
