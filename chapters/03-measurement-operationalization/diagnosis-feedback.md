# Chapter 3 — Diagnosis Feedback

Open this file only **after** you have written your diagnosis of all five defects in §6, and after you have placed the four boundary items.

Your wording will differ; what matters is whether you found the same fault.

## 1. "The monitoring-point sensor is validated, so the adequacy figure is reliable"

**Defect.** Two errors compounded in nine words.

First, validity is being predicated of the **instrument**. It is not a property an instrument can carry; it holds of an interpretation of scores in relation to a construct, for a use.

Second, *reliable* is being used to mean *correct*. Reliability is consistency on repetition, and the sentence offers a certificate about the device as though it settled whether the number means what the reader will take it to mean.

**What it costs.** The sentence licenses exactly the conclusion the chapter's arithmetic refutes. The sensor is well made, correctly reports pressure at 62 metres of elevation, and the household at 84 metres receives 3 metres of head at peak. Everything in the sentence is true and the conclusion is wrong.

**Repair.** "The sensor reports pressure at the monitoring point within its stated limits. Whether those readings can be interpreted as service adequacy for Hillcrest has not been assessed, and the elevation range of the zone gives reason to doubt it."

**Note.** A calibration certificate travels with the device. The interpretation does not.

## 2. "Accuracy: ±0.4%"

**Defect.** Accuracy is being quoted as a number. The international vocabulary is explicit that measurement accuracy "is not a quantity and is not given a numerical quantity value".

**What it costs.** Whatever the figure describes — most likely precision, possibly a manufacturing tolerance — it is being read as closeness to the true value. A buyer comparing two sensors on their quoted "accuracy" is comparing how tightly each clusters, not whether either clusters in the right place.

**Repair.** Ask the manufacturer what the figure was measured against, and under what conditions. If the answer is agreement among repeated readings, it is a precision figure and says nothing about offset. Then ask separately what is known about bias.

**Note.** This is not a trap unique to careless vendors. Precision is the one of the three that *can* be quoted, so it is the one that appears on paper. The reportable quantity is not the one you care about.

## 3. Averaging more dashboard readings

**Defect.** A systematic offset is being treated as random error.

**What it costs.** Every dashboard reading is about 0.9 ML high, because the transmitter reads high. Thirty readings averaged give a tighter estimate of a number that is 0.9 ML wrong — and the tightness will be reported as confidence, making the situation worse than doing nothing.

**Repair.** Use a **different observation path**. That is what the independent tank-level check was, and it is why it worked. Against systematic error the remedy is not more of the same measurement but a route to the quantity that does not share the suspect procedure's assumptions.

**Note.** If you marked this as "reasonable but slow", look again. It is not inefficient; it is ineffective, and its output looks like reassurance.

## 4. "Adequate pressure is what the monitoring point records"

**Defect.** The measure is being treated as the definition of the construct — rung three swallowing rung two.

**What it costs.** If this were true, the measurement could never be wrong; it could only be different. The utility could not have discovered the 3-metre reading at the top of the zone, because by this definition there is nothing to discover. Interpretations of scores are claims requiring evidence, and a stipulation is not a claim.

**Repair.** Keep the rungs apart. The construct is service pressure experienced by customers. The working definition is a specific formulation adopted for this analysis. The measure is the procedure. Each can be criticised against the one above it.

**Note.** This defect is comfortable, which is what makes it common. It ends arguments.

## 5. The 95% and the 91% in one column

**Defect.** Two scores produced under different working definitions are being compared as though commensurable.

**What it costs.** The neighbour's figure uses a **15-metre** threshold measured at **midday**. Ours uses **20 metres** and the failing count is computed at **evening peak**. Lower bar, easier conditions. The comparison invites the conclusion that the neighbour performs better, and the data cannot support any comparison at all.

**Repair.** Publish the working definition beside every figure, or do not publish the figure. If a comparison is genuinely wanted, both utilities must compute under one agreed definition — and the case does not supply enough to convert between them.

**Note.** The neighbour is not misreporting. Their definition may be entirely appropriate for a flat service area. The failure is in the comparison, not in either measurement. If your diagnosis accused the neighbour, reread it.

## The boundary placements

| Item | Placement | Why |
|---|---|---|
| The pump-station sensor reads 0.15 bar high | **Chapter 3** | The record exists; the number in it is off |
| Pressure sensors exist only at pump stations | **Chapter 4** | Nothing is wrong with the readings that exist; the question is which records came to exist |
| "Adequate" defined as 20 m at the monitoring point | **Chapter 3** | An operationalization choice — rung two |
| The monitoring point was sited where a technician could park | **Genuinely mixed** | See below |

The fourth item is both at once, and it was given to you unresolved on purpose.

Read one way, it is a fact about the **measurement procedure**: where you site an instrument is part of operationalizing a working definition, which is Chapter 3.

Read the other way, it is a fact about **which records came to exist**: a van's access decided what the utility would ever have data about, which is Chapter 4.

Both readings are correct, and the item does not resolve. If you placed it confidently on one side, go back and construct the other reading — being able to see why a case sits on a boundary is a more useful skill than placing it.
