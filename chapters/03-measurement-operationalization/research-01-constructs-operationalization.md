## 1. Q1–Q2 — The sequence from concept to number

### What the source supplies

`adcock2001validity` p. 530 sets out four levels, and they are the cleanest available account for a cross-disciplinary book because the article's explicit purpose is a standard shared by qualitative and quantitative work.

| Level | Name | What it is |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Background concept** | "the constellation of potentially diverse meanings associated with a given concept" |
| 2 | **Systematized concept** | "the specific formulation of a concept adopted by a particular researcher or group of researchers", usually as an explicit definition |
| 3 | **Indicators** | "also routinely called measures"; "any systematic scoring procedure", including classification procedures used in qualitative research |
| 4 | **Scores for cases** | "both numerical scores and the results of qualitative classification" |

The distinction between levels 1 and 2 is the one a reader is least likely to have thought about and the one that does the most work.

*Adequate water pressure* as an idea, with everything anyone might mean by it, is a background concept. *Pressure at the highest connected property, at or above 20 metres of head, during the evening peak* is a systematized concept: a specific formulation someone chose.

The choice at level 2 is where most measurement arguments actually live, and it is usually invisible because it is made silently.

### The tasks between levels

p. 530 names the downward tasks:

- **conceptualization** — background concept to systematized concept;
- **operationalization** — systematized concept to indicators;
- **scoring cases** — applying indicators to produce scores.

And, crucially, the **upward** tasks: refining indicators, modifying the systematized concept, and revisiting the background concept.

p. 530 also fixes the scope of the word `measurement` itself: "'measurement' involves the interaction among levels 2 to 4."

That is a useful boundary. Arguing about what the background concept really means is not measurement — it is conceptual dispute, and the article separates the two deliberately.

## 2. Q3 — What `operationalization` names

Precisely: the move from a systematized concept to one or more indicators.

Not "turning a vague idea into a number" — that conflates two steps. The vagueness is removed at level 2, by adopting a specific formulation. Operationalization then develops a procedure that produces scores for that formulation.

A reader who skips level 2 operationalizes a background concept directly, which means their procedure carries an unexamined choice about what the concept means.

## 3. Q4 — Is choosing a procedure the same as defining the concept?

**No, and the source structure makes the answer visible.**

If the procedure *were* the definition, levels 2 and 3 would collapse and the upward task "modifying the systematized concept in light of insights about scores and indicators" would be incoherent — there would be nothing above the indicator to modify.

The article also treats the interpretation of scores as a **claim** rather than a stipulation: p. 532, "it is essential to view the interpretations of scores in relation to systematized concepts as falsifiable claims (Messick 1989, 13–4). Scholars should treat these claims just as they would any casual hypothesis, that is, as tentative statements that require supporting evidence."

A stipulation cannot be falsified. A claim can. The article is unambiguous that this is a claim.

**Implication for Chapter 3.** The manuscript can say directly: choosing how to measure something does not settle what it is. If it did, no measurement could ever be wrong — it could only be different.

### Caution

The position that the procedure *does* define the concept is a historical one (operationism), and it is largely abandoned. Chapter 3 should state the modern position and may note that the older one existed, but **must not** characterize the operationist literature, which was not inspected.

## 4. Q5 — Does the sequence revise upward?

Yes, and this matters for how the chapter is written.

p. 530's figure shows three upward tasks. p. 532 quotes Kaplan's paradox of conceptualization — "Proper concepts are needed to formulate a good theory, but we need a good theory to arrive at the proper concepts. … The paradox is resolved by a process of approximation."

So the ladder is not a procedure executed once. This is the same iterative structure the book's Reasoning Loop already asserts, and Chapter 3 gets it here as established practice rather than as the book's pedagogical claim.

## 5. Where terminology is field-specific

**`indicator` versus `measure`.** `adcock2001validity` p. 530 treats them as synonyms — indicators are "also routinely called measures". The book has already registered `measure` in `canon/terminology.md`. Adopting `indicator` as a second controlled term would spend budget for no gain; keeping `measure` and noting `indicator` as the equivalent term in the literature is cheaper.

**`measurand`.** The metrology tradition has its own term for the quantity intended to be measured (`jcgm2012vim` §2.3), with the warning that the quantity actually measured can differ from it. This is the same structural point as the level 2 / level 3 gap, arrived at independently, but the vocabularies do not translate cleanly: a measurand is a *quantity*, whereas a systematized concept need not be quantitative at all.

**`score`.** Available and unambiguous at level 4. Not currently registered.

**`construct`.** Already registered in canon. Its Chapter 3 sense must be reconciled with the existing entry — see §8.

## 6. Where the two traditions genuinely diverge

The article's level structure assumes concepts whose meaning is contestable and must be formulated. Metrology assumes quantities with reference standards and, at least in principle, true values.

For *usable storage in a tank*, there is a defensible true value and a calibration path to it. For *adequate service pressure*, there is not — the threshold is chosen, not discovered.

**Chapter 3 must contain both kinds and must not present either as the general case.** This is the chapter's central sourcing risk.

## 7. Cautions — claims the manuscript must NOT make

1. Do not present the four levels as a one-directional procedure. The source's own figure and Kaplan's paradox both refuse this.
2. Do not attribute the falsifiable-claims point to `adcock2001validity` alone; they attribute it to Messick (1989), which was not read.
3. Do not import the article's political-science examples.
4. Do not present `measurand` and `systematized concept` as translations of each other.
5. Do not characterize operationism; it was not researched.
6. Do not claim the ladder is universal across disciplines. It is one well-argued cross-disciplinary proposal, offered for political science and explicitly extended by its authors to qualitative work.

## 8. Unresolved author decisions

1. `measure` or `indicator` as the controlled term at level 3?
2. Are all four levels named for the reader, or are levels 1–2 taught as one move with the distinction made in prose?
3. Is `measurand` reader-facing or signposted as metrology vocabulary?
4. Is `score` registered, or used as ordinary language?
5. How is the existing `construct` entry reconciled with level 1 / level 2 — is a construct the background concept, the systematized concept, or ambiguous between them?

Decision 5 matters most. The existing canon entry was written for Chapter 1's purposes, and Chapter 3 is where the term has to become precise.
