## 1. The replacement of one standard by another

This is the chapter's pivot and the source states it in two sentences.

> "What criterion should decisionmakers use to compare alternative solutions for long-term policy challenges? **Traditional decision analysis seeks the optimal strategy, that is, the one that performs best for a fixed set of assumptions about the future.** In contrast, LTPA requires a standard that allows analysts to make policy arguments true across a multiplicity of unpredictable futures." [@lempert2003shaping, p. 52]

**"For a fixed set of assumptions about the future."** That is Chapter 11, described from outside, and the description is fair: Chapter 11 needed a probability of 0.636 and everything followed from it.

And the alternative framing, from the chapter opening:

> "Rather than first predicting the future in order to act on it, decisionmakers may now gain a systematic understanding of their best near-term options for shaping a long-term future **in the absence of any reliable predictions**." [@lempert2003shaping, p. 39]

## 2. Robustness, defined

> "An argument that is insensitive to significant variation in its underlying assumptions is said to be robust. In this sense, **a strategy should be considered robust if it performs reasonably well compared to the alternatives across a wide range of plausible futures.**" [@lempert2003shaping, p. 52]

And the clause that is easy to skip and should not be:

> "The use of the normative concept of performing 'well' therefore also suggests robustness should be assessed with respect to the **many different value systems** for assessing the performance of the strategies." [@lempert2003shaping, pp. 52–53]

**That clause repairs something Chapter 11 had to break.**

Chapter 11 collapsed Chapter 10's three fundamental objectives into a single currency, because value-of-information machinery requires one — `colyvan2016voi` p. 305, recorded in Chapter 11 as its sharpest limitation.

**Robustness does not require the collapse.** It asks whether a strategy performs acceptably across futures *and* across ways of judging performance, which is Chapter 10's plural objectives surviving into Part III's arithmetic rather than being flattened by it.

The source also gives the operational form, from p. 40:

> "They then look for robust near-term policy options—i.e., those that when compared to the alternatives perform reasonably well across a wide range of futures using many different values to assess performance." [@lempert2003shaping, p. 40]

## 3. Why the criterion is defensible

> "For many decisionmakers, robustness represents the normative criterion one ought to use when confronting deep uncertainty. When much about the future remains unknowable and many parties must concur on a path forward, those who formulate policy should seek strategies that, ideally, will not fail to minimize perceived costs or yield tangible benefits no matter which future comes to pass or who judges the success of the strategies." [@lempert2003shaping, p. 53]

And its stated virtues, p. 53: it gives a computationally convenient basis for identifying arguments true across an ensemble; it offers a normative description of good choices under deep uncertainty and multiple stakeholders; and "it matches the criteria decisionmakers often use when faced with such conditions."

**That third virtue is worth carrying.** The chapter is not introducing an exotic rule; it is naming what committees already do when they say *whatever happens, this one is defensible*.

## 4. Regret, and its documented pathologies

> "This concept of robustness draws from, but is not identical to, **L. J. Savage's criterion of minimizing the maximum regret.** Savage describes his mini-max rule as a practical rule of thumb for cases where individuals or groups are 'vague' about the probabilities they attach to certain events and where all parties to the decision are certain that, for some strategies, the largest degree of loss will still seem acceptably small." [@lempert2003shaping, p. 53]

**Savage (1950) was not obtained**; the concept and the pathologies below are used **as reported at** `lempert2003shaping`.

And the source's footnote 13 lists what Savage himself said against the rule:

> "the rule often yields neither a best strategy nor a simple ordering among strategies. Furthermore, in a group context, the rule can be undemocratic because the importance of a view is independent of the number of people who hold it. Participants can easily manipulate outcomes by lying about the weights they assign to alternative futures. In some cases, the mini-max rule can be too sensitive to low-probability, high-consequence events, thereby producing clearly unreasonable results." [@lempert2003shaping, p. 53, n. 13]

**Four pathologies, from the rule's own author.** The chapter must carry them, and the first bears directly on the anchor: the minimax portfolio and the runner-up are separated by a margin the inputs cannot support, which is exactly "neither a best strategy nor a simple ordering".

The footnote also records how the study's own method responds: by "explicitly considering an interactive group process that attempts to find strategies robust across a wide range of values and expectations and that provides a systematic means of characterizing those values and expectations in which a particular choice of robust strategy will not perform well."

**That last clause is the honest close for §6 of the manuscript**: name the futures in which your robust choice fails.

## 5. Scenario ensembles

The source treats scenarios as a challenge set rather than as forecasts.

> "Scenario ensembles are powerful artifacts for eliciting information from groups and for communicating information about risk to wide audiences... Ensembles also offer compelling alternative futures that can force stakeholders to question their assumptions and provide a framework to understand the views of others who might hold very different expectations about the future." [@lempert2003shaping, p. 52]

And on what governs their construction:

> "In addition to its role in providing a suitable challenge set for analysis, **the diversity requirement** that guides the construction of scenario ensembles is crucial to building credibility among parties to a decision." [@lempert2003shaping, p. 52]

**Diversity, not count.** Twenty scenarios that differ in one parameter are one scenario.

The source also notes that plausibility judgments are embodied as constraints — "certain paths are excluded because they violate known principles of economics" [@lempert2003shaping, p. 52] — so an ensemble is not everything imaginable.
