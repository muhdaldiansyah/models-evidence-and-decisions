# Research 01 — Objectives, Constraints, and Margins

Cluster R01 of `research-plan.md`. Closed.

Sources: `epa2010economic` glossary, printed pp. xiii–xiv, read directly; `bradley2016structured` p. 8, read for Chapter 10.

## 1. What Chapter 11 handed over

Chapter 11 laid out **one** decision: three acts, two states, a payoff table. It computed, checked where the answer turned, and priced information.

**It did not scale.** Fifteen zones, seven candidate schemes, and a fixed envelope is a different object, and the governed central question says so: *at scale*.

What changes at scale is that the acts are no longer alternatives — they are **combinations**, and the interesting structure is which combinations are affordable and what the affordability limit is worth.

## 2. Margins, defined

The EPA's guidance glossary supplies the definitions this chapter needs.

> "**Marginal benefit.** The marginal benefit is the benefit received from an incremental increase in the consumption of a good or service. It is calculated as the increase in total benefit divided by the increase in consumption." [@epa2010economic, p. xiii]

> "**Marginal cost.** The marginal cost is the change in total cost that results from a unit increase in output. It is calculated as the increase in total cost divided by the increase in output." [@epa2010economic, p. xiii]

> "**Opportunity cost.** Opportunity cost is the value of the next best alternative to a particular activity or resource. Opportunity cost need not be assessed in monetary terms. It can be assessed in terms of anything that is of value to the person or persons doing the assessing." [@epa2010economic, p. xiv]

**Note the last sentence.** Opportunity cost does not require a single currency — which matters, because `colyvan2016voi` p. 305 recorded that value-of-information machinery does, and Chapter 11 paid that price. **Marginal reasoning is cheaper in exactly that respect**, and the chapter should say so.

## 3. The stopping rule, and where it comes from

The same document states the efficiency condition in passing: markets are efficient when prices "reflect their marginal costs, or when marginal benefits equal marginal costs" [@epa2010economic, glossary].

**That is the rule marginal reasoning is supposed to supply**: keep going until the next unit costs as much as it is worth, then stop.

It is a good rule, it is the reason marginal thinking is taught at all, and **§5 of the manuscript shows it failing on the anchor** — not because the rule is wrong but because the anchor's investments are lumpy, and there is no "next unit" to evaluate.

## 4. Constraints, from Chapter 10 forward

Chapter 10 introduced `constraint` and taught the practice of asking who set each one and what would change it. Two of the anchor's four dissolved on inspection.

**Chapter 12 needs the other half of the concept**: a constraint that survives inspection defines what is **feasible**, and the set of affordable combinations is the object optimization searches.

The **£2.4m envelope** is the anchor's live constraint. Chapter 10 found it soft — an annual planning convention with a precedent for exceptions — which is precisely what makes §4 of this chapter worth doing: **if the envelope can move, what is it worth to move it?**

That question is unanswerable in Chapter 10's terms and answerable in this chapter's, and the join is worth making explicit.

## 5. Where this sits in the process

`bradley2016structured` p. 8's six steps put Chapter 10 at Objectives and Alternatives, and Chapter 11 at Prospects, Trade-offs, Recommendations.

**Chapter 12 is not a further step.** It is the same steps applied to a programme rather than a choice, plus a criterion — robustness — that replaces the optimality standard when the model is in doubt. `research-03` §1 records the source's own statement of that replacement.
