# Chapter 15: Strategic Interaction, Incentives, and Endogenous Response

## 1. The Number Fell and Nothing Changed

Chapter 14 ended by naming an assumption every chapter of this book has made.

The reservoir does not know its trigger is set at 150.
The weather does not read the operating manual.
The burst main is not waiting to see whether the utility notices.

**This chapter removes that assumption**, and the first thing to say about it is that removing it does not make the analysis harder in the way you would expect.
It does not add noise or complexity.
It makes some of what you have already established stop being true, on a date, for a reason nobody recorded.

### Before reading further

Here is a seven-year series from the utility's regulatory returns.

**Properties below minimum pressure** is the count the regulator collects.
It is measured at a representative point in each of the utility's fifteen zones — the arrangement Chapter 3 established.

**Low-pressure complaints** is what customers rang up about.

| Year | Properties reported below minimum pressure | Low-pressure complaints |
|---|---:|---:|
| 2016 | 1,280 | 875 |
| 2017 | 1,210 | 884 |
| 2018 | 1,230 | 911 |
| 2019 | 770 | 905 |
| 2020 | 690 | 880 |
| 2021 | 640 | 915 |
| 2022 | 610 | 930 |

**One further fact: no capital work was done in these zones during this period.** No mains renewed, no pumps replaced, no pressure management installed.
The network in 2022 is the network of 2016 with three more years of age on it.

**Allow about ten minutes and write two things down.**

**What happened?**

**And what would you need to know to be sure?**

Do this before reading on.
The second question is the one this chapter is about.

### What the arithmetic says

Take the three years before 2019 as a baseline.

**Mean reported count: 1,240. Mean complaints: 890.**

Against that baseline:

**In 2019 the reported count fell 37.9%.
Complaints rose 1.7%.**

**By 2022 the reported count had fallen 50.8%.
Complaints had risen 4.5%.**

The count that is supposed to measure the problem halved.
The problem did not.

**Nothing further about the mechanism until §3.** If you have already worked out what happened, hold it; if you have not, the chapter is about to be more useful to you.

### Two things worth noticing about your answer

Whatever you wrote, check it against two questions before continuing.

**Did you propose a mechanism, or a story?** *The utility improved service* is a story and it is contradicted by the complaint column. *Somebody changed how it is counted* is a mechanism, and it can be checked.

**And did you say how you would check it?** This is the harder half and most first answers skip it.
It is easy to arrive at a plausible account of a table like this one; it is much harder to name the document you would ask for.

**If your answer to the second question was "look at the data more carefully", the chapter has something for you**, because the thing that settles this is not in the data at all.

### What this chapter is for

Every chapter of this book so far has assumed, without saying so, that the thing being measured does not know it is being measured.

Chapter 3 asked what the numbers stand for.
Chapter 4 asked why these records and not others came to exist.
Chapter 7 asked what would identify a causal effect.
Chapter 12 chose a portfolio against futures that do not depend on the choice.
Chapter 14 chose a rule for a system with no opinion about it.

**All of that is correct and none of it survives contact with a second party who has read your rule.**

### The phrase this chapter takes apart

Chapter 3 found something specific and slightly uncomfortable: the utility's records called a zone adequately served **because of where an instrument happened to be installed.**

*Happened to be.*

Chapter 3 was right, and the phrase was accurate when it was written.

**This chapter is about what changes when instrument placement stops happening and starts being chosen** — and about the fact that the two are indistinguishable from inside the data, which is a claim Chapter 4 made and deferred here.

### What you will be able to do

Date the moment a measure acquired consequences, and know why that date is the most useful thing in the file.

Look for a discontinuity there — and know exactly how much a discontinuity proves, and how little its absence does.

Name which of four mechanisms broke a metric, and know that only one of them requires anybody to have decided anything.

Describe a two-party interaction as a game, identify the outcome nobody can improve on alone, and recognise that such an outcome can be worse for everybody than the arrangement it replaced.

And distinguish a record shaped by institutional purpose from a record shaped by people responding to being measured — which look identical, and are not.

## 2. Thirteen Words, One Claim

This book's own architecture says that this chapter covers thirteen things.

> strategic dependence, incentives, equilibrium as consistency, commitment, information asymmetry, principal-agent relationships, delegation, endogenous response, metric gaming, Goodhart-type failures, Campbell's law, Lucas critique, and manipulation of evidence

**That is the longest list attached to any chapter in this book.** Chapter 8 had eight, Chapter 13 nine, Chapter 14 seven.

**They are not thirteen topics**, and a chapter that treated them as thirteen topics would be a glossary with a case study attached.

### The claim

Seven of the thirteen are one thing seen from different directions.

> **A measured relationship is a fact about a world in which nobody was being measured for consequences — and once consequences attach, the relationship is not evidence about the new world but a relic of the old one.**

**Endogenous response** is the general name for what happens.
**Metric gaming** is what it is called when somebody does it deliberately.
**Goodhart-type failures** are the mechanisms by which it happens, and there are four.
**Campbell's law** and the **Lucas critique** are two earlier statements of it, from different fields.
**Manipulation of evidence** is the extreme case, and this chapter's case contains none.
**Performativity** — which the architecture does not name but the sources do — is its formal statement.

Sections 3, 4, and 5 are that claim.

### The vocabulary

The other six — **strategic dependence, incentives, equilibrium as consistency, commitment, information asymmetry, principal-agent relationships, and delegation** — are not instances of the claim.
They are the machinery for reasoning about **why** it happens and what could be done about it.

They arrive in §6 and §7, with one worked instance each, and they are taught briskly because that is what a working vocabulary is for.

### Why this is being said out loud

Books do not usually explain their own organisation this way.

**This one is doing it because the alternative is worse.** A reader who checks this chapter against the book's stated architecture and finds seven of the thirteen words missing from the section headings should be able to see immediately that nothing was dropped, rather than having to take it on trust.

**And because the organisation is itself the lesson.** A chapter that gave thirteen definitions would leave you able to recognise thirteen words.
A chapter that gives one claim leaves you able to notice the thing when it happens to you, which is different and harder.

### What this does to the earlier chapters

It is fair to ask whether this chapter invalidates what came before.

**Mostly it does not, and the exception is worth being precise about.**

**Chapters 2 to 9 are safe.** They are about a physical network and records of it, and nothing in the reservoir has an interest in how it is modelled.
The demand residual of Chapter 4 was institutional purpose, not response — and §4 gives you the check that establishes that rather than assuming it.

**Chapters 10 to 12 are safe in the same way.** Objectives, values, and portfolios are internal to the utility.
Nobody outside is choosing their actions in response to which portfolio gets funded.

**Chapters 13 and 14 are where the exception lives.** Chapter 14's rules are applied to a system without interests — but the *reported* quantities those rules key on are produced by people, and after 2019 one of them is produced by people with a stake in the number. **A rule keyed to a measured count is only as good as the count's independence from the rule.**

**So the damage is bounded and specific**, which is worth more than a general warning: it is a list of which results to re-examine and which to leave alone.

### One distinction, up front

Chapter 13 taught **policy resistance** — an intervention defeated by the system's response to the intervention itself.

**That is not this.**

Policy resistance needs nobody to know anything.
A reservoir resists a production increase by physics: raise the pressure and the old main leaks more, and the main has no view about it.

**This chapter needs somebody who knows what the rule is.** The response runs through a person or an organisation reading the rule, working out what it rewards, and acting on that.

**Both are real, they often occur together, and the remedies are different**, which is why the book separates them.

### Why thirteen, and why now

It is worth asking why this chapter's list is so much longer than any other.

**Because these ideas were developed independently, in fields that were not talking to each other, and each field named the phenomenon after whoever noticed it locally.**

An economist meets the **Lucas critique**.
A public-administration scholar meets **Campbell's law**.
A monetary economist meets **Goodhart's law**.
A sociologist of science meets **performativity**.
A machine-learning researcher meets **distribution shift** and then, later, **performative prediction**.
A manager meets **gaming the numbers**.

**They are not all the same thing** — the four mechanisms in §3 are genuinely different, and Campbell's claim is stronger than Goodhart's.
But the overlap is large, the vocabularies do not cross-reference, and a reader who learns one set is liable to think the others are something else.

**So the length of the list is a fact about the history of the subject rather than about its difficulty**, and knowing that is worth something: when you meet a fourteenth name for this in some field you have not read, you will recognise it.

### One word the architecture does not name

There is a fourteenth term, and the sources use it: **performativity**.

It is not in the book's list for this chapter, because the list was written before the research.
It arrives in §5, and it is the formal statement of the claim above — the version with a definition attached rather than a slogan.

**It is registered as vocabulary and flagged**, because a chapter adding a term its own architecture does not name is exactly the kind of thing this book requires to be surfaced rather than slipped in.

## 3. Four Ways a Measure Comes Apart

There is a well-known law about this and the book could not obtain it.

**Goodhart's law** was stated by Charles Goodhart in a 1975 paper on UK monetary management.
That paper could not be found in any form this book could read, so what follows is quoted **as reported at** a source that could be read, which gives the reference in full and quotes the law verbatim:

> "any observed statistical regularity will tend to collapse once pressure is placed upon it for control purposes." [@manheim2019goodhart, p. 1, n. 1]

**Read what it actually says**, because the popular version — *when a measure becomes a target it ceases to be a good measure* — is snappier and says less.

It does not say metrics are bad.
It does not say measurement is futile.
It says that **a regularity collapses under pressure**, which locates the failure in the pressure rather than in the measuring.

### What a Goodhart effect is

The same source gives a working definition:

> "As used in this paper, a Goodhart effect is when optimization causes a collapse of the statistical relationship between a goal which the optimizer intends and the proxy used for that goal." [@manheim2019goodhart, p. 1]

**Three parts, and they are worth separating.**

**A goal** — what you actually want.
Properties receiving adequate water pressure.

**A proxy** — what you can count.
Properties recorded below minimum pressure at a representative point.

**Optimization** — somebody pushing on the proxy.

**The first two are ordinary and unavoidable.** Every measurement in this book is a proxy for something, and Chapter 3 spent a chapter on why.
The third is what turns a proxy into a problem.

And that gives a diagnostic question, from the same page:

> "The importance of Goodhart effects depends on the amount of power directed towards optimizing the proxy" [@manheim2019goodhart, p. 1]

**So the question is not "is there a metric here?"** — there always is.

**The question is "how hard is somebody pushing on it, and who?"**

### Four mechanisms

> "1) Regressional, where selection for an imperfect proxy necessarily also selects for noise, 2) Extremal, where selection for the metric pushes the state distribution into a region where old relationships no longer hold, 3) Causal, where an action on the part of the regulator causes the collapse, and 4) Adversarial, where an agent with different goals than the regulator causes the collapse." [@manheim2019goodhart, p. 2]

> "These varied forms often occur together, but defining them individually is useful." [@manheim2019goodhart, p. 2]

**Four names, and this is the section's main result: only one of them requires anybody to have decided anything.**

### Pause: which one would you expect?

You have four mechanisms and you have the series from §1 — a count halving while the thing it measures does not move.

**Before reading on, write down which of the four you expect to find, and which you expect to be absent.**

Two minutes.
Most readers name one.
The answer is four.

### Now the case

Here is what happened to the utility's count.

**In 2019 the regulator made it an incentive.** The count entered a published comparative table with a financial consequence attached, and **£1.8m** was at stake across the price-control period.
Before 2019 it was collected and reported and nothing followed from it.

**Between 2018 and 2020 the utility re-designated the representative measurement point in 9 of its 15 zones.
Seven of the nine moved to a point at lower elevation, where pressure is higher.**

**Every move was documented, justified in writing, and compliant with the regulator's guidance**, which delegates measurement-point selection to the licensee.

**Nothing illegal happened.
Nothing undisclosed happened.
Nobody lied.**

Hold on to that, because §3's remaining work is to show that three of the four mechanisms would have been present even if nobody had moved anything.

### All four, on this case

| Mechanism | On this case | Does it need somebody to decide? |
|---|---|---|
| **Regressional** | Zones enter the count by measured pressure at one point, and that measurement contains noise. Selecting the worst-measuring zones selects partly for bad luck. | **No** |
| **Extremal** | Driving the count down moves the utility into a region — sensors at the most favourable points in every zone — where the old relationship between count and complaints describes nothing. | **No** |
| **Causal** | Moving a sensor intervenes on the proxy and not on the goal. Nobody's water pressure changed. | The **regulator's** action created the pressure |
| **Adversarial** | The utility chose a measurement protocol knowing which protocol the regulator would score. | **Yes** |

**All four are present**, which the source says is usual.

**And the popular vocabulary has one word for all four.** *Gaming.* An organisation that reads this situation as gaming will go looking for the person who decided — and will find them, because in this case there is one.
But in a case with only the first two mechanisms, it will look for a villain and find none, and conclude that nothing is wrong.

### Why the four names change what an organisation does

The taxonomy earns its place because the four mechanisms call for different responses, and one vocabulary hides that.

**Regressional** cannot be fixed and can be anticipated.
Expect the top of any league table to regress, build the expectation into the target, and do not treat the regression as evidence that anything went wrong.

**Extremal** is a warning about range.
The relationship you fitted holds where you fitted it; pushing the metric to an extreme takes you out of that range, and Chapter 9's transport question is the right one to ask.

**Causal** is about what an intervention touches.
Moving a sensor moves the proxy.
The check is Chapter 7's: does the action change the goal, or only the measurement of it?

**Adversarial** is the only one where somebody has to be persuaded, deterred, or bargained with — and it is the only one where §6's table is the right tool.

**An organisation that calls all four "gaming" reaches for the fourth response every time.** It looks for the person who decided.
Where the mechanism is regressional, there is no such person, and the search ends with everybody cleared and nothing learned.

### The one that cannot be avoided

> "No matter what measure is chosen for optimization, an inexact metric necessarily leads to a divergence between the goal and the metric in the tail." [@manheim2019goodhart, p. 2]

The source calls Regressional Goodhart "the most fundamental: it cannot be avoided."

**You have met both halves of this already.**

**Chapter 3** established that a score is not the construct — every measure differs from the thing it measures.

**Chapter 8** established that estimator properties are properties of a procedure over repetitions, and that any particular estimate contains its share of noise.

**Regressional Goodhart is those two facts meeting selection.** Choose the extreme of a noisy proxy and you have chosen partly for the noise; the noise does not persist; and the thing regresses.
It is not a failure of anybody's integrity or attention. **It is arithmetic, and it is waiting in every league table ever published.**

### An earlier statement, from a different field

The same source quotes a formulation that predates Goodhart's:

> "The more any quantitative social indicator is used for social decision-making, the more subject it will be to corruption pressures and the more apt it will be to distort and corrupt the social processes it is intended to monitor." [@manheim2019goodhart, p. 8, n. 5]

**That is Campbell's law**, from a 1979 paper on assessing planned social change. **This book could not obtain that paper either**, and quotes it as reported at the same source, which gives the reference in full.

**Note the second clause.** Campbell's law says more than Goodhart's: not only that the indicator becomes untrustworthy, but that **the process being monitored is itself distorted.** In the utility's case the first happened and the second did not — measurement points moved, water pressure did not.
Campbell's stronger claim is available and this case does not demonstrate it.

### And a third, named only

Economists have a version called the **Lucas critique**, from a 1976 paper.

**This book has not read that paper.** It is named at [@manheim2019goodhart, p. 1, n. 1] as a closely related formulation, and it is not stated there.
So here is its content in the book's own words, attributed to nobody: **a relationship estimated from historical data will not survive a policy change, if the people whose behaviour produced that data will adjust their behaviour to the new policy.**

If that is a poor summary, the fault is this book's.
Nothing above is quoted, and nothing is attributed to Lucas beyond the existence of the critique.

### What the source says about its own categories

One more thing, which the source volunteers and which this chapter would be wrong to tidy away:

> "Because none of the terms were laid out formally, the categories proposed do not match what was originally discussed." [@manheim2019goodhart, p. 1, n. 1]

**The four mechanisms are a modern reconstruction**, not a division any of the three original authors made.
They are useful — this section is built on them — and they are not what Goodhart, Campbell, or Lucas said.

**Two further honesties about this source.** It is a preprint and has not been peer-reviewed.
And its taxonomy originates in blog posts, which the source states openly in its own references. **The book uses its structure and not its framing**, which is oriented toward a subject this book is not about.

## 4. Purpose or Response: a Discriminator

Chapter 4 left this chapter a specific job, and set the standard for it.

Chapter 4 taught that records exist because a process created them, and that the process's purpose shapes what gets recorded.
Then it showed a strategic example once, explicitly out of scope, and wrote:

> "nothing about the network changed. What changed was the recording process, in response to being watched."

And its specification asked for the distinction "in a form the reader can apply, **since institutional purpose and strategic response look identical from inside a dataset**."

### Why they look identical

Both produce the same signature: a recorded quantity that systematically fails to correspond to the thing it names, in a direction that suits whoever kept the record.

**Chapter 4's version** is standing.
The recording process was built for a purpose, the purpose shapes it, and nobody along the way intended a distortion.
The utility's demand figure is a subtraction residual because subtraction was the cheapest way to get a number, not because anyone wanted it to absorb a third of everything.

**This chapter's version** has an author, though not necessarily a culprit.
The record changed because somebody with an interest in the number learned that it had consequences.

**No pattern in the values distinguishes them.** Both give you a number that is wrong in a convenient direction.
A reader who goes looking for a tell-tale pattern will find whichever they were expecting to find, which is worse than finding nothing.

### Pause: which is it?

Look back at the series in §1 — before reading the answer.

**From the numbers alone, can you tell whether the utility's records were shaped by institutional purpose or by response to being measured?**

Try.
Two minutes.

### The discriminator

**Find the date the measure acquired consequences.
Then look for a discontinuity at that date and not before.**

That is the whole of it.

### Why it works

**Institutional purpose is a standing property of a recording process.** It is present throughout the record.
It drifts as the institution changes — slowly, over years, as staff and systems and priorities turn over. **It has no reason to move sharply on any particular day.**

**Strategic response has a start date.** It begins when somebody learns the number matters: the day the metric is published, penalised, bonused, league-tabled, or written into a contract.

**So the two have different time signatures**, and the time signature is available in data you already have.

### Three things that make it usable rather than merely correct

**The date is usually documentary.** Regulatory determinations, incentive schemes, published league tables, and bonus arrangements have commencement dates, and they are in writing.
You are not inferring the date from the data you are testing.

**No counterfactual is needed.** You are comparing the record with itself, before and after.
There is no control group to find and no comparison population to argue about — which is more than Chapter 7 could say about anything.

**And it fails safely, in one direction.**

### The direction it fails in, stated where you will not miss it

**A discontinuity at the date is strong evidence of response.**

**No discontinuity is weak evidence of nothing.**

The response may have been gradual, because it takes time to work out what the metric rewards.
It may have been anticipatory, because the consultation preceded the determination by two years and everybody could read.
It may be there and small.
Or the measure may genuinely have changed nothing, which does happen.

**A discriminator presented as decisive would be worse than no discriminator at all**, because it would license the conclusion that everything without a visible step is clean.
It does not license that.

### The case

**The date: 2019.** In the regulator's published determination, with a commencement date.
Documentary.

**The pre-period: flat.** 1,280, 1,210, 1,230 — three years within 6% of each other.

**The discontinuity: 1,230 to 770 in one year, a fall of 37.4%**, with no capital work and no change in the network.

**And the thing the count is supposed to track did not move.** Complaints went 911 to 905.

**That is as clean as this test ever gets**, and the chapter should say so: most real cases are messier, and a first application that looks like this one is unusually lucky.

### And now Chapter 3

Here is why the discriminator was worth four pages.

**Chapter 3 found that the utility's records called a zone adequately served because of where an instrument happened to be installed.** That finding was correct.
The book has relied on it since.

**Before 2019, "happened to be" was accurate.** The instrument was where somebody put it in 1994, for reasons involving a lay-by and a lockable cabinet.
Institutional purpose, exactly as Chapter 4 described.

**After 2019, the same sentence is false, in nine of fifteen zones.** The instrument is where it is because somebody worked out where it should be.

**The data look the same.** A zone recorded as adequately served, with a representative point that flatters it.
Chapter 3, working entirely from inside its own dataset, **could not have told the difference** — and it did not claim to, because it had no way to.

**The date is the only thing that separates them**, and Chapter 3 had no reason to look for a date, because in 2016 there was not one.

### What to do when there is no date

The discriminator needs a date, and sometimes there is not one.

**A measure can acquire consequences gradually** — first published, then compared, then discussed at board level, then written into somebody's objectives.
By the time it is penalised the response has been running for years.

**Or informally.** Nothing is in writing; everybody knows the chief executive looks at this number.

**Three things to do instead, in descending order of strength.**

**Look for a different date on the same process.** The measure may have no commencement date, but the reporting system that produces it was replaced in some particular year, or the guidance was reissued, or the team changed.
Any documented change to the recording process gives you a place to look, and Chapter 4's methods are the ones that find it.

**Look at the dispersion rather than the level.** Response to a metric tends to compress variation as everybody converges on whatever the metric rewards.
A series whose mean drifts and whose spread collapses is worth a second look, though this is much weaker evidence than a step.

**And ask who would gain.** This is the weakest of the three and the manuscript will not pretend otherwise.
It generates hypotheses; it settles nothing; and it is the one most likely to find what you went in expecting.
Use it to decide where to look, never as a finding.

**Say which of the three you used.** A conclusion reached by the third is a different kind of claim from one reached by the first, and the difference should survive into whatever you write.

## 5. When the Prediction Changes the Target

The utility lost something in 2019 that it has not yet noticed.

### The broken relationship

Its planning model used a ratio: **0.72 complaints per reported property below minimum pressure.**

That was fitted on 2016 to 2018, where it was stable — 0.68, 0.73, 0.74. It was used to forecast complaint volumes, which drives call-centre staffing.

**Apply it to 2022's reported count of 610 and it forecasts 439 complaints.**

**The actual figure was 930.**

**The outcome is 112% higher than the forecast** — or, stated the other way, **the forecast is 53% below what happened.** The gap is 491 complaints, which is most of a year's workload for the team that answers them.

**And nothing went wrong with the fitting.** The relationship was real.
The data were accurate.
The arithmetic was correct. **The relationship was a fact about a world in which the count was not being pushed on**, and that world ended in 2019.

### The general name

> "We call such predictions performative; the prediction causes a change in the distribution of the target." [@perdomo2020performative, §1]

**`performativity`.** And two immediate cautions.

**It has nothing to do with the ordinary English sense of "performative"**, where it means done for show.
That sense is far commoner and the collision is unfortunate.
In this chapter the word means what the quotation says and nothing else.

**And it is not a machine-learning problem**, although the source is a machine-learning paper.
The authors are explicit about the direction:

> "Performativity is a well-studied phenomenon in policy-making that has so far been neglected in supervised learning." [@perdomo2020performative, Abstract]

They are not announcing a discovery about the world.
They are reporting that something long recognised in policy had been overlooked in their own field. **A reader who meets the term in a technical paper and takes it for a technical problem has it backwards.**

*A note on the citations in this section.* The published version of this paper carries page numbers in the proceedings record and **does not display them in the document**.
This book's practice is to cite the version whose pagination it can see, so this source is cited by numbered section and by Abstract, both of which are printed on the page.

### The equilibrium notion, and what it does to Chapter 6

> "A conceptual novelty is an equilibrium notion we call performative stability." [@perdomo2020performative, Abstract]

> "Performative stability implies that the predictions are calibrated not against past outcomes, but against the future outcomes that manifest from acting on the prediction." [@perdomo2020performative, Abstract]

**Stop on the second sentence, because it moves something Chapter 6 fixed.**

Chapter 6 taught calibration: a forecaster is calibrated if, over a record, the things they gave 70% to happen about 70% of the time.
The record is the arbiter.
You check the forecasts against what happened.

**Here what happened depends on the forecast.**

The utility's 0.72 ratio was calibrated against 2016 to 2018. **The world it was calibrated against no longer exists, and it stopped existing partly because of what the utility did with the number.** There is no fixed target to be calibrated to; the target moves when you aim at it.

**That does not make calibration useless.** It makes the object of calibration harder to name, and the source's answer — calibrate against the outcomes that arise from acting on the prediction — is coherent and much harder to do.

### Which relationships are at risk

Not every relationship in an organisation breaks when a metric acquires consequences.
It is worth being able to say which ones will.

**A relationship is at risk if one of its variables is the metric**, or is produced by the same process as the metric.
The utility's ratio has the reported count on one side, so it breaks.

**It is at risk if one of its variables shares an instrument with the metric.** Everything derived from the fifteen representative points is now derived from points chosen for a reason, whether or not it is the metric itself.

**It is probably safe if both variables are independent of the reporting process.** Complaint volumes against temperature.
Burst frequency against pipe age.
These involve nobody whose interests the incentive touches.

**The practical form is a short list**, made once, at the moment an incentive is announced: *which of the numbers we rely on come from the process this incentive is attached to?* Everything on that list is a relationship whose historical fit has just become a claim about the past.

**Nobody makes this list**, and the reason is ordinary: the incentive arrives as a regulatory matter, and the relationships live in operational models owned by different teams.

### The most transferable observation in the source

> "Retraining is often considered an undesired—yet necessary—cat and mouse game of chasing a moving target." [@perdomo2020performative, §1]

> "Performativity therefore suggests a different perspective on retraining, exposing it as a natural equilibrating dynamic rather than a nuisance." [@perdomo2020performative, §1]

**This has nothing to do with machine learning and everything to do with organisations.**

An organisation whose forecasting model keeps needing to be refitted usually reads that as maintenance — the world drifts, we update, this is the cost of doing business.

**The alternative reading is that the refitting is the system converging**, and that each refit is a move in an exchange between the model and the thing it models.

**Which reading is right changes what you should do.** If it is drift, refit more often.
If it is convergence, refitting more often makes it converge faster to a place you may not want to be — and the thing to change is not the fitting schedule but what the model is used for.

### What the utility should have noticed, and when

The forecast failure was visible for four years before anybody would have had to be clever to see it.

**2019: forecast 554, actual 905.** The ratio jumps from 0.74 to 1.18 in a single year.

**2020, 2021, 2022: it keeps going.** 1.28, 1.43, 1.52.

**A ratio that has been stable for three years and then moves 60% in one year and keeps moving is not noise**, and Chapter 8 gave the reader everything needed to say so.
Nobody had to know the word performativity.
Somebody had to be looking at the ratio.

**And nobody was**, because the two numbers live in different places.
The reported count goes to the regulator.
The complaint volume goes to the call centre's staffing model. **They meet only in the ratio, and the ratio is nobody's report.**

**That is the ordinary shape of this failure.** Not a subtle statistical effect that a competent analyst would miss, but a comparison that no job description contains — which is the same finding Chapter 14 reached about the rule nobody compared.

### Not policy resistance

Chapter 13 taught policy resistance, and the two are easy to confuse.

**Policy resistance:** the utility raises pressure to serve Hillcrest, the old main leaks more, and two thirds of the water never arrives. **The main has no view about this.** It is physics.

**Performativity:** the utility's complaint forecast stops working because the utility changed where it measures. **This runs through somebody reading the metric and deciding.**

**The test is whether the response would survive the responder not knowing.** Leakage does not care whether anybody knows about the incentive.
Measurement-point re-designation cannot happen without somebody knowing.

## 6. Two Parties, and an Equilibrium Nobody Wanted

Everything so far has described what happened.
This section is about why it was stable.

### Strategic dependence

> "A STRATEGIC GAME is a model of interacting decision-makers. In recognition of the interaction, we refer to the decision-makers as players." [@osborne2004game, p. 11]

And the sentence that defines the condition this chapter is about:

> "The model captures interaction between the players by allowing each player to be affected by the actions of all players, not only her own action." [@osborne2004game, p. 11]

**That is `strategic dependence`, and every chapter before Part IV assumed it away.**

The reservoir is affected by what the utility does and by nothing else that has an opinion.
The demand forecast is affected by the weather, which is not watching.
Chapter 12's three futures do not depend on which portfolio is chosen.

**The regulator does depend on what the utility does, and the utility depends on what the regulator scores.**

The formal object has three ingredients:

> "A strategic game (with ordinal preferences) consists of • a set of players • for each player, a set of actions • for each player, preferences over the set of action profiles." [@osborne2004game, p. 11]

**The third is the one that matters.** Preferences over *profiles* — over the whole list of everybody's actions — not over one's own action alone.
A party who cared only about its own action would not be in a game; it would be in Chapter 11.

### And belief becomes necessary

> "In a game, the best action for any given player depends, in general, on the other players' actions. So when choosing an action a player must have in mind the actions the other players will choose. That is, she must form a belief about the other players' actions." [@osborne2004game, p. 19]

**Compare Chapter 11.** There the decision-maker faced states of the world — the pump was failing or it was not — with a probability attached.
The states did not care what act was chosen.

**Here the "states" are the other party's choices**, and those choices depend on what that party believes about yours.

### The table

Here is the situation as a table. **One player's actions are the rows, the other's are the columns, and each cell holds a pair of numbers — the row player's payoff first.** The convention is [@osborne2004game, p. 19]'s.

The regulator's payoff is in **hundreds of properties genuinely receiving adequate pressure** — its actual objective, not its metric.
The utility's is in **£m, net of costs**.

| | Utility: report as sited | Utility: re-site to favourable points |
|---|---|---|
| **Regulator: no incentive** | 12.4 , 0 | 12.4 , −0.3 |
| **Regulator: incentive on the count** | 13.1 , −1.8 | **12.4 , +1.8** |

**These numbers are this book's construction.** They are not measurements and not derived from any source; they exist to make the structure legible.

Walk the cells.

**Top left.** No incentive, no re-siting.
The situation before 2019. Service is what it is; no money moves.

**Top right.** No incentive, but the utility re-sites anyway.
Pointless and slightly costly, so it does not happen.

**Bottom left.** The incentive works as intended.
The utility improves service to avoid the penalty, the regulator gets 13.1, and the utility pays 1.8. **This is what the regulator was trying to buy.**

**Bottom right.** The incentive is in force and the utility re-sites.
Service is unchanged at 12.4. The utility avoids the penalty and collects the reward instead: +1.8.

### Pause: what should the regulator do?

You have the table.
You are the regulator.

**What do you do, and what happens next?**

Three minutes, before reading on.

### Equilibrium as consistency

The answer starts with why the bottom-right cell is where things stay.

> "First, each player chooses her action according to the model of rational choice, given her belief about the other players' actions. Second, every player's belief about the other players' actions is correct." [@osborne2004game, p. 20]

**The second component is what the book's architecture means by `equilibrium as consistency`.** Not that anyone is happy.
That everybody's expectations about everybody else are right.

The definition itself carries symbols this book does not reproduce, so here it is paraphrased, and stated as a paraphrase: **an outcome in which no player could do better by changing their own action alone, given what everybody else is doing.**

The source gives two readings worth having:

> "a Nash equilibrium corresponds to a steady state... there is no pressure on the action profile to change." [@osborne2004game, p. 20]

> "Expressed differently, a Nash equilibrium embodies a stable 'social norm': if everyone else adheres to it, no individual wishes to deviate from it." [@osborne2004game, p. 20]

### Why nobody moves

**Check the utility.** Given the incentive is in force, re-siting gets +1.8 and reporting as sited gets −1.8. It moves.

**Check the regulator.** Given the utility re-sites, keeping the incentive gets 12.4 and dropping it gets 12.4. It is indifferent, and dropping a published incentive scheme costs credibility, so it stays.

**Neither can improve by changing its own action alone.
That is the equilibrium.**

### What it cost to get there

**Look at the top-left cell and the bottom-right cell.**

**12.4 and 12.4.**

The regulator's actual objective — properties genuinely receiving adequate pressure — is exactly where it was before anybody did anything.

**And to arrive there: £1.8m has moved, nine measurement points have been relocated, a documentation exercise has been completed in fifteen zones, and the utility's complaint forecast has stopped working.**

**The equilibrium is no improvement on the arrangement it replaced.** Not a disaster, not a scandal, not a fraud.
Simply a great deal of activity that returned everybody to where they started, with one planning relationship destroyed on the way.

### What the table leaves out

Four cells is a small object to carry a section, and three things are missing from it.

**The regulator has more than two actions.** It could set the incentive on a different quantity, fix the measurement points, audit, or vary the size of the penalty. §6 uses two because two is what a two-by-two holds, and the reader should not conclude that the regulator faced a binary choice.

**The payoffs are stated as though each party had one objective.** Chapter 10 spent a chapter on why that is rarely true.
The regulator also cares about being seen to act, about consistency across companies, and about not being reversed on appeal — none of which is in the table.

**And the game is played once here, and is not.** Price controls run in five-year cycles, and each party knows there will be another.
Repeated interaction changes what is stable, sometimes dramatically, and **this book does not teach it.**

**So the table is a device for seeing the structure, not a model of the situation.** That distinction is Chapter 2's, and it applies to this table as much as to anything the utility built.

### The cell nobody can reach

**13.1.** Bottom left.
The regulator's best outcome, and better for the regulator than doing nothing.

**It is unreachable**, and the table says exactly why: it asks the utility to accept −1.8 when +1.8 is sitting next to it.

**This is the shape of the problem and it is not a shape that better intentions fix.** The regulator wanted 13.1. It designed an incentive that would have produced 13.1 if the utility had responded the way it hoped.
The utility responded the way the table says.

### Why the obvious remedies do not work

**"Use a better metric."** A better metric acquires consequences too, and then it is a metric with consequences attached, which is the situation you were in.
This is worth saying because it is the commonest proposal and it sounds like a solution.

**"Use more metrics."** More surfaces to respond to, and now the utility chooses which to optimise, which is more discretion rather than less.

**"Audit the measurement-point changes."** They were all documented and all compliant.
An audit finds exactly what the utility filed, because the utility filed it.

**"Penalise the re-siting."** Sometimes right.
But the regulator delegated point selection because it cannot site fifteen zones' worth of instruments itself, and some of the moves may have corrected genuinely poor siting. **Removing the discretion removes a capability the regulator needs.**

**None of these is stupid, and the chapter is not going to solve it.** What §7 offers instead is the vocabulary for saying precisely which of these trade-offs you are making.

### The two equilibria

`equilibrium` now means two different things in this book, in two different chapters, and the terminology registry has flagged the collision since Chapter 1.

| | Chapter 13 | Chapter 15 |
|---|---|---|
| What it is a property of | a point in the state space | a profile of actions |
| The condition | the dynamics are stationary there | nobody can do better alone, and beliefs are correct |
| Who must believe anything | nobody | everybody |
| What it says about goodness | nothing | nothing |

**A pendulum has an equilibrium and has no beliefs.**

That is the shortest statement of the difference, and it uses the book's own standing example.
Note the fourth row: **neither sense says the outcome is any good**, and this chapter's case is the demonstration — a perfectly stable arrangement that is worse than what it replaced.

### The source's own warning

One more sentence, and it is the source's, on the page that introduces the concept:

> "Whether or not the notion of Nash equilibrium is appropriate in any given situation is a matter of judgment." [@osborne2004game, p. 20]

> "Ultimately, the test of the appropriateness of the notion of Nash equilibrium is whether it gives us insights into the problem at hand." [@osborne2004game, p. 20]

**This is the fourth time in this book that a source has disqualified its own generality on the page that introduced it** — after Greenland and colleagues on P values, Deaton and Cartwright on trials, and Sutton and Barto on their own guarantees.

**Four sources, four fields, and the same move each time.** It is worth noticing as a property of good technical writing rather than as four coincidences.

## 7. The Vocabulary, and What It Is Missing

Six words remain from §2's list.
Each gets an instance and a sentence.

**`incentive`.** A consequence deliberately attached to a measured quantity in order to change behaviour. **The £1.8m.** It changed no physical fact about the network and changed a great deal of behaviour, which is what an incentive is for and also what makes it dangerous.

**`delegation`.** Assigning a choice to the party whose behaviour that choice will be used to assess. **The regulator's guidance delegates measurement-point selection to the licensee** — sensibly, because the regulator cannot site instruments in fifteen zones it does not operate.

**`information asymmetry`.** One party knows something the other cannot cheaply find out, **and both know this.** The utility knows which points in each zone are favourable.
The regulator would have to survey fifteen zones to find out, and the utility would still know more.

**`principal-agent`.** A relationship in which one party acts on another's behalf, their objectives differ, and the first cannot fully observe what the second does. **The regulator and the utility**, and the description fits without anybody behaving badly.

**`strategic dependence`.** Already defined in §6. Its instance here: **the utility's best measurement protocol depends on what the regulator scores**, and on nothing else.

**`commitment`.** Binding yourself in advance so that the other party's expectation of your future action changes.

### Three of these are about who knows what

Notice that `information asymmetry`, `principal-agent`, and `delegation` are not three separate ideas so much as one situation described from three angles.

**There is a relationship** in which one party acts for another with different objectives — principal-agent.

**There is a gap in what each can see** — information asymmetry.

**And there is a specific decision the first has handed to the second** — delegation.

**Take away any one and the situation changes character.** Without the asymmetry the regulator could simply check.
Without the delegation the utility would have nothing to choose.
Without the differing objectives there would be no reason to worry about either.

**Which gives three places to intervene rather than one**, and that is the whole practical value of having the three words: an organisation with only the word *incentive* has one lever, and an organisation with these three has four.

### Commitment gets a longer look

It is the only one of the six that suggests a remedy.

**The regulator could commit to fixed measurement points for five years.** Publish them, freeze them, and refuse changes.
The utility's re-siting option disappears, and the bottom-right cell with it.

**And here is what that costs.** Some of the utility's fifteen points were badly sited in 1994 — Chapter 3 established exactly that.
A commitment to freeze them freezes the bad ones too, for five years, in zones where the count is understating a real problem.

**So the commitment buys protection against strategic re-siting at the price of the regulator's ability to correct genuine error, and there is no version where it gets both.**

**That is what a commitment is.** Its value comes from being unable to change your mind, and its cost is being unable to change your mind.
A commitment you would break under sufficient provocation is not one.

### Manipulation of evidence, and why this case contains none

The book's architecture names **manipulation of evidence** in this chapter's competences, and it is worth separating from everything above.

**Fabricating a pressure reading is fraud.** It is illegal, it triggers audit, it ends careers, and it is not what happened here.

**Moving a sensor and documenting the move is not fraud.** It is legal, it was disclosed, it was justified in writing, and it was compliant.

**And the second is more dangerous than the first**, for a reason worth stating plainly: **nothing triggers.** There is no audit finding, no whistleblower, no discrepancy for anybody to notice.
The fall from 1,230 to 770 appears in a published table as an improvement, and it stays there.

**The mechanisms that leave no trace are the ones worth learning to see**, which is what §4's discriminator is for.

### And now the honest paragraph

Two of the terms above — **`principal-agent`** and **`information asymmetry`** — are named in this book's governed architecture, and **this book obtained no source for either.**

That is not a stylistic omission.
The book's standing rule is that a practice taught without a source must be flagged, and there is a further standing instruction that a chapter reaching for that position should **reopen research rather than rely on the fact that earlier chapters did the same.**

**Research was reopened.** Four attempts were made across three routes: two standard economics papers, both paywalled, and one open document from a prize committee — which was obtained, and whose text could not be reliably extracted, so nothing in it could be quoted under the rule this book adopted in Chapter 7.

**The reopening failed**, and the definitions you have just read come from the case rather than from any literature.
They are, as far as this book can tell, standard. **This book cannot tell you that from a source it has read**, and the chapter's decision record refers the question to the author rather than settling it.

**Nothing procedural is taught from these two terms** — no contracts, no incentive-compatibility conditions, no mechanism design — and that restraint is deliberate.

### What survives all of this

A chapter this long about how measurement fails should say what it is not claiming.

**It is not claiming that measurement is futile.** The complaint series in §1 measured something real throughout, and it is the reason we can tell what happened to the other series. **One of the two numbers held**, and it held because nothing was riding on it.

**It is not claiming that incentives never work.** The bottom-left cell of §6's table is real: had the utility responded to the incentive by improving service, everybody would have been better off.
The regulator's design was not stupid; it was beaten.

**And it is not claiming that nobody is responsible.** Chapter 13 carried a sentence on this from a source that had spent a paper explaining how structure shapes behaviour, and the point holds here: recognising that an incentive produced a response does not mean nobody chose anything. **Somebody at the utility worked out where the favourable points were.** That the choice was legal, documented, and rational does not make it nobody's.

**What the chapter claims is narrower and, I think, more useful.** A number that has consequences attached is a different object from the same number before, and the relationships you fitted to the first do not transfer to the second. **That is checkable, it is dated, and it is almost never checked.**

### Retrieve the procedure from memory

From memory, write the six steps.

1. Name the **goal** and the **proxy**, and say how they differ.
2. Ask **how much power is directed at optimizing the proxy**, and by whom. If the answer is none, most of this does not apply.
3. Find **the date** the measure acquired consequences. It is usually in a document.
4. Look for a **discontinuity at that date**. Present is strong evidence; absent proves little.
5. Ask which of the **four mechanisms** are operating, and remember that three need nobody to decide anything.
6. Ask what **relationship you were relying on** was fitted before that date — and check it, because it is the thing that breaks silently.
