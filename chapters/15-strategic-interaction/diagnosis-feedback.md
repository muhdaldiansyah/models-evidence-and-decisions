# Chapter 15 — Diagnosis Feedback

**Open this only after you have written all five diagnoses.**

Four of these statements contain a defect. **One of them correctly identifies what happened and then prescribes a remedy that will not work**, which is a different kind of failure and the one worth finding.

Nothing here is scored.

---

## 1. "Properties below minimum pressure fell 51% between 2016 and 2022, so service improved."

**The defect.** The measure improved and the thing it measures did not, and the second column was on the same page.

Complaints went from a pre-2019 average of 890 to 930 — **up 4.5%** — while the reported count halved. No capital work was done. Nothing in the network changed.

**What it stops you concluding.** Everything. This is the reading the regulator's comparative table recorded for three years, and an organisation that accepts it stops looking.

**It is also the most dangerous statement in this book**, for a reason worth naming: **every word of it is true**, it would survive any audit, and it is exactly what the published figures say.

**The repair.** Whenever a measure moves, ask what independent indicator of the same underlying thing is available, and look at it. **Here there was one, in the next column, and nobody put them side by side** — because the count goes to the regulator and the complaints go to the call centre, and the two meet only in a ratio that is nobody's report.

---

## 2. "The utility gamed the metric. This is a compliance failure and should be dealt with as one."

**The defect.** There is no compliance failure. Every measurement-point move was documented, justified in writing, and compliant with the regulator's own guidance, which delegates point selection to the licensee.

**A compliance response therefore finds nothing and closes.** The investigator asks for the file, receives the file, confirms that every move was properly made, and reports that no rule was broken — which is true, and which leaves the count halved and the complaints unchanged.

**And there is a second defect, which is worse.** *Gaming* names one of four mechanisms. Regressional and extremal Goodhart are present in this case and need nobody to decide anything; the causal mechanism is present because the **regulator's** action created the selection pressure.

**So an organisation that reads this as gaming looks for the person who decided.** Here it finds one. **In a case with only the first two mechanisms it finds nobody, and concludes that nothing is wrong.**

**What it stops you concluding.** That three of the four mechanisms would have operated even if the utility had left every sensor where it was.

**The repair.** Ask which of the four mechanisms are operating before deciding what kind of problem you have. **Only one of them is answered by finding somebody**, and it is the one that leaves a documented trail — which is to say, the easiest one and not the most common.

---

## 3. "The metric was badly chosen. We should replace it with one that cannot be gamed."

**This is the one that is right about what happened and wrong about what to do.**

The diagnosis is correct as far as it goes: the count came apart from the goal, and the count is the metric.

**The remedy will not work, for three reasons.**

**The replacement acquires consequences too.** Whatever measure the regulator adopts next becomes, on the day it is adopted, a measure with a financial consequence attached — which is the situation that produced this one. There is no measure that is immune to being pushed on, and Goodhart's law is about the pushing.

**"Cannot be gamed" is the wrong property to look for.** Gaming is the adversarial mechanism. A measure immune to it is still exposed to regressional Goodhart, which the source says **cannot be avoided** by any choice of measure whatever.

**And the cost of the change is real and is never counted.** Every relationship anybody has fitted to the old measure is invalidated on the day it is replaced, which is the loss §5 quantified — a planning ratio producing forecasts less than half the size of reality.

**What it stops you concluding.** That the problem might be the incentive rather than the measure, or the delegation rather than the incentive, or that the honest answer might be to accept a weaker measure and stop attaching money to it.

**The repair.** Before replacing a measure, ask which of the four mechanisms you are trying to defeat, and check whether the replacement defeats that one. Then ask what the change costs in broken relationships — and **write down the list of relationships fitted to the old measure**, which is a short exercise nobody does.

## 4. "There's no discontinuity in the northern zones' figures at 2019, so nothing strategic happened there."

**The defect.** The discriminator fails safely in one direction only, and this statement runs it backwards.

**A discontinuity at the date is strong evidence of response.**

**No discontinuity is weak evidence of nothing.**

Three reasons a response can leave no step. **It may have been gradual**, because working out what a metric rewards takes time. **It may have been anticipatory**, because the consultation preceded the determination and everybody could read. **Or it may be there and small**, which is what a response looks like in a zone where there was less to gain.

**What it stops you concluding.** It licenses "clean" for every series without a visible step, which is most series. A discriminator used this way is worse than none, because it converts an absence of evidence into a clearance.

**The repair.** State the asymmetry every time you use the test. And where there is no step, say which of the three explanations you are unable to rule out — rather than reporting that nothing happened.

---

## 5. "Complaints per reported property rose from 0.72 to 1.52, so the complaints data must have deteriorated."

**The defect.** The ratio has two sides, and this statement blames the one that did not move.

**Complaints went 890 to 930 across seven years** — a 4.5% drift, of the size you would expect from population and nothing else.

**The reported count went 1,240 to 610.** That is the side that moved, and it moved because of what was done to it.

**The reasoning error is worth naming**, because it is general: when a ratio changes, the instinct is to suspect whichever side you trust less. **Complaint data feels soft** — self-reported, subject to reporting propensity, everything Chapter 4 taught you to distrust. **The regulatory count feels hard** — audited, defined, submitted under licence.

**And here the hard number is the one that broke**, precisely because it was the one worth breaking. **Nothing was riding on the complaints figure**, which is exactly why it kept measuring something.

**What it stops you concluding.** It sends you to fix the data source that was working.

**The repair.** When a ratio moves, ask which side had consequences attached, and start there. **The side under pressure is the side to suspect**, and that is the reverse of the usual instinct about data quality.

---

## What the five have in common

**Statement 1 is true. Statement 3 is right about the diagnosis. Statement 5 is arithmetically correct.**

None of the five is stupid, and three of them would survive a meeting.

**The shape is the same throughout: each answers a question adjacent to the one that matters.** *Did the measure move?* rather than *did the thing move?* *Was a rule broken?* rather than *which mechanism is operating?* *Is the metric flawed?* rather than *what is the metric attached to?* *Is there a step?* rather than *what would a response look like here?* *Which number changed?* rather than *which number was under pressure?*

**And every repair is the same move.** Find the thing the number is attached to — the consequence, the date, the party with an interest — and reason about that, rather than about the number.
