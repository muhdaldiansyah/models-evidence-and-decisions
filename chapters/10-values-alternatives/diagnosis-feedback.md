# Chapter 10 — Diagnosis Feedback

Open this file only **after** you have written your diagnosis of all five statements in §6.

Your wording will differ; what matters is whether you found the same fault.

## 1. "Our objective is to become the sector leader in resilience."

**Defect.** It is a vision statement, and it fails both halves of the objective test.

**What it costs.** No item of value — "resilience" names a category, not a quantity that could be more or less. No usable direction — "become the leader" is relative to other organisations rather than to an outcome anybody experiences.

Objectives "become the evaluation criteria for identifying and evaluating alternatives" [@bradley2016structured, p. 49]. Hold two options against this sentence and neither is preferred, because nothing in it discriminates. So the decision proceeds on criteria supplied by whoever framed the options.

And it does specific damage that a plainly vague sentence would not. **It is inspiring, so nobody objects.** Everyone in the room agrees with it, which feels like alignment and is the absence of a criterion.

**Repair.** Ask what being sector leader in resilience would consist of, for whom, and then write the two-part form: an item of value, and a direction. Expect to get three or four objectives out of one slogan, and expect them to point at different options — which is the finding.

**Note.** The documented version is broader than vision statements: what decision-makers have is "a messy mix of means and ends, targets, policies and vision statements, most of which are not useful for decision-making" [@bradley2016structured, p. 49]. Vision statements are one of four things on that list, and targets are another.

## 2. "The options are Option 1 or Option 2."

**Defect.** A supplied option set is being treated as the option set.

**What it costs.** Everything not on the paper is cut off — the Latin sense of *decision* itself, "to cut off" [@bradley2016structured, p. 5] — and it is cut off before the committee reads a word.

On the Hillcrest paper, seven alternatives were derivable from three objectives in an afternoon, with no new data. One of them fits the same pump housing and costs a fraction of Option 1; one addresses the second candidate mechanism entirely; one serves the affected households directly and is not a capital project at all.

**None of those was rejected.** There was no meeting at which they were considered. The option set was fixed by the format of the document and by whoever was asked to work something up.

**Repair.** Write the objectives first, then derive: for each objective, what would serve it? Then ask what the paper's format cannot express — non-capital answers, contingent answers, combinations, and answers belonging to a different department.

**Note.** "Anchoring on the first proposed alternative" is the first of four documented traps [@bradley2016structured, p. 7]. It is the hardest to resist because by the time a paper exists, months of work sit behind Option 1, and reopening the question looks like discarding that work.

## 3. "We consulted the technical experts on what matters."

**Defect.** Two different questions have been asked of one roster.

**What it costs.** *How does this system behave* is a factual question and expertise is the qualification for answering it. *What should we be trying to achieve* is a value question, and the qualification is being affected: "All parties, regardless of education or socio-economic status know what is important to them and can communicate those values" [@bradley2016structured, p. 7].

The utility's operations manager and finance lead are the right people for the first question and are not households on the top floor of a Hillcrest block in August. **What *adequate pressure* means to the people who lose it was never established by anyone who could establish it.**

The cost is not abstract. The attribute the utility will eventually count — properties reporting below some threshold during a declared event — was chosen by people who have never been in that position, and it may be the wrong count.

**Repair.** Keep two rosters. Who knows how it works, and who has a stake. Then ask the second group one question before any options exist: *what would count as this going well for you?*

**Note.** Being in the room is not being asked. Operations crews are usually present, in a factual capacity, and their stake in which alternative is chosen is routinely skipped precisely because their presence makes the consultation feel done.

## 4. "The budget is fixed, so those are the only feasible options."

**Defect.** A constraint is being accepted without asking who set it.

**What it costs.** "Accepting constraints as immoveable" is one of the four documented traps [@bradley2016structured, p. 7], and on the Hillcrest paper two of four stated constraints did not survive one email.

The capital envelope is an annual planning convention, and the utility has moved capital between years twice in five years. The like-for-like housing requirement is a procurement convention, and a variable-speed drive fits the same housing — **so a cheaper alternative was eliminated by a framework agreement nobody in the room could have named.**

**Repair.** For each stated constraint, ask two things: **who set this, and what would change it?** Then sort into physical or statutory against convention, budget, or timetable. Email the authors of the second group.

**Note.** The constraints that feel most immoveable are usually the ones with no remembered author. A convention starts as a decision with a reason; the person leaves; the reason goes unwritten; and within a few years it is indistinguishable from a law of nature. **The statutory duty gets questioned constantly because everyone knows who set it.**

## 5. "Once we have the cost-benefit numbers, the decision follows."

**Defect.** Evidence is being asked to settle what matters.

**What it costs.** More than the other four, because it is said by the most analytically careful person present.

A cost-benefit calculation requires a statement of what counts as a benefit and how much of one thing is worth another. **Those are the value questions.** An analysis that produces a number has answered them — silently, using whatever weights were embedded in whoever specified it — and the number then arrives looking like a finding.

Nine chapters of this book were about making evidence honest. Not one of them can tell the utility whether forty households matter more than three zones' worth of mains renewal.

The source places evidence precisely: alternatives are examined for complexity, effectiveness, and consequences to other objectives, and "Effectiveness partially requires factual information" [@bradley2016structured, p. 54]. **One of three dimensions, of one step, of six.**

**Repair.** Write the objectives before commissioning the analysis. Then the analysis has something to estimate, its weights are visible, and — the useful part — you can tell which evidence is worth buying. Values "help prioritize the spending of limited resources on gathering information relevant to what is important" [@bradley2016structured, p. 6].

**Note.** The order this chapter proposes reverses the one Parts I and II implied. That is deliberate and it is the chapter's most consequential claim: **what matters is prior to which evidence is worth having**, and an organisation that gathers first will gather the wrong things carefully.
