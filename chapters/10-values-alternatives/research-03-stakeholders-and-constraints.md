# Research 03 — Stakeholders and Constraints

Cluster R03 of `research-plan.md`. Closed.

Sources: `bradley2016structured` printed pp. 6–7, read directly; `nasem2026decisionmaking` as verified for Chapter 1 and recorded at `../../sources/nasem2026decisionmaking.md`.

## 1. Who counts

The report's framing is unusually direct about the basis for inclusion:

> "Involving stakeholders: All parties, regardless of education or socio-economic status know what is important to them and can communicate those values in VFT" [@bradley2016structured, p. 7]

**The qualification for having a value is being affected**, not expertise. That matters because the reflex in a technical organisation is to consult the people who understand the system, and understanding the system is a qualification for supplying facts rather than for supplying values.

The book already has this separation. `../../sources/nasem2026decisionmaking.md` records that its source supports separating "factual evidence and analysis" from "values and evaluative premises" as distinct inputs to a decision, and treats "involving affected people" as a step of its own alongside "establishing facts".

**Two different questions, two different rosters.** Who knows how the pump behaves is not who has a stake in whether it is replaced, and a consultation list built for the first will silently answer the second.

## 2. Common values

> "For a decision-maker faced with a multi-faceted decision context involving several viewpoints across stakeholder groups, it is beneficial that the identification, creation, evaluation and selection of decision alternatives be grounded in the common values of the interested parties. Common values are those that most stakeholders will agree upon, i.e., values that they share even if at different magnitudes." [@bradley2016structured, p. 6]

**"Even if at different magnitudes."** The claim is not that stakeholders agree, and the chapter must not soften into consensus-seeking. It is that agreement is more available at the level of what matters than at the level of what to do — which §6 of `research-02` recorded from p. 54 as well: most stakeholders agree on objectives high in the hierarchy even when they disagree about approaches.

That is a structural claim with a practical use: **when a room is deadlocked over options, move up the hierarchy.** It is also falsifiable — some rooms are deadlocked over fundamentals, and the chapter should say that rather than promise the technique always works.

## 3. Four documented traps

The report lists these under the advantages of value-focused thinking, attributing them to Gregory et al. (2012):

> "Avoiding common traps: common traps including anchoring on the first proposed alternative can hamper decisions, accepting constraints as immoveable, avoiding discussions of controversial tradeoffs, and rushing to premature solutions" [@bradley2016structured, p. 7]

Four, and each is worth naming separately because each has a different remedy.

| Trap | What it looks like | The remedy this chapter can offer |
|---|---|---|
| **Anchoring on the first proposed alternative** | the paper's Option 1 becomes the thing everyone argues about | derive alternatives from objectives instead |
| **Accepting constraints as immoveable** | "the envelope is fixed" | ask who set it and what would change it |
| **Avoiding controversial trade-offs** | the conflict between objectives is never stated | write the objectives down; the conflict becomes visible |
| **Rushing to premature solutions** | an option set arrives before anyone states what matters | the chapter's whole sequence |

**Gregory et al. (2012) was not obtained**, so these are used as reported and the middle column above is the book's own illustration rather than the source's. The manuscript must say so.

**The second trap is the one this cluster exists for.** A constraint that is accepted without examination does exactly what an implicit value does: it decides the outcome while remaining unexaminable. And unlike a value, it usually has an author who could be asked.

## 4. Constraints, and the book's own treatment

Neither source gives a taxonomy of constraints, and this book does not invent one. What the sources support is the practice of **asking of each stated constraint who set it and what would change it**, which follows directly from p. 7's naming of the trap.

The distinction the chapter uses — between constraints that are physical or statutory and constraints that are conventions, budgets, or timetables — is **the book's own** and is labelled as such. It is not a theory of constraints; it is a sorting question with two buckets and a rule for which bucket is worth pushing on.

**This is not a fifth instance of the demonstrate-because-unsourced disposition.** The practice is derived from a named trap in a read source; what the book adds is an illustration of how to run it. `../../decisions/0017` clause 4.3 records the distinction rather than assuming it.

## 5. Scope discipline

The book is not a facilitation manual, and both sources are written by and for people who run deliberative processes.

**What the chapter takes:** that stakeholders are those affected; that values can be elicited from anyone; that agreement is more available higher in a hierarchy; and the four traps.

**What it leaves:** workshop design, elicitation protocols, the DPSIR concept-mapping device `bradley2016structured` uses, conflict-resolution technique, and everything in `nasem2026decisionmaking` about agency practice.

`../../sources/nasem2026decisionmaking.md` already records the governing caution: the guide "is written for transportation-agency decision-makers and is used here as an authoritative practical synthesis, not as a universal formal theory of decision-making", and Chapter 1 "does not adopt the guide's steps as a mandatory checklist or named framework." **Chapter 10 inherits both cautions.**
