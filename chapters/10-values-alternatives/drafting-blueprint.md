Status: drafting control. Governs how `chapter.md` is written. Scope, terminology, and sources are governed by `spec.md` and are not restated here.

Provisional in the same sense as `spec.md`: built on proposed `../../decisions/0017-chapter10-values-terminology-and-boundary.md`.

## 1. Drafting objective

30 pages / 5 learning hours that leave the reader able to take a paper offering a fixed option set, find that it states no usable objective, write objectives that pass a test, name who was left out, and derive options the paper did not contain.

The chapter must **defeat** twelve named collapses (`spec.md`, "Failure modes").

## 3. Voice and exposition rules

### Register discipline

Three failure modes specific to this chapter.

**Sounding like a management text.** Values, stakeholders, and objectives are words with a corporate register attached. The antidote is that every claim lands on the committee paper and produces something checkable.

**Sounding like ceremony.** The source itself says simple decisions need no formal method; §2 must carry that.

**Losing the reader who came for analysis.** Nine chapters of evidential discipline end here, and a reader may feel the subject has gone soft. §1 must say directly that this is a different kind of question and that evidence cannot settle it.

## 5. Section 1 — Two Options and No Objectives

**Beats.**

1. Quote the committee paper in full. Two options, one stated objective, a recommendation.
2. Note that it is an entirely ordinary paper and nobody drafting it did anything wrong.
3. **Opening task, about six minutes.** What is the utility trying to achieve? Preserve unscored.
4. Name the change of subject: Part III asks what should be done, and evidence cannot settle it. Say this plainly, because a reader arriving from nine chapters will try.
5. Carry Chapter 9's four closing questions forward as the chapter's agenda.
6. Close on the Latin, quoted [@bradley2016structured, p. 5]: decision means *to cut off*, and everything not chosen is cut off — which makes the completeness of the option set a first-order question.

## 7. Section 3 — What Counts as an Objective

**Beats.**

1. The documented failure, quoted twice as the source prints it [@bradley2016structured, p. 49].
2. What objectives are for: they "become the evaluation criteria" [@bradley2016structured, p. 49]. So a paper with no objectives has criteria supplied by whoever framed the options.
3. **The two-part test** [@bradley2016structured, p. 50], quoted as a rule.
4. **Self-explanation pause 1.** Run it on "improve service resilience at Hillcrest".
5. The answer: fails both halves. Work it.
6. Rewrite it three ways that pass, and note that the three are **different objectives** — which is the point.
7. **Context specificity** [@bradley2016structured, p. 50], and the book's relativity shape, eighth appearance, in prose.
8. **Fundamental versus means**, with the "why is this important" test [@bradley2016structured, p. 51].
9. Work it on **minimize reportable incidents** — the organisation's most believed-in objective, and a means.
10. Say why the distinction matters: **means generate alternatives, fundamentals evaluate them**, and optimising a means can succeed completely while failing at what was wanted.
11. **Attributes** [@bradley2016structured, p. 51], and the Chapter 3 link, once.
12. `metric` introduced with its Chapter 15 forward reference.
13. The six properties, quoted once, attributed, with only two developed and the reason stated.
14. **Reader task.** Take one objective to a fundamental.

## 8. Section 4 — To Whom Does It Matter?

**Beats.**

1. The roster: seven affected, two asked.
2. Quote the inclusion basis [@bradley2016structured, p. 7].
3. **Two rosters, one list**: who knows how the system works, and who has a stake. The consultation list was built for the first and silently answered the second. Book's own formulation.
4. **Self-explanation pause 2.** Which affected party's absence most changes the decision?
5. The answer: the 40 households, because the first fundamental objective is about them and nobody has established what *adequate pressure* means to a person on the top floor in August.
6. **Common values** [@bradley2016structured, p. 6], with "even if at different magnitudes" intact. **Not consensus.**
7. Agreement is available higher in the hierarchy [@bradley2016structured, p. 54] — the practical move when a room is deadlocked. And the honest caveat: some rooms are deadlocked over fundamentals.
8. The scope limit: the book teaches no facilitation technique, and why.
9. **Reader task.** Complete the roster; say what each party would add.

## 9. Section 5 — Constraints That Are Not Constraints

**Beats.**

1. The four traps, quoted and attributed [@bradley2016structured, p. 7]. Say the illustrations are the book's own.
2. Focus on the second: accepting constraints as immoveable.
3. **The question**: who set this, and what would change it.
4. The four stated constraints, sorted. Two dissolve or soften.
5. The like-for-like constraint is the sharpest: a procurement convention that eliminated a cheaper alternative nobody costed.
6. **A constraint accepted without examination decides the outcome while remaining unexaminable** — exactly like an implicit value, except that it usually has an author. Book's own.
7. Chapter 5's link: an unexamined constraint is an unrecorded assumption.
8. Honest limit: real constraints are real, and the chapter is not teaching that everything is negotiable.
9. **Reader task.** Sort the four; name who to ask about each.

## 10. Section 6 — Generating Alternatives

**Beats.**

1. The ordering rule [@bradley2016structured, p. 54]: alternatives only after objectives.
2. **Derivation, not brainstorming.** Take each objective and ask what would serve it.
3. The seven alternatives, with the objective each serves.
4. **Self-explanation pause 3.** Which of the seven could the paper never have produced, and why?
5. The answer: alternative 6, because it is not capital and the paper is a capital paper — the artefact's format constrained the option set before anyone thought about it.
6. **Alternative 5, and the two-framings observation.** Chapter 7 proposed it as identification; Chapter 9 repeated it; here it is derived from objectives. State once, label as the book's own.
7. The three evaluation dimensions [@bradley2016structured, p. 54].
8. **Most alternatives affect more than one objective** [@bradley2016structured, p. 54], with the conflicts listed and **not resolved**.
9. Say plainly where the chapter stops and why: making the conflict visible is this chapter; resolving it is Chapter 11.
10. If nothing scores, reformulate [@bradley2016structured, p. 54].
11. **Planted-defect diagnosis task.** Five defects per `spec.md`. Feedback linked only after production.

## 12. What the draft may not do

- Attribute anything to `keeney1996valuefocused` beyond its abstract.
- Invent glosses for the four undeveloped objective properties.
- Weigh, score, rank, or trade off.
- Teach utility, preference elicitation, or value of information.
- Teach facilitation, elicitation protocols, or the DPSIR device.
- Treat metric gaming.
- Introduce notation.
- Present the committee paper as incompetent.
- Present every decision as needing this apparatus.
- Resolve the conflicts among objectives.
- Recommend an action for the utility.
- Present synthetic case values as typical, standard, or recommended.
