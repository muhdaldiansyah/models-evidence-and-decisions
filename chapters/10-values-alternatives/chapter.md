# Chapter 10: Values, Objectives, and Alternatives

## 1. Two Options and No Objectives

Here is the paper that goes to the asset committee.

> **Hillcrest Zone — Capital Options 2027/28**
>
> **Objective:** improve service resilience at Hillcrest.
>
> **Option 1.** Replace the Hillcrest duty pump. Capital cost **£310,000**.
> **Option 2.** Defer to 2028/29 and review.
>
> **Recommendation:** approve Option 1.

Nine chapters of this book have been about whether the evidence behind such a paper is any good. This chapter is about the paper itself, and about two things on it that nobody in the room will question.

**There are two options.**

**And the stated objective cannot be used to choose between them.**

Nobody drafting that paper did anything wrong. It looks like every capital paper in every organisation, it was written by someone competent under time pressure, and it will be approved.

### Before reading further

Take about **six minutes**.

> **What is the utility actually trying to achieve here?**

Write it down. Be specific — not "better service" but something you could hold an option against.

Keep what you write. You will come back to it, and the comparison is the point.

---

### This is a different kind of question

Part II ended with the utility unable to say what a pump upgrade would do. Chapter 9 closed by listing four things it could not settle from evidence at all:

- fewer complaints at what cost;
- complaints from whom, and whether it matters that Hillcrest's top floor is where the elderly residents are;
- what the money would otherwise have funded;
- whether an average improvement that worsens the worst days is an improvement.

**Not one of those is an evidence question**, and a reader who has just spent nine chapters acquiring evidential discipline will try to settle them with it anyway. That instinct is the first thing this chapter has to interrupt.

More data will not tell the utility whether forty households matter more than three zones' worth of mains renewal. That is a question about what the utility is for, and it has an answer, and the answer has to be written down before any evidence can be brought to bear on it.

### What a decision is

There is an etymology worth having.

> "The Latin definition of 'decision' is 'to cut off' (Merriam-Webster 2013). When faced with a choice, a decision-maker considers alternatives from which one is selected and the others are cut off. Resources are then committed to the selected alternative" [@bradley2016structured, p. 5]

**Everything not on the paper is cut off before anyone reads it.**

Which makes the completeness of the option set a first-order question rather than a refinement — and it is a question the paper's format quietly answers on the committee's behalf.

Part III is called Choose. This chapter makes the decision well-posed. Chapter 11 solves it.

### What the chapter will do to the paper

By the end, the two-option paper will have become something else, and it is worth knowing where you are going.

**Three objectives** that can be tested rather than agreed to. **Seven alternatives**, none of which required new data or a consultant. **Two of four constraints** dissolved or softened, each with a person who could be emailed. **Five parties** who are affected and were never asked. And a set of conflicts stated in a form somebody could argue about.

None of that is a recommendation. All of it took an afternoon, and every piece of it was available to the person who wrote the paper.

## 2. Values, Objectives, Alternatives

There is an established framework for this, and the book's route to it needs stating before it is used.

### A note on where this comes from

The framework is **value-focused thinking**, associated with Ralph Keeney.

The article this book holds in its bibliography for it, `keeney1996valuefocused`, was verified at abstract level only when Chapter 1 needed it, and its source note has carried a prohibition ever since: do not attribute value-focused procedures to it without full-text verification. **Full-text verification was attempted for this chapter and failed.**

So the framework is taken from a source that was obtained in full and read: a US Environmental Protection Agency report applying a structured decision process to a watershed in Puerto Rico, which sets the framework out and attributes it to Keeney's own work.

That is the same device this book has used four times now — Brier through a scoring-rules review, a Supreme Court judgment through a methods paper, Russell's chicken through a working paper. **Where a source cannot be read, it is cited through one that can be**, and the fact is stated rather than hidden.

### Three lines

> - "Values are what we fundamentally care about"
> - "Objectives define what matters in the decision and are based upon values"
> - "Alternatives are means to achieve our objectives"
>
> [@bradley2016structured, p. 5]

Read the third line again. **Alternatives are means.** Not the decision, not the subject, not the thing to be argued about — the instruments by which something wanted gets achieved.

The committee paper puts them first, and puts nothing behind them.

### The reversal, and why it is not an aberration

> "Making decisions based on 'what is important' is the basis of value-focused decision-making and is fundamentally distinct from the more common alternative-focused decision-making that emphasizes the range of possible routes to achieve a single, primary objective." [@bradley2016structured, p. 5]

**"The more common."**

Starting from options is not a mistake made by careless organisations. It is the default everywhere, and there are good reasons for it: options are concrete, they have costs attached, somebody has already worked them up, and a meeting can be held about them. Values are abstract and a meeting about them feels like a waste of a morning.

The report's figure caption states the consequence: "The common tendency is to start with AFT without first clarifying and explicitly stating the values and criteria that will be used for making a decision" [@bradley2016structured, p. 5].

### The sentence this chapter turns on

> "Alternative-focused decision-making does consider values, but often only implicitly. They may not be clearly stated and thus not fully considered when making a decision." [@bradley2016structured, p. 5]

**Values are present either way.** No decision has ever been made without them. The only question is whether anyone wrote them down.

So the committee paper is not value-free. It embodies a set of values — that capital projects are the relevant response, that the zone is the relevant unit, that the choice is between acting on the pump and not acting — and every one of them was decided by whoever drafted it, unexaminably, before the committee met.

**An option set is already a claim about what matters.**

That formulation is this book's, and you have met its shape four times:

| Chapter | What was present and unwritten |
|---|---|
| 6 | what a probability was conditional on |
| 7 | the identifying assumption a causal claim required |
| 8 | how the analysis was conducted |
| 10 | **what the decision is for** |

Same structure, fourth setting. In each case something load-bearing operates whether or not it is stated, and stating it is what makes it arguable.

### What the discipline buys

The report lists advantages, reporting them from Keeney's work [@bradley2016structured, pp. 6–7]: improving communication, involving stakeholders, recognising interconnectedness, guiding strategic thinking, creating alternatives, and avoiding common traps.

One deserves singling out for a book that has spent nine chapters on evidence.

> "Guiding information collection: Values help prioritize the spending of limited resources on gathering information relevant to what is important to the stakeholders" [@bradley2016structured, p. 6]

**That reverses the order Parts I and II implied.**

This book taught you to interrogate evidence, then estimate, then combine. All of that presupposes somebody decided which evidence was worth having — and that decision is a value question, answered before any of the evidential work begins.

The utility spent a year assembling five sources about pump upgrades. Had it written its objectives first, at least two of the seven alternatives in §6 would have been on the table, and the evidence worth buying would have looked different.

### Two honest limits

**It adds a step.** The report says so: "Although adding an extra step in the decision process, VFT gives decision-makers greater flexibility in finding acceptable solutions to problems" [@bradley2016structured, p. 6]. The discipline is not free and this chapter is not going to pretend it is.

**And most decisions do not need it.**

> "In many cases, single issue, well-defined decisions do not need a formal methodology for successful outcomes. Everyday decisions (e.g., when to hold a meeting or the best travel route to work) are quickly made with available information and common sense guided by experience." [@bradley2016structured, p. 7]

The test is complexity, multiple viewpoints, and consequential commitment of resources. A reader who builds an objectives hierarchy before choosing lunch has learned ceremony rather than method.

### Values are not the soft part

One objection needs answering before the chapter goes further, because a reader who holds it will read the rest as decoration.

*Values are subjective. Evidence is objective. So the values part is where rigour ends.*

The first two clauses are roughly right and the conclusion does not follow.

**Values are not arbitrary.** The forty households have a definite view about what losing pressure on an August afternoon costs them, and it is a fact about the world that could be established by asking. That it is a fact about people rather than about pipes does not make it unknowable.

**Values are structurable.** The whole of §3 is a set of tests: does this statement have an item of value, does it have a direction, does asking why produce another objective or stop. Those are checks, they have determinate answers, and applying them to a real corporate plan takes minutes and produces findings.

**And the alternative to structuring them is not objectivity.** It is the same values, held by fewer people, unwritten. The committee paper is not more rigorous for omitting them; it is less examinable.

Which is the same argument this book has made four times about four other things. Chapter 6 did not make probabilities subjective by asking what they were conditional on. Chapter 7 did not weaken causal claims by requiring their assumptions be stated. **Making the load-bearing part visible is what rigour consists of**, and it does not become soft when the load-bearing part is a preference.

### Where this chapter sits

The report gives a generic decision process, attributing it to Carriger and Benson (2012):

| Step | As printed |
|---|---|
| Decision context | "The reason for a decision opportunity" |
| Objectives | "Expressions of what is valued in the decision opportunity" |
| Alternatives | "The choices needed to fulfill the objectives" |
| Prospects | "The potential outcomes from the decisions and their uncertainties" |
| Trade-offs | "The willingness of stakeholders to accept more or less of one objective for another" |
| Recommendations | "The optimal strategy for achieving the objectives" |
>
> [@bradley2016structured, p. 8]

**Chapter 10 is the first three. Chapter 11 is the last three.**

Worth noting: two of this book's own governing documents divided that boundary differently, and the decision record for this chapter resolves it against the table above. The disagreement is recorded rather than quietly settled.

The report also notes that a structured process adds implementation and monitoring, so that "each management decision sets the context for the next round of decision-making" [@bradley2016structured, p. 8]. **That loop is Chapter 17's**, and it is named here so you know the sequence closes.

### Why the default is so stable

It is worth being fair to the alternative-focused habit, because a chapter that treated it as laziness would be missing why it survives.

**Options are fundable.** A budget line attaches to an option, not to a value. Somebody has to write a number in a plan, and the number attaches to a thing being bought.

**Options are owned.** An option has a sponsor, an engineer who worked it up, and a supplier. A value has none of those, so nobody's job is to bring it to the meeting.

**Options can be decided.** A committee can approve or refuse an option in an hour. A committee asked what the organisation is for will produce a discussion, not a decision, and everyone in the room knows it.

**And the work is already done.** By the time a paper reaches a committee, months of engineering have gone into Option 1. Asking what matters at that point looks like discarding the work — which is why the question has to be asked earlier, and why "we'll do that next time" is the standard outcome.

**So the discipline has to be cheap or it will not happen.** That is the design constraint on everything in this chapter: the objective test takes fifteen seconds, the roster takes ten minutes, the constraint question is an email. Nothing here requires a workshop, and a version that did would be true and useless.

Take the committee paper from §1 and the six steps above.

2. For any you mark *implicitly*, say who did it and when.

Question 2 is the useful one. Every implicit step had an author, and the author is usually available to be asked.

## 3. What Counts as an Objective

A reader might reasonably say the paper does state an objective — it is right there at the top.

This section is about why that sentence cannot do the job.

### The documented failure

The report prints this once in its text and once as a pulled callout, which is as close to underlining as an EPA report gets:

> "While many decision-makers say they have clear objectives, what they really have is a messy mix of means and ends, targets, policies and vision statements, most of which are not useful for decision-making (Gregory et al. 2012)." [@bradley2016structured, p. 49]

**"Most of which are not useful for decision-making."**

The problem is not that organisations lack ambitions. They have too many, at incompatible levels of abstraction, in a mixture where the thing wanted for its own sake sits beside the thing wanted because it leads somewhere else, beside a number somebody committed to in a plan.

### What an objective is for

> "Objectives in decision-making have a very specific purpose. They focus decision-makers on what matters in terms of outcomes and become the evaluation criteria for identifying and evaluating alternatives (Gregory et al. 2012)." [@bradley2016structured, p. 49]

**Objectives become the evaluation criteria.**

Which yields an immediate consequence. A decision with no usable objectives does not thereby have no criteria. It has criteria nobody wrote down — supplied by whoever framed the options, applied by whoever speaks most confidently in the meeting, and unavailable for anyone to disagree with.

### The test

Here is the format, and it is short enough to apply while reading.

> "Objectives are usually described as something that matters … and a verb indicating the preferred direction of change (e.g., maximize or minimize)" [@bradley2016structured, p. 50]

Stated as a rule on the same page:

> "Objectives have a direction of preference and an item of value.
> - Maximize the availability of quality habitat for a fish species.
> - Minimize the sediment loading into Guánica Bay." [@bradley2016structured, p. 50]

**Two parts. An item of value, and a direction of preference.**

The report says what each does: "The item of value is the key part of the usefulness of objectives in reflecting values. The direction of preference further specifies whether the item of value should be achieved or avoided" [@bradley2016structured, p. 50].

### Pause: run it on the paper

Before reading on, write two or three sentences.

> The committee paper's stated objective is: **improve service resilience at Hillcrest.**
>
> **Does it have an item of value? Does it have a direction of preference?**

---

**It fails both halves.**

**No item of value.** "Service resilience" is a category, not a quantity. It does not name something that could be more or less. Ask two people in the utility what would count as more of it and you will get two different answers, both defensible, neither checkable against the other.

**No usable direction.** "Improve" points a direction, but at what? Improve it from what to what, measured how?

And now watch what happens to the paper. Option 1 costs £310,000 and Option 2 costs nothing. Which one better improves service resilience? **The stated objective cannot answer**, which means the recommendation was reached on criteria that are not on the page.

### Three rewrites, which are three different decisions

Here are three objectives that pass the test.

> **Minimize** the number of households without adequate pressure during heat events.
>
> **Minimize** the number of low-pressure incidents reportable to the regulator.
>
> **Minimize** whole-life cost of maintaining Hillcrest at current service levels.

Every one is a legitimate reading of "improve service resilience". Every one has an item of value and a direction.

**And they do not choose the same option.**

The first favours anything that gets water to the top of the zone, including things that are not capital projects. The second favours whatever reduces the count of reportable events, which is not the same as reducing the number of affected households. The third favours deferring, or the cheapest intervention, or possibly relining the main.

**One vague sentence, three decisions.** That is what the format test buys: it converts an agreement that everyone thought they had into three positions that can be argued between.

### Objectives are for this decision

One more constraint from the same page:

> "Objectives are context specific: they are defined for the decision at hand, not for universal usage." [@bradley2016structured, p. 50]

You have met this shape now in eight chapters. Adequacy is relative to a use. Validity is relative to an interpretation. Trustworthiness is relative to a quantity. Criticism is relative to stakes. Probability is relative to information. Identification is relative to assumptions. Transport is relative to a setting.

**And objectives are relative to a decision.** A corporate objective that applies to everything the utility does is, by that fact, not an objective for choosing between two pump options.

### Which of them are actually wanted?

Not every objective on a list is wanted for its own sake, and the test for telling is a question a child would ask.

> "A fundamental objective is usually determined when the answer to 'why is this important' is '…just because'…. meaning that it is simply something that humans need or want." [@bradley2016structured, p. 51]

**Keep asking why until the answer stops being another objective.**

Run it on the second rewrite above.

*Minimize reportable low-pressure incidents.* Why is this important?

*Because incidents attract regulatory attention and penalties.* Why is that important?

*Because penalties cost money, and a poor record makes the next price review harder.* Why is that important?

*Because money spent on penalties is money not spent on the network, and because we exist to supply water reliably.*

**So it is a means, not an end.** Two steps down it becomes cost and service — which are on the fundamental list already.

That matters more than it looks. **Minimizing reportable incidents is the objective the utility most believes in.** It is in the corporate plan, it has a named owner, it is reported to the board monthly, and it is not a fundamental objective.

### Why the distinction earns its space

**Means objectives generate alternatives. Fundamental objectives evaluate them.**

That is the practical division, and getting it backwards has a characteristic failure: the organisation optimises the means, achieves it completely, and fails at what it wanted.

An organisation whose evaluation criterion is *minimize reportable incidents* will prefer interventions that reduce the count of reportable events. Reportability has thresholds and definitions. There are ways to reduce the count that do nothing for anybody's water pressure, and an organisation evaluating against the means will find them, without anyone intending it.

**Neither kind is defective.** Means objectives are where alternatives come from — §6 derives seven of them, mostly from means. The error is only in evaluating against a means as though it were the thing wanted.

### The objective that is a number

One more shape appears constantly and fails the test in an interesting way.

> *Reduce low-pressure incidents at Hillcrest to fewer than four per year by 2029.*

That looks far more rigorous than "improve service resilience". It has a quantity, a threshold, a location, and a date. It is the kind of statement organisations are praised for writing.

**It is a target, not an objective**, and the difference matters when you use it to choose.

An objective has a **direction of preference** — minimize, maximize — and a direction has no floor. A target has a threshold, and a threshold says that below four per year, further improvement has no value.

**Which is a claim about what matters, made by whoever picked the number.** Is three per year genuinely as good as one? If the answer is no, the target has misstated the objective. If the answer is yes, then the objective is not really about incidents at all — it is about clearing a threshold, which is usually about the regulator, which §3 has already shown is a means.

Targets are useful things. They commit an organisation, they can be reported against, and they concentrate effort. **They are not evaluation criteria**, because two alternatives that both clear the threshold are indistinguishable to a target and are not indistinguishable to anybody who cares about the households.

The report's own list of what decision-makers actually have includes "targets" among the things that are "not useful for decision-making" [@bradley2016structured, p. 49], and this is why.

### Attributes, and a warning

At the bottom of a set of objectives sit the things you actually count.

> "Evaluation measures, attributes that can be used to evaluate performance toward higher-level objectives, are at the bottom of the OH (Keeney 1992). Effective attributes are characterized by their measurability, understandability, and operability." [@bradley2016structured, p. 51]

And the pairing, on the same page:

> "The objectives and their corresponding attributes form a common vision for what is valued (objectives) and what will be assessed (attributes)." [@bradley2016structured, p. 51]

**What is valued, and what will be assessed. Deliberately two things.**

You have met this. Chapter 3 separated the `construct` — what you are trying to measure — from the `measure`, the procedure that produces numbers, and established that choosing a measure does not fix the construct. Here the objective is what is valued and the attribute is what gets counted, and the same gap opens.

*Minimize households without adequate pressure* is an objective. *Count of properties reporting pressure below 15 metres head during a declared heat event* is an attribute. The second is countable and the first is what you want, and they are not the same.

**`metric` is the management word for an attribute**, and it carries a hazard this chapter names and does not treat: when the stand-in becomes the target, when the organisation manages the number rather than the thing, the failure has a name and it is Chapter 15's.

### Six properties, and an honest limit

The report lists what makes objectives good:

> "Good objectives are:
> - Fundamental
> - Complete
> - Concise
> - Sensitive
> - Understandable
> - Independent (Gregory et al. 2012)" [@bradley2016structured, p. 51]

**Two of those this chapter can develop and four it cannot.**

*Fundamental* has the why-test above. *Independent* bears on something §6 returns to — that most alternatives affect more than one objective, so objectives that are secretly the same thing will double-count.

The source that defines the other four was not obtained for this book. The report quotes the list without glossing it, and inventing definitions for *Sensitive* and *Concise* would be attributing content to a text nobody here has read. **So the list is quoted, and four of its six terms are left as pointers to a literature rather than dressed up.**

### A note on how many objectives

Readers who try this on a real decision often produce twenty objectives and stall.

The report offers one relevant observation: "At first, the OH is normally very broad, and is further refined over time" [@bradley2016structured, p. 51]. A long first list is expected.

Two moves shorten it, and both come from material already in this section.

**Ask why of each.** Most of a long list is means. Twenty objectives typically collapse to three or four fundamentals with sixteen means hanging off them — and the means are not wasted, because §6 uses them to generate alternatives.

**Check for independence.** This is the second of the six properties, and it earns its place here: two objectives that are secretly the same thing will double-count, so an alternative serving one appears to serve two. *Minimize reportable incidents* and *minimize households without pressure* are not independent, because a household without pressure is frequently the thing being reported.

**What does not shorten the list is deciding what matters most.** That is weighting, it is Chapter 11's, and doing it here has a specific cost: an objective dropped early because it seemed less important is dropped without anyone seeing what it would have ruled out.

### Task: take one to a fundamental

Pick any objective from your organisation's current plan — a real one, from a real document.

1. Test it against the two-part format. Item of value? Direction of preference?
2. If it passes, ask **why is this important?** until you reach something wanted just because.
3. Write down how many steps it took.
4. Then ask: is the thing at the top of your chain something the organisation actually evaluates against, or does it evaluate against the thing you started with?

Question 4 is the one worth having, and the answer is usually uncomfortable.

## 4. To Whom Does It Matter?

The chapter's question has three parts and the second is *to whom*.

### The roster

Here is who is affected by the Hillcrest decision, and who was asked.

| Affected party | Consulted? |
|---|---|
| The **40 households** at the top of the zone, who lose pressure first | no |
| The other **1,100 households** in the zone | no |
| The **three zones** scheduled for mains renewal from the same envelope | no |
| **Operations crews**, who take the callouts during heat events | no |
| The **regulator**, to whom low-pressure incidents are reported | no |
| The **finance committee** | yes |
| The **operations manager** | yes |

**Two of seven.**

### Who qualifies

The report's basis for inclusion is worth reading twice:

> "Involving stakeholders: All parties, regardless of education or socio-economic status know what is important to them and can communicate those values" [@bradley2016structured, p. 7]

**The qualification for having a value is being affected.** Not expertise, not seniority, not the ability to read a hydraulic model.

That sounds like a platitude until you notice what it rules out. It rules out the idea that some parties' values need to be interpreted for them by people who understand the system better — which is exactly what happens when a technical organisation decides what its customers want.

### Two rosters, one list

Here is the mechanism by which the utility ended up at two of seven, and nobody made a decision.

**There are two different questions a decision needs answered.**

*How does this system behave?* — a factual question, and the qualification for answering it is knowing how pumps and pipes work.

*What should we be trying to achieve?* — a value question, and the qualification for answering it is having a stake.

The utility built one consultation list. It built it for the first question, because that is the question an engineering organisation knows it has. And that list then silently answered the second, because it was the only list anybody had.

**The operations manager and the finance lead are exactly the right people to ask about facts.** Neither of them is a household on the top floor of a Hillcrest block in August.

This separation is one the book has had since Chapter 1, where evidence about consequences was distinguished from the values used to evaluate them. What Chapter 10 adds is that the separation has an organisational form: **it is two rosters, and most organisations keep one.**

### Pause: whose absence matters most?

Before reading on, write two or three sentences.

> Seven parties are affected and two were asked.
>
> **Whose absence most changes the decision, and why?**

---

**The forty households.**

Not because they are the most numerous — they are the fewest. Because the first fundamental objective is *minimize households without adequate pressure during heat events*, and **nobody has established what adequate means to a person living on the top floor in August.**

Adequate to fill a kettle? To run a shower? At what hour? Is a two-hour loss on the hottest afternoon of the year a different thing from a two-hour loss at three in the morning? Does it matter that the top floor of those blocks is where the flats with lifts and grab rails are?

The attribute the utility will eventually count — properties reporting pressure below some threshold during a declared event — was set by people who have never been at the top of that zone in August. It may be the right attribute. **Nobody knows, because the question was never asked of anyone who could answer it.**

And note the second-order effect. The three zones scheduled for mains renewal are also absent, and the money is the same money. Their absence does not change what *adequate pressure at Hillcrest* means; it changes whether Hillcrest is the right place to spend.

### The party who is present and not counted

One more entry deserves comment, because it is the one organisations habitually mishandle in the opposite direction.

**Operations crews take the callouts.**

They are on the affected list, and they are also the people most likely to be in the room — as suppliers of facts. The operations manager was consulted. Somebody from operations wrote the technical annex.

**And their stake was never elicited**, because they were present in the factual capacity and nobody switched registers.

A crew turning out at 9pm on the hottest day of the year to a zone with no pressure has a definite view about which alternative is preferable, and it is not necessarily the one the operations manager would give. It bears on the third fundamental objective — disruption — and on a fourth nobody has written down, which is whether the work is tolerable to do.

**Being in the room is not the same as being asked**, and the parties most likely to be in the room are the ones whose values are most likely to be skipped, precisely because their presence makes everyone feel the consultation happened.

### Common values, not consensus

A reasonable worry: seven parties with seven sets of values sounds like paralysis.

> "Common values are those that most stakeholders will agree upon, i.e., values that they share even if at different magnitudes." [@bradley2016structured, p. 6]

**"Even if at different magnitudes."** The claim is not that stakeholders agree on how much something matters. It is that they frequently agree on *what* matters, and disagree about weight.

That is a much weaker claim than consensus and a much more useful one, because agreeing on the list is what makes the disagreement about weights tractable — which is Chapter 11's subject.

And there is a structural reason it works:

> "most stakeholders will agree on objectives high in the hierarchy even if not specific approaches for reaching an objective" [@bradley2016structured, p. 54]

**When a room is deadlocked, move up.**

A room cannot agree whether to spend £310,000 on a pump. The same room will agree, in about ninety seconds, that it wants fewer households without water on hot days, at lower whole-life cost, with less disruption. **The disagreement was never about what mattered. It was about which option served it**, which is an answerable question, and about how to weigh three things against each other, which is Chapter 11's.

The honest caveat: some rooms *are* deadlocked over fundamentals. A dispute about whether the utility exists to serve customers or to return capital is not resolved by moving up a hierarchy — it is located by moving up a hierarchy, which is still worth something.

### The three zones nobody mentioned

One entry on the roster deserves separate treatment, because it is the one that most changes what the decision is.

**Three zones are scheduled for mains renewal from the same £2.4m envelope.**

They do not appear on the committee paper. They are not a stakeholder in the ordinary sense — nobody from those zones will write in about Hillcrest, and their residents do not know the decision exists. But £310,000 spent at Hillcrest is £310,000 not spent there, and the paper's Option 2 is *defer*, not *spend it somewhere better*.

**Which reveals something about the option set.** The paper presents a choice between acting at Hillcrest and not acting at Hillcrest. The actual choice facing the utility is where to spend a fixed sum, and Hillcrest is one candidate among several.

That is not a criticism of the drafter. A paper about Hillcrest is a paper about Hillcrest, and somewhere else in the organisation is a paper about each of the three zones, each equally sensible, each written as though its zone were the only claim on the money.

**The comparison never happens because no single document contains it.** This is the same failure as alternative 6 in §6 — the artefact's boundary decides what can be compared — and it is more consequential here, because the money is genuinely shared and the papers genuinely are not.

### What consulting is and is not

One clarification, because the word does a lot of work and most of it bad.

**Being informed is not being asked.** A letter telling the forty households that works will occur is not a consultation about whether pressure at 3pm matters more than pressure at 3am.

**Being asked for facts is not being asked for values.** "How often do you lose pressure?" is a factual question with a factual answer, and it does not establish what losing pressure costs the person answering.

**And being asked after the options are fixed is not being asked.** A consultation on Option 1 versus Option 2 is a consultation about two things somebody else chose. It can only produce a preference between them, which is why organisations that consult late so often report that stakeholders had no strong views.

**The useful question is asked before the options exist**, and it is short: *what would count as this going well for you?* On the Hillcrest paper nobody asked it, and the answer would have produced at least two of §6's alternatives.

### What this book does not teach

Both of this chapter's sources were written by people who run deliberative processes, and both contain a great deal about how to do that: workshop design, elicitation devices, facilitation, conflict handling.

**None of it is here.** This book is not a facilitation manual, and a reader who needs to run a fifty-person values workshop should read those sources rather than this chapter.

What transfers without any of that machinery is the question. **Who is affected, and who was asked?** It takes ten minutes, it needs no workshop, and on the Hillcrest paper it returns two of seven.

### Task: complete the roster

For a decision currently live in your own organisation:

1. List everyone materially affected. Aim for at least six.
2. Mark who has been asked what they value — not who has been *informed*, and not who has been asked for facts.
3. For each unasked party, write one sentence on what they would add.
4. Then say which of them you could actually ask this week, and what it would cost.

Most readers find that the cheapest omission is also the largest.

## 5. Constraints That Are Not Constraints

The committee paper has two options partly because of what everyone knows cannot be done.

### Four traps

The report names them, reporting from Gregory et al. (2012):

> "common traps including anchoring on the first proposed alternative can hamper decisions, accepting constraints as immoveable, avoiding discussions of controversial tradeoffs, and rushing to premature solutions" [@bradley2016structured, p. 7]

Four, each with a different remedy. The illustrations below are this book's own; the source names the traps and does not gloss them.

| Trap | What it looks like on the Hillcrest paper |
|---|---|
| Anchoring on the first proposed alternative | the committee will spend its hour on whether £310,000 is worth it |
| **Accepting constraints as immoveable** | **this section** |
| Avoiding controversial trade-offs | the three zones losing their renewal money are not mentioned |
| Rushing to premature solutions | the paper arrived before anyone stated what matters |

### The question

For every stated constraint: **who set this, and what would change it?**

That is the whole method. It is one question, it can be asked in an email, and it distinguishes the constraints that are facts about the world from the constraints that are decisions somebody made.

### Four constraints, sorted

The Hillcrest paper's two options exist inside four constraints, three of them unwritten.

**"The capital envelope is fixed at £2.4m for 2027/28."**

Who set it? The annual planning process. What would change it? A case made before the envelope is set, or a mid-year reallocation — and the utility has moved capital between years twice in five years.

**Soft.** It is a real constraint on this paper, at this moment, and it is not a fact about the world. It has an author, a timetable, and a precedent for exceptions.

**"The pump must be replaced like-for-like because of the housing."**

Who set it? Nobody, in the sense of a decision. It is a procurement convention: the standing framework agreement covers like-for-like replacement, so like-for-like is what gets specified.

What would change it? Asking whether anything else fits the housing. **A variable-speed drive does.**

**Dissolves entirely** — and note what it cost. A cheaper alternative that might serve the same objective was eliminated before the paper was drafted, by a convention nobody in the room could have named.

**"Low-pressure incidents must be reported to the regulator."**

Statutory. **Real.** No amount of asking changes it.

**"No works between June and September."**

Peak demand; the works themselves would cause outages during the period the works are meant to protect. **Real**, and physical rather than conventional.

### Two of four

Half the constraints shaping this decision are conventions, and both have people who could be asked.

**And that is the general pattern.** In most organisations, the constraints that feel most immoveable are the ones nobody remembers setting — because a constraint with a visible author invites a conversation, and a constraint with no author is just how things are.

### Why this matters as much as the values

**A constraint accepted without examination decides the outcome while remaining unexaminable.**

That is exactly what §2 said about implicit values, and the parallel is exact. Both are load-bearing. Both operate whether or not anyone states them. Both determine the answer.

**With one difference in your favour: a constraint usually has an author, and a value usually does not.** Nobody can tell you where the organisation's preference for capital projects came from. Somebody can tell you who wrote the framework agreement.

Chapter 5 asked you to keep an assumption record — a list of what the analysis is taking as true, with what would show each one false. **An unexamined constraint is an unrecorded assumption**, sitting in the decision rather than in the analysis, and it belongs on the same list.

### Why conventions harden

There is a mechanism here worth naming, because it explains why the soft constraints are the ones that feel hardest.

**A convention starts as a decision with a reason.** Somebody set up a framework agreement for like-for-like pump replacement, and at the time it was sensible: it covered the common case, it saved procurement effort, and the exceptions were rare.

**Then the reason leaves.** The person who set it up moves on. The document stays. The reason is not written anywhere, because at the time it was obvious.

**And then it becomes a fact about the world.** New engineers arrive and learn that pumps are replaced like-for-like. Nobody tells them why, because nobody knows. It is simply how pumps are replaced, and it has the same standing in their heads as the statutory reporting duty.

**A constraint with no remembered author is indistinguishable from a law of nature**, from the inside. That is why the question is *who set this* rather than *is this really necessary* — the second invites a judgment nobody in the room is qualified to make, and the first is a matter of fact that somebody in procurement can settle before lunch.

And it explains the asymmetry in which constraints get questioned. **The statutory duty gets questioned constantly**, because everyone knows it has an author and the author is the regulator. The procurement convention never does.

### One more asymmetry

There is a reason the constraint question is worth asking even when the answer is *real*.

**Asking costs almost nothing and the payoff is uncapped.**

The like-for-like housing constraint took one email to procurement to dissolve, and dissolving it produced an alternative that may serve the same objective for a fraction of £310,000. The statutory reporting duty took one email to confirm, and confirming it cost nothing and now nobody wonders.

**Both emails were worth sending.** The second produced no change and removed a doubt; the first produced an option.

Compare that with the usual practice, which is to assume all four are real because two obviously are. That practice is free, feels sensible, and left the cheaper option off the paper.

### And an honest limit

Real constraints are real.

This section is not teaching that everything is negotiable, and a reader who leaves it believing that budgets are notional and regulations are optional has learned the wrong thing. Two of the four constraints above survive inspection completely, and no amount of value-focused thinking moves a statutory reporting duty.

**The point is the sorting, not the outcome.** You do not know which constraints are real until you ask, and the cost of asking is an email.

### Task: sort four constraints

Take a decision currently live in your own organisation, and list four things everyone agrees cannot be done.

1. For each, write **who set it**.
2. For each, write **what would change it**.
3. Sort them: physical or statutory, against convention, budget, or timetable.
4. For the soft ones, name the person you would email.

If you cannot name who set a constraint, that is not evidence that it is fundamental. It is usually the opposite.

## 6. Generating Alternatives

Now the third part of the question: what options exist beyond those offered.

### The ordering rule

> "Decision alternatives describe the different avenues that might be taken to achieve a particular objective. As emphasized in Chapter 2, decision alternatives should be considered only after objectives are understood." [@bradley2016structured, p. 54]

**Only after.** Not as a matter of tidiness — because the alternatives are supposed to be *derived from* the objectives, and you cannot derive from something you have not written.

This is not brainstorming. Brainstorming produces a longer list of options that are still answers to whatever question was in the room. Derivation produces options that serve a stated objective, including options nobody would have thought of, because the objective is doing the generating.

The report also notes where alternatives come from, and it is not the analyst: stakeholders "have innovative ideas and a strong local sense of what is threatened, what is creating the threat, what responses are feasible in their community" [@bradley2016structured, p. 54].

### Seven, from three objectives

Take the fundamental objectives from §3 and ask, for each, what would serve it.

| | Alternative | The objective that produced it |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Reline the feeder main | pressure, if Mechanism B is what operates |
| 2 | Fit a **variable-speed drive** to the existing pump | pressure, at lower cost — and it survives the housing constraint that §5 dissolved |
| 3 | Create a pressure-managed sub-zone for the top 40 households | pressure, for exactly the households who lose it |
| 4 | Install local storage at the top of the zone | pressure, without touching the pump at all |
| 5 | Upgrade one comparable zone deliberately and instrument it | pressure later, and evidence now |
| 6 | Targeted contingency for the 40 households on forecast hot days | *adequate pressure* reframed as *water at the tap* |
| 7 | Defer, and instrument Hillcrest through one summer | evidence, at low cost |

**Seven, and the paper had two.**

None of these required a workshop, new data, or a consultant. They came from writing down three objectives and asking what would serve each.

### Pause: which could the paper never have produced?

Before reading on, write two or three sentences.

> Look at the seven. **Which one could the committee paper never have contained, and why?**

---

**Alternative 6.**

Targeted contingency for forty households on forecast hot days — tankering, bottled water, direct contact with the residents most likely to be affected — is not a capital project. It is operational spend, in a different budget, approved by different people, on a different timetable.

**And the paper is a capital paper.**

The artefact's format constrained the option set before anybody thought about options. A capital paper produces capital options, so an intervention that serves the first fundamental objective more directly than any of the others is structurally invisible to the process that generated the choice.

Nobody excluded it. There was no meeting at which alternative 6 was considered and rejected. **The form of the document did the excluding**, which is the most complete kind of exclusion available.

That is worth carrying beyond this case. **Whatever template your organisation uses to present decisions has an option set built into it**, and the options it cannot express are cut off — in the §1 sense — before anyone reads it.

### One alternative you have already met

**Alternative 5 has appeared twice in this book, for entirely different reasons.**

Chapter 7 proposed it as an **identification strategy**. The pump effect was not identified because no zone resembling Hillcrest had ever been upgraded, and deliberately upgrading one such zone — chosen for reasons unrelated to how bad it was — would make an identifying assumption true rather than assumed.

Chapter 9 repeated it as the thing that would settle the **transport** question, since none of five sources contained a comparable case.

Here it arrives as a **decision alternative**, derived from an objective, with no reference to identification or transport at all. *Minimize households without adequate pressure* over the long run is served by knowing what actually works, and the way to know is to try one and watch.

**Two framings, one act.** An analyst reaches it by asking what would make the evidence interpretable. A decision-maker reaches it by asking what serves the objective. They arrive at the same intervention from opposite ends of the book.

*That observation is this book's own, and it is the second of its kind — the first being that Chapter 7's positivity failure and Chapter 9's transport failure are one fact seen from two directions.*

It also has a practical consequence. An organisation that treats analysis and decision as separate activities will have the analyst propose alternative 5 as a research request, which competes for research money and loses. **Proposed as a decision alternative, it competes for capital money, against the £310,000, and it is cheaper.**

### Four moves that generate

Derivation sounds abstract. Here are the moves that actually produced the seven, so you can run them.

**Take each objective and ask what would serve it.** *Minimize households without adequate pressure* is served by more pressure — but also by fewer affected households (alternative 3), by water arriving another way (alternative 6), and by storage that does not need pressure at the moment of demand (alternative 4). One objective, four routes, and only one of them is the pump.

**Take each mechanism and ask what acts on it.** Chapter 2 gave two mechanisms. The paper's Option 1 acts on Mechanism A only. Alternative 1 acts on Mechanism B. **The paper's single option addressed one of two candidate causes**, which Chapter 7 established were both still live.

**Take each constraint you dissolved and ask what it was hiding.** §5 dissolved the like-for-like housing constraint, and alternative 2 fell straight out. **Dissolved constraints generate alternatives directly**, which is why §5 comes before §6.

**Ask what the format cannot express.** Capital paper, so: what would you do if this were not capital? What if it were operational, or a change to a procedure, or nothing at all? Alternatives 6 and 7 come from here.

**None of the four is creativity.** They are questions, applied in order, to material already on the desk.

### Examining an alternative

Once you have a set, the report gives three dimensions:

> "Each decision alternative can be rated for complexity (which affects the amount of time or cost to implement), effectiveness for the proposed objective, and potential consequences to other objectives." [@bradley2016structured, p. 54]

And what drives the first: "The scale …, the number and level of decision-makers required, the funding, and the acceptability within the community all affect the complexity" [@bradley2016structured, p. 54].

**Note the placement of everything Parts I and II taught.**

> "Effectiveness partially requires factual information; this may require a best estimate of which alternative or combination of alternatives is more likely to achieve the objective." [@bradley2016structured, p. 54]

**Partially requires factual information.** Nine chapters of evidential work feed into one of three dimensions of one step of a six-step process.

That is not a demotion. It is a correct placement, and it is worth stating plainly because the change of register in Part III can feel like the book has stopped being serious. It has not. It has established where the serious evidential work fits, which is something the evidential work could not establish about itself.

### Where the conflicts are, and where the chapter stops

> "Most decisions will affect more than one objective." [@bradley2016structured, p. 54]

The report's own example: a marine protected area maximises long-term fishery health but "could maximize or minimize tourism, recreation and scientific learning" and "may also detract from economic opportunities" [@bradley2016structured, p. 54].

The Hillcrest conflicts, now visible:

- **Alternative 1** serves pressure and **worsens** disruption — relining is a long excavation through occupied streets.
- **Alternative 5** serves evidence and **defers** any improvement at Hillcrest for at least a year.
- **Any capital option** serves Hillcrest and **removes money** from three zones scheduled for mains renewal.
- **Alternative 6** serves households cheaply and does **nothing** for reportable incidents — which will matter to whoever owns that objective, even though §3 established it is a means.

**This chapter does not resolve any of them.**

Making the conflict visible is the whole of what §§2–6 have been for. An organisation that has got this far has converted an argument about £310,000 into a statement of what it wants, who it affects, what actually constrains it, and seven ways forward that trade against one another in stateable directions.

Weighing them — and asking what information would be worth buying before deciding — is Chapter 11.

One more thing the report says, which is easy to miss and worth having:

> "If alternatives are not achieving the objectives, it could imply that they should be reformulated." [@bradley2016structured, p. 54]

**A set of alternatives that all score badly is information about the set**, not a counsel of despair. Go back and derive more.

### The combined and contingent ones

Two kinds of alternative deserve naming, because they are almost never on a paper and they are frequently the answer.

**Combinations.** The seven are presented as a list, which invites treating them as exclusive. They are not. Alternative 6 costs little and could run alongside anything; alternative 7 could run alongside alternative 6; alternative 2 could be done now and alternative 1 later if it does not work.

**A list of seven options is really a much larger set**, and papers that present options as mutually exclusive have made an assumption nobody stated.

**Contingent alternatives.** *Do X, and if Y happens, do Z.* Fit the variable-speed drive; instrument the zone through one summer; if pressure at the top does not recover, that is evidence for Mechanism B and the main gets relined.

Contingent alternatives are hard to put on a capital paper because they do not have a single number. They are also frequently the best available action under exactly the conditions Part II described — where the cause is not identified and the evidence does not transport — because they buy an outcome and information at the same time.

**Chapter 1 told you to consider a combined, contingent, or information-gathering alternative when material.** Ten chapters later the reason is visible: those are the shapes that a fixed-option paper structurally cannot contain, and they are exactly the shapes that suit an uncertain situation.

## 7. Cold-Start Practice and Retrieval

### Return to your six-minute answer

Find what you wrote at the start of §1, about what the utility is trying to achieve.

Read it against what you can now produce. Do not score it.

- Does it have an **item of value** and a **direction of preference**?
- Is it a **fundamental** objective, or does asking *why is this important* produce another one?
- Is it about **Hillcrest households**, or about the utility?

Three patterns are common.

Most readers write something close to the paper's own sentence, in slightly better words. *Maintain reliable supply.* *Improve resilience for customers.* Neither passes the format, and noticing that in your own handwriting is worth more than being told.

Some readers write a **means** — replace the failing asset, reduce reportable incidents — and it is usually the one their own organisation would recognise.

A few write something about the forty households. That is the strongest opening answer, and the next question is what *adequate* means to them, which §4 established nobody has asked.

### Independent transfer

Now work an unfamiliar situation, without this chapter, the water case, or any rubric in front of you.

You have been assigned **one** of the forms below. Open only that one.

- [Form A — A university deciding on lecture-capture technology](transfer-form-a.md)
- [Form B — A food manufacturer deciding on an allergen-control upgrade](transfer-form-b.md)

Allow about **45 minutes**.
Every fact you need is supplied. Do not look anything up.

Do not open the other form. You will work it after a delay.

When your response is complete — and only then — use [`transfer-rubric.md`](transfer-rubric.md) to review it.

### Retrieve the procedure from memory

Before looking back, write down how you would examine a paper offering a fixed set of options.

Aim for the sequence, not the wording.

1. What does the paper say it is trying to achieve?
2. Does that statement have an **item of value** and a **direction of preference**?
3. Rewrite it two or three ways that pass. Do the versions choose the same option?
4. For each, ask **why is this important** until the answer is *just because*.
5. Which of the stated objectives turn out to be **means**?
6. Who is **affected**? Aim for six or more.
7. Who was **asked** — about values, not about facts?
8. What does everyone agree cannot be done, and **who set each of those**?
9. Taking each fundamental objective in turn: **what would serve it?** Include things the paper's format cannot express.
10. Where do the objectives **conflict**? State it; do not resolve it.

Step 2 takes fifteen seconds and disqualifies most stated objectives.

Step 7 is the one that reveals whether the organisation keeps one roster or two.

Step 9 is where the alternatives come from, and it works only if steps 1 to 5 were done first.

### If the transfer went badly

- **You produced options rather than objectives.** That is the default the chapter exists to interrupt, and doing it while reading a chapter about it is extremely common.
- **Your objectives were vision statements.** Run the two-part test on each. Most will fail.
- **You listed the people who were consulted.** The roster is who is affected.
- **You accepted the constraints.** Ask who set each one. At least one usually has an author and a precedent for exceptions.
- **You resolved the trade-offs.** That is Chapter 11. Making them visible is enough here, and doing it early hides the fact that a choice was made.

### Delayed retest

After the interval your reading plan specifies, you will work the other form.

Do not reread this chapter immediately beforehand, and do not look at the form you have not done.

### What this chapter did not give you

**Any way to choose.** Seven alternatives, three objectives that conflict, and no method for weighing them. That is Chapter 11's, deliberately.

**Any way to weigh objectives against each other.** Utility, preferences, and trade-off structure are all next chapter.

**Any way to decide what information to buy first.** Value of information is the other half of Chapter 11, and it is the formal answer to the question §2 raised when it said values guide information collection.

**Four of the six properties of a good objective.** The source defining them was not obtained, and inventing definitions would have been worse than leaving pointers.

**Any facilitation technique.** How to run a values workshop with fifty people is a real skill and this is not the book for it.

**And nothing about what happens when people game the attribute.** Chapter 10 established that attributes stand in for objectives. Chapter 15 is about what happens when the people being measured notice.

### What Chapter 11 asks next

You now have what a decision needs to be well-posed: what matters, to whom, what actually constrains it, and what could be done.

You do not have a decision.

Three fundamental objectives point at different alternatives. Seven alternatives trade against one another. And every one of them has consequences nobody can predict with certainty — which is where Parts I and II come back, because the uncertainty in those consequences is exactly what nine chapters taught you to characterise honestly.

Two questions remain, and Chapter 11 is both.

**How do you choose when the objectives conflict and the consequences are uncertain?** Which needs a way to say how much of one objective you would give up for another, and a way to act when you cannot say what will happen.

**And what would it be worth to know more before deciding?** That question has a formal answer, it is frequently *nothing*, and an organisation that could compute it would stop commissioning studies that cannot change what it does.

The utility has spent nine chapters gathering evidence and one chapter working out what it wants. Chapter 11 asks whether it should have bought the evidence at all.
