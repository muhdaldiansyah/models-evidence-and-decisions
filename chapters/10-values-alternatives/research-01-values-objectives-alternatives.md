# Research 01 — Values, Objectives, Alternatives

Cluster R01 of `research-plan.md`. Closed.

Source read directly: `bradley2016structured` printed pp. 5–8.

## 1. The three-term relationship

The report states it as three lines, set out as key concepts:

> - "Values are what we fundamentally care about"
> - "Objectives define what matters in the decision and are based upon values"
> - "Alternatives are means to achieve our objectives"
>
> [@bradley2016structured, p. 5]

And expands it on the same page:

> "Values are what we fundamentally care about in decision-making. Alternatives are means to achieve our objectives, which are based upon our values." [@bradley2016structured, p. 5]

**The report attributes the relationship to Keeney (1992)**, a book this project did not obtain. The framework is therefore used **as reported at** `bradley2016structured`, which is the same device the book applied to Brier, to Matrixx, and to Russell's chicken.

`../../sources/keeney1996valuefocused.md` prohibits attributing value-focused procedures to the 1996 article without full-text verification. **That prohibition is honoured**: nothing in Chapter 10 is attributed to `keeney1996valuefocused` beyond what its verified abstract supports.

## 2. The reversal the chapter is built on

> "Making decisions based on 'what is important' is the basis of value-focused decision-making and is fundamentally distinct from the more common alternative-focused decision-making that emphasizes the range of possible routes to achieve a single, primary objective." [@bradley2016structured, p. 5]

**"The more common."** The failure mode is the default, not an aberration.

And the sharpest sentence in the passage:

> "Alternative-focused decision-making does consider values, but often only implicitly. They may not be clearly stated and thus not fully considered when making a decision." [@bradley2016structured, p. 5]

**Values are always present.** The question is never whether a decision involves values; it is whether they were written down. An option set is already a claim about what matters, made by whoever drafted it, and the claim is unexaminable while it stays implicit.

This is the same structure the book has used repeatedly — Chapter 6's unstated conditioning, Chapter 7's unstated identifying assumption, Chapter 8's unstated analytic conduct. **In every case something load-bearing is present and unwritten**, and the remedy is the same.

The report's figure caption puts the practical consequence plainly: "The common tendency is to start with AFT without first clarifying and explicitly stating the values and criteria that will be used for making a decision" [@bradley2016structured, p. 5].

## 3. Why it is worth the extra step

> "Explicitly stating the values, and by extension the objectives and criteria used to define and measure their attainment, promotes a more transparent, inclusive, and defensible process. It helps to create an environment for fostering options with better prospects for desired outcomes and minimal negative impacts" [@bradley2016structured, p. 6]

And the honest accounting, on the same page: "Although adding an extra step in the decision process, VFT gives decision-makers greater flexibility in finding acceptable solutions to problems" [@bradley2016structured, p. 6].

**The report concedes the cost.** It is an extra step. The chapter should concede it too rather than pretending the discipline is free.

The listed advantages, reported at p. 6–7 from Keeney (1992), include guiding information collection, improving communication, involving stakeholders, recognizing interconnectedness, guiding strategic thinking, and **creating alternatives**: "New alternatives can be created that are directly responsive to stakeholder values" [@bradley2016structured, p. 7].

**The first of those is worth flagging for this book specifically.** Values "help prioritize the spending of limited resources on gathering information relevant to what is important to the stakeholders" [@bradley2016structured, p. 6] — which reverses the order Parts I and II implied. Nine chapters treated evidence as prior; this says what matters is prior to which evidence is worth collecting.

## 4. Where this chapter sits in a decision process

The report gives a generic six-step structure, attributed to Carriger and Benson (2012):

| Step | Description as printed |
|---|---|
| Decision context | "The reason for a decision opportunity" |
| Objectives | "Expressions of what is valued in the decision opportunity" |
| Alternatives | "The choices needed to fulfill the objectives" |
| Prospects | "The potential outcomes from the decisions and their uncertainties" |
| Trade-offs | "The willingness of stakeholders to accept more or less of one objective for another" |
| Recommendations | "The optimal strategy for achieving the objectives" |
>
> [@bradley2016structured, p. 8, Table 2-1]

**This divides Part III cleanly.** Chapter 10 owns the first three. Chapter 11 owns the last three.

That is a useful finding because two of the book's own governing documents divide the boundary differently — `decisions/0006` assigns "trade-off structure" to Chapter 10, while `README.md` gives trade-offs and value of information to Chapter 11. **The table resolves it in favour of Chapter 11 for the machinery**, and `../../decisions/0017` clause 5 proposes that Chapter 10 establishes only that objectives conflict.

The report also notes that a structured-decision-making formulation adds "the additional step of implementing the decision and monitoring to evaluate the results against stated values. Thus, each management decision sets the context for the next round of decision-making" [@bradley2016structured, p. 8]. **That loop is Chapter 17's**, and Chapter 10 names it once.

## 5. One definition worth quoting for its own sake

> "The Latin definition of 'decision' is 'to cut off' (Merriam-Webster 2013). When faced with a choice, a decision-maker considers alternatives from which one is selected and the others are cut off. Resources are then committed to the selected alternative" [@bradley2016structured, p. 5]

**Everything not chosen is cut off.** Which makes the completeness of the alternative set a first-order question rather than a refinement, and gives the chapter its opening image.

The same page notes the condition under which any of this matters: "In an environment with multiple stakeholder perspectives and limited resources, the need to clearly establish what to consider (criteria) for choosing among alternatives is necessary" [@bradley2016structured, p. 5].

## 6. And a limit the source states on itself

> "In many cases, single issue, well-defined decisions do not need a formal methodology for successful outcomes. Everyday decisions (e.g., when to hold a meeting or the best travel route to work) are quickly made with available information and common sense guided by experience." [@bradley2016structured, p. 7]

**The chapter must carry this.** A book that told readers to build an objectives hierarchy before choosing a lunch venue would be teaching ceremony. The report's own test is complexity, multiple viewpoints, and consequential resource commitment.

The report also quotes a definition worth keeping: decision analysis as "a formalization of common sense for decisions that were too complex for the informal use of common sense", attributed there to Keeney (1982) [@bradley2016structured, p. 7].
