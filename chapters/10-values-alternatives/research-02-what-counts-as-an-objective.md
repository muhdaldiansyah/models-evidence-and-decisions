# Research 02 — What Counts as an Objective

Cluster R02 of `research-plan.md`. Closed.

Source read directly: `bradley2016structured` printed pp. 49–51 and 54.

## 1. The finding that justifies the section

> "While many decision-makers say they have clear objectives, what they really have is a messy mix of means and ends, targets, policies and vision statements, most of which are not useful for decision-making (Gregory et al. 2012)." [@bradley2016structured, p. 49]

The report prints this twice on the page — once in the running text and once as a pulled callout — which is as close as an EPA report comes to underlining.

**"Most of which are not useful for decision-making"** is the operative clause. The problem is not that decision-makers lack ambitions. It is that what they have cannot do the job an objective has to do.

And what is that job?

> "Objectives in decision-making have a very specific purpose. They focus decision-makers on what matters in terms of outcomes and become the evaluation criteria for identifying and evaluating alternatives (Gregory et al. 2012). The process of thinking through and writing down objectives helps decision-makers to make more informed decisions." [@bradley2016structured, p. 49]

**Objectives become the evaluation criteria.** Which means a decision with no usable objectives has no criteria, and whatever criteria it ends up applying were supplied by whoever framed the options.

## 2. A testable format

This is the cluster's most valuable finding, because it converts a philosophical-sounding requirement into something a reader can check in five seconds.

> "Objectives are usually described as something that matters (e.g., availability of quality habitat for important species, or quantity of sediment loads into Guánica Bay) and a verb indicating the preferred direction of change (e.g., maximize or minimize)" [@bradley2016structured, p. 50]

Stated as a rule on the same page:

> "Objectives have a direction of preference and an item of value.
> - Maximize the availability of quality habitat for a fish species.
> - Minimize the sediment loading into Guánica Bay." [@bradley2016structured, p. 50]

**Two parts. An item of value, and a direction of preference.**

The report explains why each part is doing work: "The item of value is the key part of the usefulness of objectives in reflecting values. The direction of preference further specifies whether the item of value should be achieved or avoided" [@bradley2016structured, p. 50].

And one further constraint:

> "Objectives are context specific: they are defined for the decision at hand, not for universal usage." [@bradley2016structured, p. 50]

**Which is the book's recurring relativity shape again**, now in Part III. Adequacy for a use, validity for an interpretation, trustworthiness for a quantity, criticism for stakes, probability for information, identification for assumptions, transport for a setting — and objectives for a decision.

### Why this is the section's centrepiece

Take an ordinary organisational aim: *improve service resilience*.

Item of value? "Service resilience" is a category, not a quantity — it does not name something that could be more or less. Direction of preference? "Improve" points somewhere, but at what.

**It fails both halves of a two-part test.** And it is the kind of sentence that appears at the top of committee papers everywhere, is nodded at, and cannot be used to evaluate a single alternative.

The test is cheap, mechanical, and produces an immediate verdict. That combination — cheap, falsifiable, consequential — is the same one Chapter 8 gave for model checks.

## 3. Fundamental and means objectives

> "Objectives in the upper levels of the hierarchy reflect broad or inclusive values–the fundamental objectives. A fundamental objective is usually determined when the answer to 'why is this important' is '…just because'…. meaning that it is simply something that humans need or want." [@bradley2016structured, p. 51]

**The "why is this important?" test.** Keep asking until the answer stops being another objective.

The report's examples of fundamental objectives: "'Maximize ecological integrity', 'maximize human health', and 'minimize management costs' are good examples of broad, encompassing fundamental objectives" [@bradley2016structured, p. 51].

The distinction matters for a specific and practical reason. **Means objectives generate alternatives; fundamental objectives evaluate them.** An organisation that mistakes a means for an end will optimise the means and can achieve it completely while failing at what it actually wanted — which is the structure of a great many organisational failures and is why §6 of the manuscript can generate alternatives at all.

The report also notes the structure is not fixed at the outset: "At first, the OH is normally very broad, and is further refined over time" [@bradley2016structured, p. 51].

## 4. Attributes, and the link to Chapter 3

> "Evaluation measures, attributes that can be used to evaluate performance toward higher-level objectives, are at the bottom of the OH (Keeney 1992). Effective attributes are characterized by their measurability, understandability, and operability." [@bradley2016structured, p. 51]

And the pairing:

> "The objectives and their corresponding attributes form a common vision for what is valued (objectives) and what will be assessed (attributes)." [@bradley2016structured, p. 51]

**What is valued, and what will be assessed. Two different things, deliberately paired.**

That is Chapter 3's ladder in a new setting. Chapter 3 separated the `construct` (what you are trying to measure) from the `measure` (the procedure that produces numbers), and established that choosing a measure does not fix the construct. Here the objective is the thing valued and the attribute is what gets counted, and the same gap opens between them.

**Which is where `metric` gets its warning.** An attribute is a stand-in for an objective. When the stand-in becomes the target — when the organisation starts managing the number rather than the thing — the failure has a name and it is Chapter 15's. Canon already records this forward reference.

## 5. Six properties, reported

> "Good objectives are:
> - Fundamental
> - Complete
> - Concise
> - Sensitive
> - Understandable
> - Independent (Gregory et al. 2012)" [@bradley2016structured, p. 51]

**Gregory et al. (2012) was not obtained**, so the list is used as reported and the report gives no gloss on the six terms. The chapter should therefore either use the list as a checklist without inventing definitions, or use only the properties it can explain from other material in the source.

**Recommendation:** quote the list once, attributed, and develop only **Fundamental** (p. 51's test) and **Independent** (which p. 54's "most alternatives will affect more than one objective" bears on). Inventing glosses for *Sensitive* and *Concise* would be attributing content to an unobtained source.

## 6. Value of the hierarchy

> "one is that it represents a common vision—most stakeholders will agree on objectives high in the hierarchy even if not specific approaches for reaching an objective." [@bradley2016structured, p. 54]

**This is the most politically useful sentence in the chapter's source material.** Disagreement in a room is usually about alternatives; agreement is usually available higher up. A group that cannot agree whether to upgrade a pump can generally agree that it wants fewer households without water on hot days.

Also recorded: "objectives low in the hierarchy usually present opportunities for measuring performance", and that the transparency of a hierarchy "helps to avoid future confusion and miscommunication" [@bradley2016structured, p. 54].
