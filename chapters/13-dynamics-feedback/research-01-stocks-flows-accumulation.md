# Research 01: Stocks, Flows, and Accumulation

Cluster 1 of four. Every locator below was taken from reading the document directly.

## 1. The definition, and what it excludes

`sterman2006evidence` p. 508 introduces stocks and flows by naming their cognates rather than by defining them formally:

> "Stocks and the flows that alter them (the concepts of prevalence and incidence in epidemiology) are fundamental in disciplines from accounting to zoology: a population is increased by births and decreased by mortality; the burden of mercury in a child's body is increased by ingestion and decreased by excretion."

Two things follow that matter for the chapter.

**The stock/flow distinction is not domain-specific.** Prevalence and incidence, balance and cash flow, population and births are the same distinction under four sets of words. That is the reason to teach it once rather than per domain.

**The flows are named in pairs.** Increased by, decreased by. A stock with only an inflow named is a stock whose analysis has not been finished.

Same page, on what stocks are made of in non-physical systems:

> "In physical and biological systems, resources are usually tangible: the stock of glucose in the blood; the number of active smokers in a population. The performance of public health systems, however, is also determined by resources such as physician skills, patient knowledge, community norms, and other forms of human, social, and political capital."

**Usable for the chapter's boundary**, and a caution: the book's anchor stock is physical, so the chapter should name the intangible case once and not build on it.

## 2. The accumulation claim, which is the chapter's centre

`sterman2006evidence` p. 508:

> "Research shows people's intuitive understanding of stocks and flows is poor in two ways. First, narrow mental model boundaries mean that people are often unaware of the networks of stocks and flows that supply resources and absorb wastes."

And the second way, which is the one the chapter teaches:

> "Second, people have poor intuitive understanding of the process of accumulation. Most people assume that system inputs and outputs are correlated (e.g., the higher the federal budget deficit, the greater the national debt will be). However, stocks integrate (accumulate) their net inflows. A stock rises even as its net inflow falls, as long as the net inflow is positive: the national debt rises even as the deficit falls—debt falls only when the government runs a surplus; the number of people living with HIV continues to rise even as incidence falls—prevalence falls only when infection falls below mortality."

**This passage carries the whole of §2 and §3.** It states the error, names its form (assuming inputs and outputs are correlated), states the correct principle (stocks integrate their net inflows), and gives two worked instances in different domains.

**Extraction note.** The passage survives cleanly and contains no comparison symbols; "greater", "falls below", and "equal" are all in words. It is quotable in full.

## 3. The bathtub, and the conservation argument

`sterman2006evidence` pp. 508–509, on beliefs about greenhouse gas concentrations:

> "Such beliefs are analogous to arguing that a bathtub filled faster than it drains will never overflow. They violate conservation of matter, and the violation matters"

**Useful and slightly dangerous.** The image is memorable and the chapter should use it, but the *reason* it is wrong is conservation, not intuition — and stating the reason is what stops the image becoming a slogan.

## 4. Why the same page supports the "wait and see" failure

Same page:

> "Such wait-and-see policies erroneously presume that climate change can be reversed quickly should harm become evident, underestimating immense delays in the climate's response to GHG emissions."

**This is the accumulation argument reaching a decision**, which is how Chapter 13 connects to Part III rather than sitting beside it. A stock with a long delay cannot be managed by waiting for evidence, because by the time the evidence arrives the stock has already accumulated.

The chapter should make this its own argument about the water case and cite the source for the general form.

## 5. Irreversibility

`sterman2006evidence` p. 507, in the list of why systems are policy-resistant:

> "History-dependent. Many actions are irreversible: you can't unscramble an egg (the second law of thermodynamics). Stocks and flows (accumulations) and long time delays often mean doing and undoing have fundamentally different time constants"

**"Doing and undoing have fundamentally different time constants"** is quotable and is the sharpest available statement of why a stock is not a dial.
