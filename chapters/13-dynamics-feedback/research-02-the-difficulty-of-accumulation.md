# Research 02: The Difficulty of Accumulation — the empirical warrant

Cluster 2 of four. Every locator below was taken from reading the document directly.

This dossier exists because `decisions/0007` made `boothsweeney2000bathtub` conditional: it "remains an optional pedagogical source and is not promoted unless Chapter 1 makes an explicit empirical claim about learner difficulty with stock-flow or delay reasoning."

**Chapter 13 makes exactly that claim**, so the condition is met and the source was obtained in full.

## 1. What the study was

`boothsweeney2000bathtub` p. 249, abstract:

> "The inventory consists of brief tasks designed to assess particular systems thinking concepts such as feedback, delays, and stocks and flows. Initial findings indicate that subjects from an elite business school with essentially no prior exposure to system dynamics concepts have a poor level of understanding of stock and flow relationships and time delays."

And, on what did *not* predict performance:

> "Performance did not vary systematically with prior education, age, national origin, or other demographic variables."

**Subjects** (p. 264): MIT Sloan graduate students, "highly international, with 35 countries represented", of whom "more than half the subjects had played the beer game as part of Sloan's MBA orientation program."

`boothsweeney2000bathtub` p. 264, Table 2, mean fraction of correct coding criteria:

| Task | N | Mean |

Same page, stated plainly by the authors:

> "Table 2 summarizes overall performance. In general, performance is poor."

## 3. Which specific ideas failed

`boothsweeney2000bathtub` p. 264, on the simplest task:

> "They did worst on items 6 and 7, which test the basic concepts that the net rate is the slope of the stock and that the area enclosed by the net rate in any interval is the quantity added to the stock during the interval. One fifth did not correctly show the stock rising (falling) when the inflow was greater than (less than) the outflow. More than a fifth failed to show the stock rising and falling linearly during each segment, though the net rate was constant. Nearly two fifths failed to relate the net flow over each interval to the change in the stock."

The individual criterion scores from Table 3, p. 265, for the **simplest** task:

- "When the inflow exceeds the outflow, the stock is rising" — **0.80**
- "The slope of the stock during each segment is the net rate" — **0.66**
- "The quantity added to (removed from) the stock during each segment is the area enclosed by the net rate" — **0.63**

**Extraction note.** Table 3's criterion text contains parenthesised symbolic glosses that mangle under extraction. The criterion sentences themselves are clean and are quoted without their parentheses; the mangled fragments are not quoted anywhere.

## 4. The authors' own answer to the obvious objection

`boothsweeney2000bathtub` p. 265:

> "These concepts are the most basic and intuitive features of accumulation. Further, they are the fundamental concepts of calculus, a subject all MIT students are required to have. It is possible that their poor performance arose from numerical errors in the required computations, but the arithmetic required is modest and examination of the responses suggests conceptual confusion, not arithmetical error."

**This is why the source is worth having.** It anticipates and answers the two dismissals a reader will reach for — *they hadn't studied it* and *they made arithmetic slips*.

## 5. The finding, stated by the authors

`boothsweeney2000bathtub` p. 278:

> "The results strongly suggest that highly educated subjects with extensive training in mathematics and science have poor understanding of some of the most basic concepts of system dynamics, specifically stocks and flows, time delays, and feedback. The errors are highly systematic and indicate violations of basic principles, not merely calculation errors."

And the specific error shape, same page:

> "Many subjects appear to believe that the stock trajectory should have the same qualitative shape as the net rate."

**That sentence names the misconception precisely**, and it is the one the chapter's own accumulation exercise is built to expose.

## 6. Cautions — recorded, and carried into the manuscript

**Single institution, one student body.** p. 264 says the demographics "are typical of the Sloan School's student body." Nothing about the general population follows.

**Convenience sample, voluntary demographics.** p. 278: "Reporting of demographic data was voluntary; as a result of missing data, the degrees of freedom for each factor may differ across tasks."

**The demographic analysis is reported with threshold verdicts, and the book does not repeat them that way.** p. 278 reports that males outperformed females on all three tasks "though the effect is only marginally significant", that region of origin was "marginally significant" on two tasks, and that "The results provide only limited support for the hypothesis that prior training in the sciences helps performance."

**Chapter 8's discipline applies to the book's own sources.** The chapter uses the study's *measurements* — 0.77, 0.48, 0.41, and the per-criterion figures — and does not repeat any of its significance verdicts. The gender comparison is not used at all: it is a marginal result on a convenience sample, reported without an effect size in the text, and nothing in this chapter needs it.

**Published in 2000.** The chapter says so.
