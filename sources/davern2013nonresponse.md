## Role in Chapter 4

This source defeats one specific and very common collapse: treating a response rate as a measure of how biased a dataset is.

It gives Chapter 4 a checkable correction — the headline number that everyone reports about a dataset's completeness is a poor guide to whether that dataset will mislead you.

## Verified locators

- *(verbatim)* "Response rates lack validity in that there is not even a moderate correlation with nonresponse bias."
- *(verbatim)* "nonresponse bias is an estimate level measure and it cannot be easily summarized by a survey level proxy measure."
- *(paraphrase)* Bias arises at the level of an individual estimate rather than at the level of a survey; what matters is how respondents and nonrespondents differ **on the particular variable being estimated**, so one survey with one response rate can carry serious bias for one estimate and none for another.
- *(paraphrase)* A survey run with "a less aggressive protocol and achieving a lower response rate" produced results where "the approximately 40 percent reduction in response rate would not have produced one statistically different result."
- *(paraphrase)* The article draws on Groves (2006), which demonstrated substantial variability in nonresponse bias across estimates within the same survey at the same response rate.

## Chapter 4 use and cautions

**Do not read this as "response rates do not matter".** The claim is that the response rate is a poor *indicator of bias*, not that nonresponse is harmless. A low response rate is a reason to investigate, not a measurement of the damage.

**Do not generalize beyond nonresponse.** The article concerns survey nonresponse. Chapter 4 also treats selection, censoring, and administrative records, and this source speaks to none of those.

**Groves (2006) was not read.** It is cited here as reported by Davern; do not cite it directly.

**Domain.** Health services research and survey methodology. The book's application to administrative and operational records is the book's own extension.

The estimate-level point is the one worth carrying: **a dataset is not biased or unbiased as a whole.** It can support one quantity well and another badly, which is why Chapter 4 asks what the records were produced *for*.
