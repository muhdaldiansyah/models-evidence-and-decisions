# Source note: `colyvan2016voi`

## Bibliographic record

Colyvan, Mark. 2016. "Value of Information and Monitoring in Conservation Biology." *Environment Systems and Decisions* 36(3): 302–309. DOI 10.1007/s10669-016-9603-8. Published online 18 July 2016.

Read from the author-hosted Springer e-offprint, **which carries the published pagination 302–309 printed on its pages**. All locators below are published page numbers, checked against the running headers.

## Verification status

Read directly at printed pp. 302–306 and footnote 16 at p. 308. **Pages 306 onward were read only far enough to establish that the paper moves into cooperation games**, which is Chapter 15's material and which this book does not use.

## Verified locators

### The premise the paper opposes

- **p. 302**, abstract: "It is a common assumption that more and better quality data will lead to better conservation management decisions. Indeed, this assumption lies behind, and motivates, a great deal of current work in conservation biology. Of course, more data can lead to better decisions in some cases but decision-theoretic models of the value of information show that this need not always be the case: sometimes the cost of data collection is too high."
- **p. 302**, opening line: "It is natural to think that more information will lead to better decision making and thus better outcomes."

### The two-interval example

- **p. 302**: on a bet paying $20 for heads and costing $10 otherwise, with the probability of heads known only to lie between 0.4 and 0.6 — "a simple sensitivity analysis of the decision model here shows that the expected utility of accepting the bet is greater than rejecting it. No further information is required in deciding whether to accept this bet or not."
- **p. 302**: with the interval 0.2 to 0.4 instead, "Because the interval [0.2, 0.4] straddles the critical value of 1/3, all we can say is that if the probability of heads is between 1/3 and 0.4, we should accept the bet but not otherwise."
- **pp. 302–303**: in that second case "sensitivity analysis does not help in deciding whether to accept the bet."

### Zero value, and the ceiling

- **p. 303**: "some medical diagnostic tests have this character. Radiographing a suspected broken toe costs time and money (not to mention the radiation exposure to the patient), and yet the treatment for a bruised toe or a broken toe is the same: strap it and avoid any activities that hurt the toe. The value of information delivered by the radiographic examination in this decision about treatment is zero."
- **p. 303**: "Of course the same information might be valuable for other purposes."
- **p. 303**: "the value of imperfect information is always less than that of perfect information."
- **p. 303**: "The bottom line is that it does not always pay to get the best information available—sometimes the price is too high."
- **p. 303**, on a fair-coin bet with an uncertain prize: "The expected monetary value of accepting the bet is the same as not accepting it; it's $0 and decision theory recommends indifference between accepting and not accepting the bet."

### The worked application and the summary

- **p. 304**, reporting Rhodes et al. (2011) on koala monitoring: "It was shown that there is little value in additional monitoring; the money spent on monitoring would be better spent directly on conservation efforts: '[I]f resolving uncertainty costs more than 1.7 % of the koala management budget, it would be more cost-effective to allocate that money to direct management action now.'"
- **p. 304**: "Reducing ignorance is not the name of the game, it's improving management decisions."
- **p. 304**: "As always in value of information studies, it depends on what you're going to do with the information."
- **p. 304**: "as with many formal decision tools, value of information studies need to be used with a certain amount of caution."

### Limitations

- **p. 305**, §3.1: "Finding the appropriate formulation of a value of information decision can be difficult and requires careful reflection on what the aim of the exercise is and to what purposes we might put the data in question."
- **p. 305**, §3.2: "value of information studies generally need to trade in a common currency—typically monetary value—but, as we are well aware, we need to deal with other kinds of value attached to conservation outcomes"; and "value of information studies typically do require a single currency for the relevant values, be it monetary value, a biodiversity measure, or whatever."
- **p. 305**: "funds not spent on data collection are not always available (in their entirety) for conservation efforts. For example, resources cannot always be (costlessly) reallocated. Sometimes funds are tied to particular data collection projects and cannot be reallocated to conservation interventions at all."
- **p. 305**, reporting Møller and Fiedler (2010): amateur bird records "are now valuable components of long-term data sets, painting a clear picture of changes in bird numbers and species over long time periods"; and "It is all too easy to overlook such value in a narrowly-construed value of information study conducted at the time of data collection."

### Three kinds of information gathering, and the cost of the study

- **p. 306**: "(1) information gathered for pure science, for intellectual curiosity and with no intended benefits for practical decisions (2) information gathered for the purposes of improving a specific well-defined decision and (3) information gathered because it might be useful for some ill-defined or unknown decision down the track."
- **p. 306**: "Surely there should be a place for (1), pure scientific research, and it might be argued that such research is not an appropriate target for value of information studies. The question is how to guard against cases of (3) which might slip though under the guise of (1) or gesturing towards (2)".
- **p. 308, footnote 16**: "In some cases we also need to factor in the cost of the value of information study itself. Sometimes these studies require considerable resources (additional scenario modelling and the like) and this cost should not be ignored."

## Role in Chapter 11

- supplies the premise the chapter exists to interrogate, stated as the motivating assumption of a research programme rather than as an error;
- supplies the two-interval example, which shows that what matters is whether the uncertainty straddles the point at which the best act changes;
- supplies the broken-toe case: informative, accurate, and worth nothing for the decision at hand;
- supplies the perfect-information ceiling as a screening rule;
- supplies four documented limitations, of which the single-currency requirement bears hardest on this book because Chapter 10 established that decisions have several conflicting objectives;
- supplies the three kinds of information gathering, and the warning that most organisational collection is the third;
- supplies, at footnote 16, the fact that the analysis itself costs something.

## Cautions

**Cited within and not obtained:** Rhodes et al. (2011), Møller and Fiedler (2010), Colyvan et al. (2011), Raiffa (1968), Gould (1974). All are used **as reported** or not at all.

**Do not use this paper for game theory.** It reaches cooperation games at p. 306; that material is Chapter 15's and was not read.

**Do not present value of information as a settled routine.** The paper says such studies "need to be used with a certain amount of caution" (p. 304) and devotes a section to limitations.

**Do not use the paper to argue against data collection.** Its position is that value depends on the decision, not that information is generally worthless — and it explicitly defends a place for research not aimed at any decision (p. 306).

**The conservation setting is the paper's own.** This book takes the framework and does not import the ecological examples as its own case material; the koala and bird-watching cases appear only as reported illustrations.
