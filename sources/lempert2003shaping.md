# Source note: `lempert2003shaping`

## Bibliographic record

Lempert, Robert J., Steven W. Popper, and Steven C. Bankes. 2003. *Shaping the Next One Hundred Years: New Methods for Quantitative, Long-Term Policy Analysis*. MR-1626-RPC. Santa Monica, CA: RAND Corporation.

Freely distributed by RAND. Read from the RAND PDF, **which carries printed pagination**. Printed page = PDF page − 22, checked against running headers.

## Verification status

Read directly at printed **pp. 39–40, 52–53, and 57–58**, with the table of contents inspected. **Chapters Four through Seven and both appendices were not read**, and nothing in this book characterises them — including the XLRM framework, the Wonderland scenario generator, and the study's own application to global sustainability.

## Verified locators

### The pivot: optimality against robustness

- **p. 39**: "Rather than first predicting the future in order to act on it, decisionmakers may now gain a systematic understanding of their best near-term options for shaping a long-term future in the absence of any reliable predictions."
- **p. 40**: "a robust decision approach allows analysts to use the computer to lay out a wide range of plausible paths into the long term. They then look for robust near-term policy options—i.e., those that when compared to the alternatives perform reasonably well across a wide range of futures using many different values to assess performance."
- **p. 40**: "Often strategies are robust because they are adaptive—that is, they are explicitly designed to evolve in response to new information."
- **p. 52**: "What criterion should decisionmakers use to compare alternative solutions for long-term policy challenges? Traditional decision analysis seeks the optimal strategy, that is, the one that performs best for a fixed set of assumptions about the future. In contrast, LTPA requires a standard that allows analysts to make policy arguments true across a multiplicity of unpredictable futures."

### Robustness, defined

- **p. 52**: "An argument that is insensitive to significant variation in its underlying assumptions is said to be robust. In this sense, a strategy should be considered robust if it performs reasonably well compared to the alternatives across a wide range of plausible futures."
- **pp. 52–53**: "The use of the normative concept of performing 'well' therefore also suggests robustness should be assessed with respect to the many different value systems for assessing the performance of the strategies."
- **p. 53**: "For many decisionmakers, robustness represents the normative criterion one ought to use when confronting deep uncertainty."
- **p. 53**: the concept's stated virtues — a computationally convenient basis, a normative description of good choices under deep uncertainty and multiple stakeholders, and "it matches the criteria decisionmakers often use when faced with such conditions."

### Scenario ensembles

- **p. 52**: "Scenario ensembles are powerful artifacts for eliciting information from groups and for communicating information about risk to wide audiences."
- **p. 52**: "Ensembles also offer compelling alternative futures that can force stakeholders to question their assumptions and provide a framework to understand the views of others who might hold very different expectations about the future."
- **p. 52**: "the diversity requirement that guides the construction of scenario ensembles is crucial to building credibility among parties to a decision."
- **p. 52**: plausibility judgments are embodied as constraints — "certain paths are excluded because they violate known principles of economics".

### Regret, as reported

- **p. 53**: "This concept of robustness draws from, but is not identical to, L. J. Savage's criterion of minimizing the maximum regret. Savage describes his mini-max rule as a practical rule of thumb for cases where individuals or groups are 'vague' about the probabilities they attach to certain events and where all parties to the decision are certain that, for some strategies, the largest degree of loss will still seem acceptably small."
- **p. 53, footnote 13**, Savage's own pathologies as reported: "the rule often yields neither a best strategy nor a simple ordering among strategies. Furthermore, in a group context, the rule can be undemocratic because the importance of a view is independent of the number of people who hold it. Participants can easily manipulate outcomes by lying about the weights they assign to alternative futures. In some cases, the mini-max rule can be too sensitive to low-probability, high-consequence events, thereby producing clearly unreasonable results."

### Adaptive strategies

- **p. 57**: "People learn. Over time, they will gain new information. Accordingly, adaptive decision strategies are the means most commonly used to achieve robustness because they are designed to evolve in response to new data. Faced with a multiplicity of plausible futures, a decisionmaker may settle on near-term actions but plan to adjust them in specific ways as new information renders some futures implausible and others more likely."
- **p. 57**: "one is rarely fortunate enough to engage in LTPA that results in an ideal strategy with good performance properties in all plausible futures judged by all relevant value systems. In practice, long-term decisionmaking becomes an exercise in juggling difficult trade-offs..."
- **p. 57**: "they should emerge with a robust strategy and a clear understanding of the values and futures for which it performs adequately. They should also be explicitly aware of the futures and values that, by virtue of selecting the candidate strategy, have been implicitly classed as unimportant."
- **p. 58**, reporting Dewar: "Dewar's Assumption-Based Planning process (1993, 2001) provides a framework for designing adaptive strategies. This approach comprises shaping actions intended to influence the future that comes to pass, hedging actions intended to reduce vulnerability if adverse futures come to pass, and signposts or observations that warn of the need to change the mix of actions."

## Role in Chapter 12

- supplies the chapter's pivot: optimality is defined relative to a fixed set of assumptions, and robustness is the standard that replaces it when those assumptions are in doubt;
- supplies the many-value-systems clause, which lets Chapter 10's plural objectives survive into Part III's arithmetic instead of being flattened as Chapter 11 had to flatten them;
- supplies regret and, unusually, the rule's own author's objections to it;
- supplies the adaptivity/robustness link and the shaping/hedging/signposts structure;
- supplies the closing discipline: name the futures your robust choice has implicitly classed as unimportant.

## Cautions

**Cited within and not obtained:** Savage (1950), Dewar (1993, 2001), Rosenhead (1989), Gupta and Rosenhead (1972), Walters (1986), Trigeorgis (1996), Payne et al. (1993), Klein (1998). All are used **as reported** or not at all.

**Do not present minimax regret as this book's recommended rule.** The source explicitly says robustness "draws from, but is not identical to" it, and reports four pathologies from Savage himself.

**Do not import the method.** The XLRM framework, the scenario generator, and the study's software are on unread pages and are method-specific.

**The source is twenty-three years old at the time of writing** and its claims about computing capability are dated. This book uses its conceptual apparatus, not its technology claims.
