## What this source is, and what it is not

**It is the first author's own retrospective commentary**, published twelve years after the paper it discusses. It reproduces the 1981 abstract verbatim and adds an account of how the finding emerged and how it was received.

**It is not the study.**

**The 1981 paper could not be obtained.** Three routes were attempted and recorded in `../chapters/16-integration-full-loop/research-plan.md`: the publisher's full text (403), a university mirror (403), and a Defense Technical Information Center report version returning a PDF with no extractable text.

**Chapter 16's central empirical warrant therefore rests on the author's own commentary rather than on the paper**, which is weaker than this book prefers. Recorded here, in the research plan, and at `../decisions/0023`.

## Role in Chapter 16

Supplies the description of what triage *is*: the first thing anybody does with an unfamiliar problem is decide what kind of problem it is, and there is more than one basis for doing so — what the problem is about, or what it needs.

## Verified locators

All from the single page.

- **The 1981 abstract, reproduced**: "The mental representation of physics problems was investigated in experts and novices. Results from sorting tasks and protocols revealed that experts initiated problem solving by abstracting physics principles, suggesting that their problem schema were organized around the principles, whereas novices approached problem solving on the basis of the problem's literal surface features."
- Chi's retrospective: "It was clear that novices sorted together problems that 'looked' alike, but experts did not."
- "This suggested that novices sorted according to the concrete objects mentioned in the problems (e.g., pulleys); but it was not clear how experts were sorting."
- "We concluded that experts mentally represented and directed their problem solving by the 'deep' principles embodied in the problems."
- **The author's own hedge, twelve years later**: "Although this interpretation must be correct at some level, we continue to elaborate on the characterization of the experts' deep level of representation of physics concepts."
- The generality claim, with its parenthesis: "The basic expert-novice result, that experts' knowledge is represented at a 'deep' level (however one characterizes 'deep'), while novices' knowledge is represented at a more concrete level, has been replicated in many domains, ranging from knowledge possessed by scientists to taxi drivers."
- The document also records that the 1981 paper "has been cited in more than 375 publications, making it the most-cited paper published in this journal."

## Cautions

- **A retrospective commentary, not the study.** The manuscript says so where it uses it.
- **Physics.** The book applies the surface/structure distinction to a different kind of problem entirely, and that widening is the book's own pedagogical synthesis.
- **The author's hedge must travel with the finding.** "However one characterizes 'deep'" and "we continue to elaborate" are the author's own words about her own central term.
- **The citation count is a fact about influence, not about evidence.** Chapter 9 established that agreement among dependent sources is cheap; 375 citations of one paper are not 375 findings, and the manuscript must not use the number as corroboration.
- **The population claim does not survive replication unchanged.** See `masonsingh2016categorization`, which found a much wider distribution among introductory students and states that calling them all novices is not appropriate. **Neither source may be used without the other.**
- Nothing is claimed about how expertise is acquired, which this source does not address.
