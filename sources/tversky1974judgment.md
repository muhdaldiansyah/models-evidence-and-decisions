# Source note: `tversky1974judgment`

## Citation

Tversky, Amos, and Daniel Kahneman. 1974. "Judgment under Uncertainty: Heuristics and Biases." *Science*, New Series, 185 (4157): 1124–1131.

## Verification status

Verified direct source. A JSTOR scan of the published article was inspected. The front matter prints *Science*, New Series, Vol. 185, No. 4157, 27 September 1974, pp. 1124–1131, published by the American Association for the Advancement of Science. The authors are identified as members of the department of psychology at the Hebrew University, Jerusalem.

Printed pages **1124–1125** were read in full. Pages 1126–1131 were **not** inspected in this pass and may not be cited.

**Author order.** The article prints "Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman", Tversky first. Many secondary references reverse this. The bibliography follows the printed order.

## Role in Chapter 6

This is Chapter 6's source for base rates and for what people actually do with prior probabilities when evidence arrives. It supplies experimental results, not exhortation, which is what makes it usable — the chapter can show that base-rate neglect is a documented pattern rather than a folk warning.

## Verified locators

- p. 1124, subtitle: "Biases in judgments reveal some heuristics of thinking under uncertainty."
- p. 1124: "people rely on a limited number of heuristic principles which reduce the complex tasks of assessing probabilities and predicting values to simpler judgmental operations. In general, these heuristics are quite useful, but sometimes they lead to severe and systematic errors."
- p. 1124, representativeness: "probabilities are evaluated by the degree to which A is representative of B, that is, by the degree to which A resembles B."
- p. 1124, "Insensitivity to prior probability of outcomes": "One of the factors that have no effect on representativeness but should have a major effect on probability is the prior probability, or base-rate frequency, of the outcomes."
- p. 1124, the engineers-and-lawyers experiment: subjects were told the group consisted of 70 engineers and 30 lawyers, or of 30 engineers and 70 lawyers. "In a sharp violation of Bayes' rule, the subjects in the two conditions produced essentially the same probability judgments."
- p. 1125: "The subjects used prior probabilities correctly when they had no other information. … However, prior probabilities were effectively ignored when a description was introduced, even when this description was totally uninformative."
- **p. 1125, the sharpest formulation: "Evidently, people respond differently when given no evidence and when given worthless evidence. When no specific evidence is given, prior probabilities are properly utilized; when worthless evidence is given, prior probabilities are ignored."**
- p. 1125, "Insensitivity to sample size", the two-hospitals problem: of the respondents, 21 chose the larger hospital, 21 the smaller, and 53 "about the same". The correct answer follows because "a large sample is less likely to stray from 50 percent."
- p. 1125, the urn problem: "the correct posterior odds are 8 to 1 for the 4:1 sample and 16 to 1 for the 12:8 sample, assuming equal prior probabilities. However, most people feel that the first sample provides much stronger evidence".
- p. 1125: "The underestimation of the impact of evidence has been observed repeatedly in problems of this type. It has been labeled 'conservatism.'"
- p. 1125, "Misconceptions of chance": the gambler's fallacy is described, with the correction that "deviations are not 'corrected' as a chance process unfolds, they are merely diluted."

## Chapter 6 use and cautions

**The "worthless evidence" result is the chapter's best single finding** and should be quoted rather than paraphrased. It is more surprising and more useful than the generic claim that people neglect base rates: they do not neglect them always, they abandon them precisely when handed something that looks like information and is not.

**Do not present the heuristics as simply defective.** p. 1124 says explicitly that they "are quite useful, but sometimes they lead to severe and systematic errors." A chapter that treats intuition as merely broken would misreport the source and would also be poor pedagogy.

**Do not cite beyond p. 1125.** Availability and anchoring are on later pages that were not read.

**Do not extend to debiasing.** Nothing in the inspected pages says the biases can be trained away, and a substantial later literature disputes how far they can. That literature was not read and must not be characterized.

**Do not use this source for anything about decisions.** It concerns judgments of probability. Decision-making under uncertainty is Chapter 11.

**Domain caution.** The experiments are laboratory studies with student subjects on stylised problems. The book's application to professional analytical work is an extension, and a defensible one, but it is the book's extension.
