# Source note: `platt1964strong`

## Citation

Platt, John R. 1964. "Strong Inference." *Science*, New Series, 146 (3642): 347–353.

## Verification status

Verified direct source. A JSTOR scan of the published article was inspected. The masthead prints "16 October 1964, Volume 146, Number 3642", the running foot prints "16 OCTOBER 1964" and page 347, and the author is identified as professor of biophysics and physics at the University of Chicago. A footnote records that the article is the text of an address to the Division of Physical Chemistry of the American Chemical Society, September 1963, under the title "The New Baconians."

Printed pages **347–348** were read in full. Pages 349–353 were **not** inspected in this pass and may not be cited.

## Role in Chapter 5

This is Chapter 5's spine source. It supplies a method that maps almost exactly onto the chapter's governed competence: devise alternatives, devise something that would exclude one or more of them, and carry it out.

It also supplies the chapter's sharpest single line about what makes a conclusion secure.

## Verified locators

- p. 347, subtitle: "Certain systematic methods of scientific thinking may produce much more rapid progress than others."
- p. 347, the method: "Strong inference consists of applying the following steps to every problem in science, formally and explicitly and regularly: 1) Devising alternative hypotheses; 2) Devising a crucial experiment (or several of them), with alternative possible outcomes, each of which will, as nearly as possible, exclude one or more of the hypotheses; 3) Carrying out the experiment so as to get a clean result; 1′) Recycling the procedure, making subhypotheses or sequential hypotheses to refine the possibilities that remain; and so on."
- p. 347: "It is like climbing a tree. At the first fork, we choose—or in this case, 'nature' or the experimental outcome chooses—to go to the right branch or the left; at the next fork, to go left or right; and so on."
- p. 347: the schema is described as a "conditional inductive tree" or "logical tree", attributed to Francis Bacon.
- p. 347: "Steps 1 and 2 require intellectual inventions, which must be cleverly chosen so that hypothesis, experiment, outcome, and exclusion will be related in a rigorous syllogism".
- p. 347: "What the formal schema reminds us to do is to try to make these inventions, to take the next step, to proceed to the next fork, without dawdling or getting tied up in irrelevancies."
- **p. 347, the load-bearing line: "Any conclusion that is not an exclusion is insecure and must be rechecked."**
- p. 347: "For exploring the unknown, there is no faster method; this is the minimum sequence of steps."
- p. 348, on what people actually do: "How many of us write down our alternatives and crucial experiments every day, focusing on the *exclusion* of a hypothesis? We may write out our scientific papers so that it looks as if we had steps 1, 2, and 3 in mind all along. But in between, we do busywork. We become 'method-oriented' rather than 'problem-oriented.'"
- p. 348: "we do not realize the added power that the regular and explicit use of alternative hypotheses and sharp exclusions could give us at every step of our research."
- p. 348, the engine analogy: "The difference between the average scientist's informal methods and the methods of the strong-inference users is somewhat like the difference between a gasoline engine that fires occasionally and one that fires in steady sequence."
- p. 348, the practice made concrete: Lederberg is credited with giving "a list of nine propositions 'subject to denial,' discussing which ones would be 'most vulnerable to experimental test'"; and Jacob and Monod are quoted writing "Our conclusions … might be invalid if … (i) … (ii) … or (iii)…. We shall describe experiments which eliminate these alternatives."

## Chapter 5 use and cautions

**The three steps are the chapter's organising method** and may be taught as such, attributed.

**"Any conclusion that is not an exclusion is insecure" is the chapter's strongest line** and should be quoted rather than paraphrased.

**The Lederberg and Jacob–Monod examples give the reader a practice, not just a principle:** write the list of things that would make your conclusion invalid, then say what would eliminate each. That is exactly the artifact Chapter 5 asks readers to produce.

### Cautions

**This is an argument, not a result.** It is the text of an after-dinner-style address, it is polemical in places, and it asserts from the author's reading of molecular biology and physics rather than demonstrating anything. Cite it as an influential methodological argument.

**It assumes crucial experiments are available.** Much of this book concerns decisions where no crucial experiment can be run — a utility cannot rerun a drought. Chapter 5 must adapt the schema honestly: the reader devises alternatives and names *what observation would exclude one*, and often the honest finding is that the discriminating observation is not obtainable. That adaptation is **the book's own** and must not be attributed to this source.

**The paper is contested.** A substantial later literature disputes how well strong inference describes or should describe scientific practice. That literature was **not read** and must not be characterized; the manuscript should say only that the paper is influential and has been debated.

**Do not import the disciplinary polemic.** Pages 347–348 contain sharp remarks about fields that do and do not move quickly, and a reported dispute among named scientists. None of that belongs in this book.

**Do not cite beyond p. 348.** Pages 349–353 were not read.
