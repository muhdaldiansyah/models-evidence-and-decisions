# Source note: `levins1966strategy`

## Citation

Levins, Richard. 1966. "The Strategy of Model Building in Population Biology." *American Scientist* 54 (4): 421–431.

## Verification status

Verified direct source. A scanned copy of the published article was inspected. The JSTOR front matter and the article's own running heads confirm: *American Scientist*, December 1966, Vol. 54, No. 4, pp. 421–431, published by Sigma Xi, The Scientific Research Honor Society. JSTOR stable URL `https://www.jstor.org/stable/27836590`.

Pages 421–424 were read in full. Later pages were not inspected in this pass.

## Role in Chapter 2

This is Chapter 2's strongest single source. It is a working scientist arguing, in print, that a faithful one-to-one model would be useless, that which simplifications are legitimate depends on the question, and that building several deliberately different models is a method rather than an embarrassment.

- supports the claim that maximum detail is not the goal — with three distinct reasons;
- supplies a **worked historical case in which the same simplification is legitimate for one question and illegitimate for another**;
- supplies the generality/realism/precision trade-off and the three modelling strategies;
- supplies the map analogy for distinguishing representational features from artifacts;
- supplies a *reason* to build alternative representations: robustness of conclusions across them.

## Verified locators

- p. 421: the brute-force option and why it fails — "The naive, brute force approach would be to set up a mathematical model which is a faithful, one-to-one reflection of this complexity." It fails because "(a) there are too many parameters to measure; some are still only vaguely defined; many would require a lifetime each for their measurement. (b) The equations are insoluble analytically and exceed the capacity of even good computers. (c) Even if soluble, the result expressed in the form of quotients of sums of products of parameters would have no meaning for us."
- p. 421: "Clearly we have to simplify the models in a way that preserves the essential features of the problem."
- pp. 421–422: "The difference between legitimate and illegitimate simplifications depends not only on the reality to be described but also on the state of the science."
- p. 422, the purpose-relativity case: Haldane, Fisher and Wright assumed a constant environment though "each author was aware that environments are not constant. But the problem at hand was: Could weak natural selection account for evolutionary change? For the purposes of this problem, a selection coefficient that varies between .001 and .01 will have effects somewhere between constant selection pressures at those values, and would be an unnecessary complication."
- p. 422, the same omission under a different problem: "But, for us today, environmental heterogeneity is an essential ingredient of the problems and therefore of our mathematical models."
- p. 422, the trade-off: "It is of course desirable to work with manageable models which maximize generality, realism, and precision toward the overlapping but not identical goals of understanding, predicting, and modifying nature. But this cannot be done." Three strategies follow: (1) sacrifice generality to realism and precision; (2) sacrifice realism to generality and precision; (3) sacrifice precision to realism and generality.
- p. 423, the map analogy: doubt about whether a result depends on essentials or on simplifying details "does not arise in the more familiar models, such as the geographic map, where we all know that contiguity on the map implies contiguity in reality, relative distances on the map correspond to relative distances in reality, but color is arbitrary and a microscopic view of the map would only show the fibers of the paper on which it is printed. But, in the mathematical models of population biology, it is not always obvious when we are looking at too high a magnification."
- p. 423, robustness: "we attempt to treat the same problem with several alternative models each with different simplifications but with a common biological assumption. Then, if these models, despite their different assumptions, lead to similar results we have what we can call a robust theorem which is relatively free of the details of the model. Hence our truth is the intersection of independent lies."
- p. 424: Levins uses "fine-grained heterogeneity in space" and distinguishes "coarse- and fine-grained environments."

## Chapter 2 use and cautions

**The three goals are a gift and must be quoted accurately.** Levins names "the overlapping but not identical goals of understanding, predicting, and modifying nature." Chapter 2 may use this to show that purpose is not one thing. Do not paraphrase it into the book's own categories and then attribute the paraphrase to Levins.

**The `grain` usage at p. 424 is not model resolution.** Levins's coarse-/fine-grained distinction concerns environmental heterogeneity relative to an organism's experience. Chapter 2 uses `grain` for the resolution of a representation. These are different concepts that share a word — do not cite p. 424 for the Chapter 2 sense.

**"Our truth is the intersection of independent lies" is a claim about robust theorems, not a general epistemology.** It concerns conclusions that survive across models sharing a common assumption. Do not quote it as licence for building arbitrary alternative models.

**The trade-off claim is contested.** The subsequent literature (Orzack and Sober's critical assessment, and Odenbaugh's replies) disputes whether generality, realism and precision genuinely trade off. Chapter 2 should present the trade-off as Levins's influential *strategy argument*, not as a proven constraint. This caution was identified from the surrounding literature, which was **not** itself inspected — do not cite those critics without reading them.

**Domain.** The paper is about population biology. Its use in a cross-domain book is the book's own extension.

Chapter 2 does not import fitness sets, niche theory, or the mathematics of pp. 423–431.
