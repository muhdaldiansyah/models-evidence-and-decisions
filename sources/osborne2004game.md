## Role in Chapter 15

This is the book's source for `strategic dependence`, `strategic game`, and **`equilibrium as consistency`** — the strategic sense of `equilibrium`, which `canon/terminology.md` has reserved for Chapter 15 since Chapter 1 with the instruction that the two senses must not be conflated.

## Verified locators

- p. 11: "A STRATEGIC GAME is a model of interacting decision-makers. In recognition of the interaction, we refer to the decision-makers as players."
- p. 11, **strategic dependence in one clause**: "The model captures interaction between the players by allowing each player to be affected by the actions of all players, not only her own action."
- p. 11, Definition 11.1: "A strategic game (with ordinal preferences) consists of • a set of players • for each player, a set of actions • for each player, preferences over the set of action profiles."
- p. 19: "In a game, the best action for any given player depends, in general, on the other players' actions. So when choosing an action a player must have in mind the actions the other players will choose. That is, she must form a belief about the other players' actions."
- p. 19: "The assumption underlying the analysis in this chapter and the next two chapters is that each player's belief is derived from her past experience playing the game, and that this experience is sufficiently extensive that she knows how her opponents will behave."
- p. 20, **the two components**: "First, each player chooses her action according to the model of rational choice, given her belief about the other players' actions. Second, every player's belief about the other players' actions is correct."
- p. 20, the definition of Nash equilibrium — **not quoted**; it carries an asterisked profile and player subscripts. **The declared paraphrase**: an outcome in which no player could do better by changing their own action alone, given what everybody else is doing.
- p. 20: "a Nash equilibrium corresponds to a steady state... there is no pressure on the action profile to change."
- p. 20: "Expressed differently, a Nash equilibrium embodies a stable 'social norm': if everyone else adheres to it, no individual wishes to deviate from it."
- p. 20: "For this reason, the condition is sometimes said to be that the players' 'expectations are coordinated'."
- p. 20, **the source's own caution**: "The situations to which we wish to apply the theory of Nash equilibrium do not in general correspond exactly to the idealized setting described above... Whether or not the notion of Nash equilibrium is appropriate in any given situation is a matter of judgment." And: "Ultimately, the test of the appropriateness of the notion of Nash equilibrium is whether it gives us insights into the problem at hand."
- p. 19, Figure 19.1: a two-player payoff table displayed as a two-by-two grid of paired numbers. Used as the convention for the chapter's own table; see `../decisions/0022` clause 5.

## Cautions

- **A pre-publication draft**, not the published book.
- **Read at three pages of forty-two.** Nothing is claimed about game theory beyond the strategic form, strategic dependence, and the definition and interpretation of Nash equilibrium.
- **The definition of Nash equilibrium is paraphrased and not quoted**, under the standing rule from Chapter 8 — it carries symbols that do not survive being lifted out of the page.
- **The named example games at §§2.2–2.5 are not taught.** The Prisoner's Dilemma, Bach or Stravinsky, Matching Pennies, and the Stag Hunt were read only far enough to see the table convention; the book uses its own case.
- **§2.7 onward unread**: examples of equilibrium, best response functions, dominated actions, symmetric equilibria. No mixed strategies, no extensive form, no repeated games, no bargaining.
- **The source's caution at p. 20 must travel with the concept.** Whether Nash equilibrium applies is a matter of judgment, on the source's own statement, and a chapter that presented it as a general fact about interacting parties would be misusing it.
- **`equilibrium` collides with Chapter 13's dynamic sense**, which `canon/terminology.md` already flags. The strategic sense is a consistency condition on beliefs and actions; the dynamic sense is a stationary condition on dynamics. A pendulum has the second and has no beliefs.
