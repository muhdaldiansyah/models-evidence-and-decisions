# Source note: `asa2016pvalue`

## Bibliographic record

American Statistical Association. "American Statistical Association Releases Statement on Statistical Significance and P-Values." Press release, 7 March 2016.

## What this document is, stated precisely

**This is the ASA's press release, not the statement article.**

The statement itself was published as Wasserstein, Ronald L., and Nicole A. Lazar, "The ASA's Statement on p-Values: Context, Process, and Purpose," *The American Statistician* 70(2): 129–133. **That article could not be obtained.**

The release is a primary document issued by the ASA and it prints the six principles in full. It is cited here for the six principles and for two attributed quotations, and for nothing else.

The release itself notes that "The statement has short paragraphs elaborating on each principle." **Those paragraphs are in the article and were not read.** Nothing in this book characterises them.

This is the same handling given to Brier (1950) in Chapter 6 and to the published Deaton and Cartwright in Chapter 7: cite what was read, name what was not.

## Verification status

Read directly, in full, as a four-page PDF obtained from the ASA's own site.

## Verified content

The six principles, printed in the release as a numbered list:

1. "P-values can indicate how incompatible the data are with a specified statistical model."
2. "P-values do not measure the probability that the studied hypothesis is true, or the probability that the data were produced by random chance alone."
3. "Scientific conclusions and business or policy decisions should not be based only on whether a p-value passes a specific threshold."
4. "Proper inference requires full reporting and transparency."
5. "A p-value, or statistical significance, does not measure the size of an effect or the importance of a result."
6. "By itself, a p-value does not provide a good measure of evidence regarding a model or hypothesis."

Attributed quotations printed in the release:

- Ron Wasserstein, ASA executive director: "The p-value was never intended to be a substitute for scientific reasoning… Well-reasoned statistical arguments contain much more than the value of a single number and whether that number exceeds an arbitrary threshold." He is also quoted saying the statement "is intended to steer research into a 'post p<0.05 era.'"
- Jessica Utts, ASA president: "Over time it appears the p-value has become a gatekeeper for whether work is publishable, at least in some fields… This apparent editorial bias leads to the 'file-drawer effect,' in which research with statistically significant outcomes are much more likely to get published, while other work that might well be just as important scientifically is never seen in print."

The release also records that statisticians "often supplement or even replace p-values with other approaches", listing methods "that emphasize estimation over testing such as confidence, credibility, or prediction intervals; Bayesian methods; alternative measures of evidence such as likelihood ratios or Bayes factors; and other approaches such as decision-theoretic modeling and false discovery rates."

## Role in Chapter 8

- supplies the professional body's own position, which a reader cannot dismiss as one methodologist's view;
- principle 4 supplies, from the discipline's institution, the same claim `greenland2016misinterpretations` p. 339 makes from a methods paper: that how the analysis was conducted is part of what makes inference proper;
- the Utts quotation supplies the mechanism by which the ritual is sustained, which is institutional rather than intellectual.

## Cautions

**Never cite this as Wasserstein and Lazar (2016).** It is not that article. If the article is later obtained, both this note and every locator built on it must be revisited.

**Do not attribute the elaborating paragraphs.** They were not read.

**Do not present the listed alternatives as the ASA's recommendations.** The release describes what statisticians do, not what the ASA endorses, and the chapter must not upgrade that.
