## Role in Chapter 2

- supplies the post-2000 consensus ("minimal mechanism") characterization, which is broader across the sciences than `machamer2000mechanisms`;
- supplies **phenomenon-relativity**: a mechanism is always a mechanism *of* something, and its boundaries are set by the phenomenon;
- supports the boundary between describing a mechanism and establishing a causal claim;
- supplies the how-possibly / how-actually contrast, which lets Chapter 2 name the difference between a drawable mechanism and an established one without teaching causal identification.

## Verified locators

- §2 *(verbatim)*: "A mechanism for a phenomenon consists of entities (or parts) whose activities and interactions are organized so as to be responsible for the phenomenon." The entry attributes this formulation to Glennan (2017) and Glennan & Illari (2017a).
- §2 *(paraphrase)*: this "minimal mechanism" formulation deliberately omits the regularity requirement, so as to accommodate one-off mechanisms, and drops function language so that mechanisms serving no end still qualify.
- §2.1.1 *(verbatim)*: "All mechanisms are mechanisms *of* some phenomenon, a consequential truism first articulated by Kauffman (1971 [1976])."
- §5.1 *(verbatim)*: "mechanisms are defined only relative to the phenomenon they cause, underlie, or otherwise explain."
- §5.1 *(paraphrase)*: mechanism boundaries are fixed by relevance to the phenomenon, so different phenomena yield different mechanistic decompositions of the same system.
- §2.1.3 *(paraphrase)*: mechanists unpack causation within mechanisms in several competing ways — conserved-quantity transmission, activity-based accounts, counterfactual approaches, and Glennan's account on which non-fundamental causation derives from lower-level mechanisms. Depicting mechanism components does not by itself settle the causal claim.
- §3.3 *(paraphrase)*: a how-possibly model describes an organization that *could* produce the phenomenon; a how-actually model describes one that *actually* produces it; mechanists ask whether a model is "how-actually-enough" for its purpose.
- §3.5.1 *(paraphrase, quoting Darden)*: "Mechanism schemas are abstract descriptions of mechanisms that can be filled in with details to yield a specific type or token mechanism"; sketches remain incomplete and use placeholders.
- §4 *(paraphrase)*: discovery strategies discussed in the literature derive largely from biological exemplars, and their relation to formal computational causal-discovery methods remains largely disconnected.

## Chapter 2 use and cautions

Chapter 2 takes two things from this entry: the cross-science minimal characterization, and phenomenon-relativity.

**Phenomenon-relativity is the load-bearing item.** It gives Chapter 2 a sourced reason for its central claim that a representation's boundary follows from the question rather than from the world. Chapter 2 generalises this from "phenomenon" to "stated purpose"; that generalisation is the **book's own pedagogical move** and must not be attributed to this entry.

Do not cite this entry as resolving how mechanisms relate to causation. §2.1.3 records live disagreement; the entry maps positions rather than settling them.

Do not use the minimal-mechanism formulation to imply that any diagram of parts and arrows is a mechanism. The formulation requires that the organization be *responsible for* the phenomenon — which is exactly what a Chapter 2 reader has not yet shown.

Formal causal identification remains Chapter 7. Chapter 2 may say a mechanism is *proposed*; it may not say it is *established*.
