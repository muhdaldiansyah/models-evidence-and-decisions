## Role in Chapter 2

- supports the model/target-system distinction, including the point that a model has properties its description does not;
- supplies established vocabulary for the *kinds* of idealization, and an independent statement of the abstraction-as-silence idea;
- supports the claim that several mutually incompatible models of one target system can be used successfully.

## Verified locators

- §1 (Semantics: Models and Representation): "Many scientific models are representational models: they represent a selected part or aspect of the world, which is the model's target system."
- §2.4: models are distinguished from their descriptions — "the Newtonian model of the solar system consists of orbiting spheres, but it makes no sense to say this about its description."
- §1: Aristotelian idealization is characterised as "stripping away, in our imagination, all properties from a concrete object that we believe are not relevant to the problem"; Galilean idealization "involve[s] deliberate distortions: physicists build models consisting of point masses moving on frictionless planes."
- §1, on abstraction: "Jones (2005) and Godfrey-Smith (2009) offer an analysis of abstraction in terms of truth: while an abstraction remains silent about certain features … it does not say anything false."
- §4.2 (Models as Independent from Theories): models are "autonomous agents" independent of both theories and target systems, and this independence is what allows them to mediate.
- §5.1: the "incompatible-models argument" records that "scientists often successfully use several incompatible models of *one and the same* target system for predictive purposes."

## Chapter 2 use and cautions

Do not cite this entry for purpose-relativity or boundary choice; see the negative findings above.

The abstraction-as-silence point is here attributed by SEP to Jones (2005) and Godfrey-Smith (2009). It converges with the same attribution to Jones recorded independently in `weisberg2007idealization`. Two sources reporting the same third author is convergent testimony about the distinction's provenance — it is not two independent defences of it.

Chapter 2 does not import the entry's debates on ontology of models, models as fictions, or the semantic view of theories.
