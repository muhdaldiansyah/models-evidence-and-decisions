# Source note: `machamer2000mechanisms`

## Citation

Machamer, Peter, Lindley Darden, and Carl F. Craver. 2000. "Thinking about Mechanisms." *Philosophy of Science* 67 (1): 1–25.

## Verification status

Verified direct source. A scanned copy of the published article was inspected page by page. Journal title, volume, issue, month, year, page range, ISSN `0031-8248`, and the University of Chicago Press / Philosophy of Science Association imprint are printed on the article's first page.

Received November 1998; revised July 1999.

## Role in Chapter 2

- supplies the canonical entities-and-activities characterization of `mechanism`;
- supports representing a mechanism as organized parts whose activities produce a phenomenon, without yet claiming a causal effect has been identified;
- supplies the schema/sketch distinction, which gives Chapter 2 a principled way to say that a drawn mechanism may be a hypothesis with unfilled gaps;
- supplies an explicit separation of **abstraction** (amount of detail) from **generality/scope** (size of the domain of instantiation);
- supports the claim that intervention, not the diagram alone, is what supplies evidence for a hypothesized mechanism.

## Verified locators

- Abstract, printed p. 1: "The concept of mechanism is analyzed in terms of entities and activities, organized such that they are productive of regular changes."
- §1, printed p. 1: "In many fields of science what is taken to be a satisfactory explanation requires providing a description of a mechanism."
- §1, printed p. 2, caveats: "one should not think of mechanisms as exclusively mechanical (push-pull) systems"; the authors "confine our attention to mechanisms in molecular biology and neurobiology" and state "We do not claim that all scientists look for mechanisms or that all explanations are descriptions of mechanisms." Applicability to cognitive or social mechanisms is left "as an open question."
- §2, printed p. 3, the definition: "Mechanisms are entities and activities organized such that they are productive of regular changes from start or set-up to finish or termination conditions."
- §2, printed p. 3: "Activities are the producers of change. Entities are the things that engage in activities."
- §2, printed p. 3: "The organization of these entities and activities determines the ways in which they produce the phenomenon."
- §2, printed p. 3: "Mechanisms are regular in that they work always or for the most part in the same way under the same conditions."
- §2, printed p. 3: "A missing arrow, namely, the inability to specify an activity, leaves an explanatory gap in the productive continuity of the mechanism."
- §5.3, printed p. 15: "mechanism schema" is introduced "for an abstract description of a type of mechanism"; "A mechanism schema is a truncated abstract description of a mechanism that can be filled with descriptions of known component parts and activities."
- §5.3, printed p. 16: "Abstractions may be constructed by taking an exemplary case or instance and removing detail. For example, a constant can be made into a variable."
- §5.3, printed p. 16: "Degrees of abstraction should not be confused with degrees of generality or scope. Abstraction is an issue of the amount of detail included in the description of one or more mechanism instances. The generality of a schema is the scope (small or large) of the domain in which it can be instantiated."
- §5.3, printed p. 17: "While the mechanism is operating, the experimenter may intervene to alter some part of the mechanism and observe the changes in a termination condition or what the mechanism does. Changes produced by such interventions can provide evidence for the hypothesized schema."
- §5.3, printed p. 17: when a prediction from a hypothesized mechanism fails, the responses include diagnosing where the schema fails and redesigning entities, activities, or stages.
- §5.3, printed p. 18: "A sketch is an abstraction for which bottom out entities and activities cannot (yet) be supplied or which contains gaps in its stages. The productive continuity from one stage to the next has missing pieces, black boxes, which we do not yet know how to fill in. A sketch thus serves to indicate what further work needs to be done in order to have a mechanism schema."

## Chapter 2 use and cautions

Chapter 2 uses the entities/activities/organization structure and the schema–sketch distinction at introductory depth. It does not adopt the paper's wider programme on laws, reduction, or scientific change.

**Do not present this source as licensing causal conclusions.** The paper is an account of what mechanisms *are* and how they are described, not a method for establishing that a described mechanism is the actual one. Its own p. 17 remark attributes evidential force to intervention.

**Do not overstate scope.** The authors explicitly restrict their attention to molecular biology and neurobiology and leave social and cognitive application open. Where Chapter 2 uses mechanism language outside biology, that extension is the book's own cautious usage and must not be attributed to this source.

**Do not treat `regular` as a requirement Chapter 2 imposes.** Later consensus formulations drop the regularity condition; see `craver2026mechanisms`.
