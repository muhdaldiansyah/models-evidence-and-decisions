# Source note: `adcock2001validity`

## Citation

Adcock, Robert, and David Collier. 2001. "Measurement Validity: A Shared Standard for Qualitative and Quantitative Research." *American Political Science Review* 95 (3): 529–546.

## Verification status

Verified direct source. The published article was inspected page by page. The journal running heads print *American Political Science Review*, Vol. 95, No. 3, September 2001, and the article begins on printed p. 529. Authors are given as of the University of California, Berkeley.

Printed pages 529–532 were read in full. Later pages were not inspected in this pass.

## Role in Chapter 3

This is Chapter 3's spine source. It supplies the operationalization ladder, a definition of measurement validity that locates validity in the *interpretation of scores* rather than in an instrument, and the contextual-specificity point that mirrors the purpose-relativity taught in Chapters 1 and 2.

It is also deliberately cross-disciplinary — its stated aim is a standard shared by qualitative and quantitative research — which suits a book that must not become a psychometrics text.

## Verified locators

- p. 529: "Measurement validity is specifically concerned with whether operationalization and the scoring of cases adequately reflect the concept the researcher seeks to measure."
- p. 529: measurement validity is distinguished from the validity of causal inference, and from Cook and Campbell's internal and external validity — "Although measurement validity is interconnected with causal inference, it stands as an important methodological topic in its own right."
- p. 530, Figure 1 and "Levels and Tasks", the four levels:
  - **Level 1, background concept** — "the constellation of potentially diverse meanings associated with a given concept";
  - **Level 2, systematized concept** — "the specific formulation of a concept adopted by a particular researcher or group of researchers. It is usually formulated in terms of an explicit definition";
  - **Level 3, indicators** — "also routinely called measures", including "any systematic scoring procedure, ranging from simple measures to complex aggregated indexes", and encompassing "the classification procedures employed in qualitative research";
  - **Level 4, scores for cases** — "the scores for cases generated by a particular indicator", including "both numerical scores and the results of qualitative classification".
- p. 530, the tasks: conceptualization moves from background to systematized concept; **operationalization** moves from the systematized concept to indicators; **scoring cases** applies indicators to produce scores. Upward tasks are refining indicators, modifying the systematized concept, and revisiting the background concept.
- p. 530: "'measurement' involves the interaction among levels 2 to 4."
- p. 530, "Defining Measurement Validity": "Valid measurement is achieved when scores (including the results of qualitative classification) meaningfully capture the ideas contained in the corresponding concept."
- p. 530: contextual specificity is introduced as "an issue that arises when a measure that is valid in one context is invalid in another."
- p. 530: the terminological problem — the authors "found 37 different adjectives" attached to "validity"; they differentiate procedures as "different *types of evidence for validity*" rather than "multiple independent *types of validity*", and they distinguish "validity" (the overall idea) from "validation" (the assessment procedures).
- p. 531: "Scores are never examined in isolation; rather, they are interpreted and given meaning in relation to the systematized concept."
- p. 531: "measurement is valid when the scores (level 4 …), derived from a given indicator (level 3), can meaningfully be interpreted in terms of the systematized concept (level 2) that the indicator seeks to operationalize"; "the appropriate focus of measurement validation is on the conjunction of these components."
- p. 531, "Measurement Error, Reliability, and Validity": "Measurement error may be systematic—in which case it is called bias—or random. Random error, which occurs when repeated applications of a given measurement procedure yield inconsistent results, is conventionally labeled a problem of reliability."
- p. 532: the two accounts of the reliability/validity relation — on one, "unreliable scores may still be correct 'on average' and in this sense valid"; on the other, scholars "view reliability as a necessary but not sufficient condition of measurement validity."
- p. 532: the authors' own focus is "the systematic error that arises when the links among systematized concepts, indicators, and scores are poorly developed."
- p. 532: "Because error is a pervasive threat to measurement, it is essential to view the interpretations of scores in relation to systematized concepts as falsifiable claims (Messick 1989, 13–4). Scholars should treat these claims just as they would any casual hypothesis, that is, as tentative statements that require supporting evidence. Validity assessment is the search for this evidence."
- p. 532: Kaplan's paradox of conceptualization — "Proper concepts are needed to formulate a good theory, but we need a good theory to arrive at the proper concepts. … The paradox is resolved by a process of approximation."
- p. 532: three traps in forming a systematized concept — misconstruing flexibility as everything being up for grabs; claiming too much for one's chosen systematization; and stopping short of a fleshed-out account of it.

## Chapter 3 use and cautions

**Do not present the four levels as a procedure to be executed once.** The article's own figure shows upward tasks — refining indicators, modifying the systematized concept, revisiting the background concept — and Kaplan's paradox is resolved "by a process of approximation."

**The falsifiable-claims point at p. 532 is attributed by the authors to Messick (1989), which was not read in this pass.** Attribute it as reported unless Messick is obtained directly.

**Do not import the article's political-science examples.** Democracy, ethnic conflict, and the contested-concepts literature are the article's illustrations, not this book's.

**Do not extend "37 adjectives" into a claim that validity terminology is meaningless.** The authors' point is that the proliferation is confusing and that the procedures supply *types of evidence*, not separate validities.

**Domain.** The article is written for political science and argues explicitly for a standard shared with qualitative research. This book's use across engineering and physical measurement is the book's own extension, and where Chapter 3 pairs this source with `jcgm2012vim` it must not imply that metrology and social-science measurement use one vocabulary.
