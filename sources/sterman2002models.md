# Source note: `sterman2002models`

## Citation

Sterman, John D. 2002. "All Models Are Wrong: Reflections on Becoming a Systems Scientist." *System Dynamics Review* 18 (4): 501–531. DOI 10.1002/sdr.261.

## Verification status

Verified direct source. The published article was inspected page by page. Its first page prints *System Dynamics Review* Vol. 18, No. 4, (Winter 2002): 501–531, the DOI, and "Jay Wright Forrester Prize Lecture, 2002". Received and accepted October 2002. Author affiliation: MIT Sloan School of Management.

Printed pages 501–507 were read in full. Later pages were **not** inspected in this pass.

**Not the same source as `sterman2006evidence`.** That is a 2006 *American Journal of Public Health* article. Both are in the bibliography and must not be conflated.

### A verification note worth recording

An automated fetch of this PDF returned four confident-looking quotations with page numbers — including "The choice of model boundary—what to include and exclude—is perhaps the most critical modeling decision" attributed to p. 503.

**None of those quotations appears in this article.** Printed p. 503 is about what system dynamics is as a field. The quotations and their page numbers were fabricated.

They were caught only because the PDF was then read directly. Every locator below comes from that direct reading.

## Role in Chapter 2

This source materially strengthens Chapter 2's boundary section. It supplies a memorable, quotable principle connecting boundaries to what modellers call "side effects", and it names the endogenous view.

**It does not close the recorded gap.** Chapter 2's gap is the absence of a sourced *general procedure* for boundary selection, and this article offers a principle and an attitude rather than criteria. `decisions/0009` clause 2.3 stands.

## Verified locators

- p. 504, defining policy resistance: "policy resistance, the tendency for interventions to be defeated by the response of the system to the intervention itself."
- p. 504: "At the root of this phenomenon lies the narrow, event-oriented, reductionist worldview most people live by. We have been trained to see the world as a series of events, to view our situation as the result of forces outside ourselves, forces largely unpredictable and uncontrollable."
- p. 505, the boundary claim: "There are no side effects—only effects. Those we thought of in advance, the ones we like, we call the main, or intended, effects, and take credit for them. The ones we didn't anticipate, the ones that came around and bit us in the rear—those are the 'side effects'."
- p. 505, continuing: "When we point to outside shocks and side effects to excuse the failure of our policies, we think we are describing a capricious and unpredictable reality. In fact, we are highlighting the limitations of our mental models. System dynamics helps us expand the boundaries of our mental models so that we become aware of and take responsibility for the feedbacks created by our decisions."
- p. 505, section heading and claim: "**(Almost) nothing is exogenous**" — "It is hard to underestimate the power of the feedback view. Indeed, almost nothing is exogenous."
- p. 506: "Failure to recognize the feedbacks in which we are embedded, the way in which we shape the situation in which we find ourselves, leads to policy resistance as we persistently react to the symptoms of difficulty, intervening at low leverage points and triggering delayed and distant, but powerful feedbacks."

## Chapter 2 use and cautions

**The "no side effects" passage is about boundaries, and Chapter 2 may use it that way.** A side effect is an effect that fell outside the boundary someone drew. That is a genuine and useful reframing, and it is the author's own point.

**Do not teach policy resistance.** `README.md` assigns policy resistance to Chapter 13's core competence, and this article's p. 504 definition is the natural place a reader would learn it. Chapter 2 may not.

**Do not teach feedback, stocks and flows, or delays** from this source. Its pp. 506–507 are about stock-and-flow comprehension and belong to Chapter 13.

**"(Almost) nothing is exogenous" is a system-dynamics position, not a neutral fact.** It is a strong claim from one modelling tradition, and Chapter 2 — which teaches that boundaries are purpose-relative — should present it as an argument for widening boundaries rather than as a rule that everything must be endogenous. Treating it as the latter would contradict Chapter 2's own thesis that some exclusions are correct.

**This is a prize lecture, not a research paper.** Its claims are asserted from experience rather than demonstrated, and the manuscript should not cite it as though it reported a result.

Do not cite beyond p. 507; later pages were not read.
