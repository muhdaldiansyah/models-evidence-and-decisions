## Role in Chapter 15

This source does two jobs.

**It supplies the four mechanisms** by which a metric and the goal behind it come apart, which is what lets Chapter 15 teach metric failure as four distinguishable things rather than one word.

**And it carries two laws the book could not obtain.** `README.md`'s Chapter 15 core competence names Goodhart-type failures, Campbell's law, and the Lucas critique; none of the three originals was obtainable, and this source quotes the first two verbatim with full references. They are used **as reported at** this source, the device the book has used since Chapter 6.

## Verified locators

- p. 1, **Goodhart's law as reported at**: "any observed statistical regularity will tend to collapse once pressure is placed upon it for control purposes." Footnote 1, with the reference given in the source's bibliography as Charles E. Goodhart, *Problems of Monetary Management: The U.K. Experience*, 1975, Papers in Monetary Economics, Reserve Bank of Australia.
- p. 1, footnote 1, naming the others: Campbell's law "(which arguably has scholarly precedence)" and the Lucas critique "were also initially specific, and their interpretation has also been expanded greatly."
- p. 1, footnote 1, **the source's own caveat**: "Because none of the terms were laid out formally, the categories proposed do not match what was originally discussed."
- p. 1, the definition: "As used in this paper, a Goodhart effect is when optimization causes a collapse of the statistical relationship between a goal which the optimizer intends and the proxy used for that goal."
- p. 1: "The importance of Goodhart effects depends on the amount of power directed towards optimizing the proxy"
- p. 2, **the four mechanisms**: "1) Regressional, where selection for an imperfect proxy necessarily also selects for noise, 2) Extremal, where selection for the metric pushes the state distribution into a region where old relationships no longer hold, 3) Causal, where an action on the part of the regulator causes the collapse, and 4) Adversarial, where an agent with different goals than the regulator causes the collapse."
- p. 2: "These varied forms often occur together, but defining them individually is useful."
- p. 2, on Regressional: "No matter what measure is chosen for optimization, an inexact metric necessarily leads to a divergence between the goal and the metric in the tail." The source calls it "the most fundamental: it cannot be avoided."
- p. 8: "Adversarial Misalignment Goodhart - The agent applies selection pressure knowing the regulator will apply different selection pressure on the basis of the metric"
- p. 8: "Campbell's Law - Agents select a metric knowing the choice of regulator metric. Agents can correlate their metric with the regulator's metric, and select on their metric. This further reduces the usefulness of selection using the metric for acheiving the original goal." *The spelling of "acheiving" is the source's and is reproduced as it stands.*
- p. 8, footnote 5, **Campbell's law as reported at**: "The more any quantitative social indicator is used for social decision-making, the more subject it will be to corruption pressures and the more apt it will be to distort and corrupt the social processes it is intended to monitor." Reference given as Donald T. Campbell, "Assessing the impact of planned social change", *Evaluation and Program Planning* 2(1): 67–90.
- p. 8, the Cobra Effect named, with the source's own hedge that it is "a supposed situation".

## Cautions

- **Preprint, not peer-reviewed.**
- **Written for artificial-intelligence alignment**, which is not this book's subject. **The taxonomy transfers; the motivation does not**, and the manuscript does not import it.
- **The taxonomy originates informally.** The source's reference [2] is a blog post and reference [5] is two blog posts, which the source states openly. The book uses this paper as the citable, structured statement of an idea that began informally, and says so.
- **The source's own caveat is carried into the manuscript**: the four categories do not match the original Goodhart, Campbell, and Lucas formulations.
- **The Lucas critique is named and not quoted here**, so the book names it and states its content in its own words, saying it has not read Lucas (1976). Nothing is attributed to Lucas beyond the existence of the critique.
- **The "Simple Model" equations in each section are not quoted and not taught.** Several carry comparison symbols and set membership.
- Do not use this source for claims about artificial intelligence, about regulation in general, or about the Cobra Effect as history.
